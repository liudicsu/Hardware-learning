## Reading a schematic: connections, not physical layout

A schematic describes who connects to whom, not where parts sit on the physical board. On a real PCB parts are packed tight and traces wander across layers, but the schematic only cares about one thing: the electrical connection. So don't read it as a spatial map ("input on the left, output on the right"); read it as a list of "which pin goes to which".

Learning the symbols is step one. You will meet these over and over:

| Symbol name | What it does | How it's labelled |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resistor | Limits current, divides voltage | R1, R2…; drawn as a zigzag or a box |
| Capacitor | Stores charge, smooths voltage | C1, C2…; a polarised electrolytic carries a + mark |
| Diode | Lets current flow one way | D1, D2…; the bar side is the cathode |
| LED | Emits light when current flows | Labelled D or LED, with two arrows pointing away |
| NPN transistor | A small current controls a big current | Q1…; the emitter's arrow points **out** of the symbol |
| Switch / button | Opens or closes a circuit | SW or S |
| Ground | The 0 V reference for everything | GND |
| Supply labels | Name the power nets | VCC, VDD, 5V, 3V3, VBAT |

Three reading rules; keep them handy and you avoid most misreads:

- **Junction dot**: a filled dot where wires cross means those wires are actually connected.
- **Crossing without a dot = not connected**: the wires merely cross; electrically they may live on different layers.
- **Same net label = connected**: two places marked `3V3` or `SDA` are one net even if no wire is drawn between them; big schematics stay tidy thanks to this.

Bench tip: don't try to swallow the whole sheet at once. Pick one net (say GND or a signal), trace every pin on it, understand that one fully, then move to the next. Reading schematics is like reading a map — you go one road at a time.

## Capacitors: storing charge, resisting sudden change

A capacitor is two metal plates placed very close together; charge "sits" between them. Two behaviours are enough to carry with you:

- It **resists sudden voltage change**: to jump the voltage across a capacitor instantly you would need an infinite current. More current charges it faster, but "instant" is impossible.
- Hence the classic motto: **block DC, pass changes**. A steady DC eventually cannot get through, while fast changes pass. It sounds abstract until you run it once in the simulator.

How fast it charges or discharges is set by the time constant (τ), the product of the resistance and the capacitance:

<Formula label="RC time constant">τ = R × C</Formula>

Rules of thumb (charging from zero):

- After **1 τ** the capacitor reaches **≈ 63 %** of its target voltage;
- After **5 τ** it reaches **≈ 99 %** — engineering-wise, "charged".

**Worked example**: the embedded Falstad RC example uses R = 180 Ω and C = 33 µF.

```text
τ = 180 Ω × 33 µF = 5940 µs ≈ 5.9 ms
```

So roughly 5.9 ms after charging starts it is at 63 %, and about 5 × 5.9 ≈ 29.5 ms gets it to 99 %.

<Falstad title="RC charge/discharge (official Falstad example)" circuit="capac" height={420} />

How to play with it:

1. A square-wave source repeatedly charges and discharges the capacitor. Watch the scope trace: the voltage climbs and decays.
2. Compare against the numbers above: each climb passes 63 % around 1 τ and 99 % around 5 τ — the curve is steep first, then flattens, forever "almost there".
3. **Predict, then act**: double-click the capacitor and make it 10× bigger (330 µF). Write your prediction first, then run: the time constant grows 10× and charging visibly slows. A whole ×10 is chosen so the change is obvious, not a subtle shift.

<Callout type="warn" title="Electrolytics have polarity!">
The aluminium electrolytic (the metal can) is **polarised**: a stripe or − mark on the shell marks the negative lead. Reversed, it heats and vents at best; at worst it **swells and pops** — the pre-scored cross on top is the vent. And never exceed its voltage rating: a 6.3 V cap on 5 V is fine, on 12 V is self-destruction. Check polarity and rating before applying power.
</Callout>

## Decoupling capacitors: a tiny water tower for every chip

Inside a digital IC, gates switch extremely fast, and every switch redraws a sudden gulp of current from the supply (a current spike). If that gulp must travel from a distant power source, supply-trace resistance and inductance dip the voltage right at the chip's pins — supply noise lands straight on the die. Symptoms range from glitchy behaviour to resets.

The fix is almost mechanical: **give every IC a 100 nF ceramic capacitor (decoupling capacitor) directly across its supply pin (VCC) and GND, as physically close to the chip as possible**. It is the little water tower beside the chip: it stores charge locally and supplies a spike on the spot, so the rail doesn't sag. Hobby boards and volume production alike are wired this way.

- **100 nF ceramic**: one per chip, hugging its supply pins. If the board has multiple supply domains (say 3.3 V and 5 V coexisting), each domain needs its own.
- **Bulk electrolytic (100–470 µF)**: placed near motor drivers and other loads that gulp big currents, acting as the board-wide reservoir.

A phrase worth knowing: 100 nF is often quoted as “0.1 µF” — when a veteran says “just add a 0.1”, that is what they mean.

## Diodes and LEDs: a one-way valve and a lamp that must be current-limited

A diode is a one-way valve: current flows from the anode to the cathode, the bar in the symbol is the cathode. Before current flows, it charges a toll — the forward voltage (Vf):

- Ordinary silicon diode: **≈ 0.6–0.7 V**;
- An LED's Vf depends on colour: **red ≈ 1.8–2.2 V**, **green/blue/white ≈ 2.8–3.3 V**.

An LED is a current-driven device: push its voltage just a little above Vf and the current shoots up steeply — a careless few millivolts can kill it. That is why **an LED must always have a series current-limiting resistor**, the number-one cause of beginner LED deaths. The resistor follows straight from Ohm's law:

<Formula label="LED current-limiting resistor">R = (Vsupply − Vf) / I</Formula>

where Vsupply is the supply voltage, Vf the LED's forward voltage, I the target current.

**Worked example**: 5 V supply, red LED (Vf = 2 V), target 10 mA.

```text
R = (5 V − 2 V) / 10 mA = 3 V / 0.01 A = 300 Ω
```

300 Ω is not an E12 standard value (…270, 330, 390…), so pick **up** to the nearest: **330 Ω**. Err large, never small: a bigger resistor only dims the LED; a smaller one over-currents it and shortens its life until it dies.

<Falstad title="LED current limiting: 5 V, 330 Ω, red LED" circuit="ledLimit" height={380} />

The ammeter in series reads about 9.8 mA. Falstad's default LED model drops about 1.78 V rather than the 2 V used in the worked example, so the current is a little higher than the 9.1 mA you get from (5 − 2) / 330. That difference is normal — real LEDs vary too, which is why we design with margin. Try it: double-click the resistor, change 330 Ω to 100 Ω, predict the new current before looking, and notice how quickly the current rises.

<Quiz id="q3" question="A 3.3 V supply, a red LED with Vf = 2.0 V, target 5 mA: what resistor, in Ω?" answer={260} unit="Ω" tolerance={0.05} explanation="R = (3.3 V − 2.0 V) / 5 mA = 1.3 V / 0.005 A = 260 Ω. Up to the next E12 value you'd pick 270 Ω." />

One more "invisible but lifesaving" diode job: the **flyback diode**. Motors and relays are inductive loads; the instant you switch them off, the inductor — which refuses to change its current abruptly — throws a high-voltage spike (tens of volts are common) that can punch through the driving transistor. Wire a diode anti-parallel across the load and it shorts the spike away. Month 2 puts this to work on motors; file the concept now.

## The transistor as a switch: small current steers big current

Why a transistor? A microcontroller GPIO pin can **safely source only about 10–20 mA** (exact figure: read the datasheet), and its logic level is often just 3.3 V (ESP32 and most modern chips; the classic Arduino Uno is 5 V). Motors, LED strips and relays want hundreds of milliamps or more; wired straight to a pin, something burns. The idea: **the GPIO only makes the decision with a tiny current; the transistor does the heavy switching**.

The most common arrangement is the NPN low-side switch: the load sits between the supply and the collector, the emitter goes to ground, and the GPIO feeds a small current into the base through a base resistor. Transistor on, load circuit complete.

Sizing the base resistor: say target collector current Ic = 20 mA (lighting a small LED strip), and your NPN's current gain β (hFE) ≈ 100.

```text
Minimum base current: Ib(min) = Ic / β = 20 mA / 100 = 0.2 mA
```

But a switch must be **fully on** (saturated) — half-on wastes heat. Practice is to overdrive the base 5–10×; taking ×10:

```text
Ib ≈ 10 × 0.2 mA = 2 mA
Rb = (Vgpio − Vbe) / Ib = (3.3 V − 0.7 V) / 2 mA ≈ 1.3 kΩ
```

1.3 kΩ isn't standard either, so go **down** to 1 kΩ: a bit more base current, deeper saturation, safer margin. Direction matters: when sizing the base resistor, err small (more saturation), not large.

<Falstad title="Transistor switch driving an LED (practice circuit)" circuit="transistorLed" height={420} />

Circuit: on the left a 3.3 V source through a switch (playing the role of a GPIO output HIGH) and a 1 kΩ base resistor drive the NPN; the collector side is 5 V → 220 Ω → LED. Steps:

1. **Click the switch** — the GPIO "goes HIGH" — and watch the LED light up.
2. Note the currents the sim reports: the **base branch carries only about 2–3 mA while the collector branch carries well over 10 mA** — a small current steering a large one.
3. **Break it**: double-click the 1 kΩ base resistor and set it to 100 kΩ. The LED dims or nearly dies. Why? Now Ib = (3.3 − 0.7) / 100 000 Ω ≈ 26 µA, so even with β = 100 the transistor can pass at most 2.6 mA — far below the 10+ mA the LED draws when fully on. The transistor **fails to saturate** and sits half-on: it drops voltage while refusing to carry the current, so the LED glows dimly. Lesson: Rb isn't "the bigger the more economical"; too large and the switch never fully closes.

For genuinely bigger loads (real motors at hundreds of mA to amps), practice trades the BJT for a **logic-level N-channel MOSFET**: its gate draws almost no current and its on-drop is smaller. Month 2 unpacks that; plant the seed here.

## Pull-up and pull-down resistors: never leave a pin speechless

An input pin with nothing attached is floating: like an antenna, it picks up stray charge and noise and reads **random values** — high, low, high — and your program twitches along. This is the classic cause of "sometimes it works, sometimes nudging the board fixes it".

The cure: with a large resistor (10 kΩ is the habit) pin the input to a known level —

- **Pull-up**: 10 kΩ from the pin to VCC; it reads HIGH at rest. Add a button from the pin to GND: **pressed**, the pin is dragged straight to GND and reads LOW; released, the pull-up restores HIGH. This "reads low when pressed" wiring is called **active low**.
- **Pull-down**: the mirror image. 10 kΩ from the pin to GND; it reads LOW at rest; a button from the pin to VCC makes it read HIGH when pressed.

Why 10 kΩ? The resistor only needs to hold the level, not supply current. Too large and noise can push the pin around; too small and pressing the button wastes current for nothing. 10 kΩ is the decades-old compromise.

Good news: many MCUs have configurable internal pull-ups, usable with one setting (`INPUT_PULLUP` on Arduino), and then the external resistor can go — but note this automatically means "pressed reads LOW".

## Batteries and LiPo safety

The lithium-polymer (LiPo) packs powering DIY robots come with parameters and rules you simply memorise:

- **One cell is 3.7 V nominal, 4.2 V full**; discharge **no lower than ≈ 3.0–3.3 V per cell**. Over-discharge damages the cell permanently — and a damaged cell is also a hazardous one to recharge.
- **"S" is the count of cells in series**: 2S = 2 × 3.7 V = 7.4 V nominal, 3S = 11.1 V. Check the S-count first when matching a battery to a design.
- **Capacity in mAh**: 2200 mAh means it can theoretically deliver 2200 mA for an hour (a manufacturer's estimate; treat it as such).
- **C rating × capacity = maximum continuous current**. Example: a 2200 mAh, 25C pack → 25 × 2200 mAh = 55 A.

<Quiz id="q6" question="A 1500 mAh, 20C LiPo: what is the maximum continuous current in A?" answer={30} unit="A" explanation="20 × 1500 mAh = 30 000 mA = 30 A." />

<Callout type="danger" title="LiPo safety rules">
**Never** charge unattended; only use a balance charger and charge inside a **LiPo-safe bag**. Overcharge, short circuit, puncture or mechanical damage can start a fire that arrives fast and is very hard to put out. Retire any **puffy** pack immediately — do not repair it, sell it, or push through. For long storage, hold each cell at **≈ 3.8 V** (many chargers have a one-button storage mode). These rules are not decoration; they come from people who burned a bench.
</Callout>

## Why a stalling motor resets your microcontroller (brownout)

The classic script: the robot snags a wall corner, the motor stalls, and in that instant the MCU resets and the program starts over. Not bad luck — circuit arithmetic.

**Step 1: how big is the stall current?** While spinning, a motor generates back-EMF that cancels part of the applied voltage, so current stays moderate. Stall the rotor completely and back-EMF vanishes; the motor becomes a small resistance and draws its **stall current — often several amps, 5–10× normal**.

**Step 2: how does that collapse the rail?** The battery has internal resistance; wires and connectors add more; all of it sits **in series** with the motor in the same loop. Ohm's law is unsentimental: V = I × R — whatever voltage those resistances swallow is voltage your devices never see.

**Worked example**: 2 A stall current, 0.5 Ω total series resistance (battery + wires + connectors).

```text
Drop = I × R = 2 A × 0.5 Ω = 1 V
```

A nominal 5 V rail sags to 4 V in that instant. If the 3.3 V regulator's input is already near its drop-out, its output falls right behind.

**Step 3: the MCU defensively resets.** MCUs contain a **brown-out detector**: when VCC dips below a safe threshold it forces a reset rather than letting the CPU misexecute in a half-dead state. So the "reboot" you saw was the protection working. Worse is when it doesn't reset but misbehaves: drifting sensor readings, garbled serial, random freezes.

**Fixes (roughly in order of effectiveness):**

1. Give the logic its **own regulated supply**: the MCU runs off its own LDO or buck, not the motor's dirty rail.
2. **Bulk electrolytic near the motor driver** (say 220–470 µF): absorbs the surge locally and buys the regulator time.
3. **Thicker, shorter power wires**: shrink that series resistance R directly.
4. **Common ground**: digital ground and power ground must meet at one point — but don't route motor current through the logic ground path.
5. **Soft-start / current limiting**: ramp PWM from zero or cap current in software, avoiding the shock at its source.

## Common mistakes and a before-you-move-on checklist

The beginner pits, in no particular order:

- No current-limiting resistor on an LED, or one chosen too small — it dies far faster than you'd think.
- Electrolytic reversed — swells, pops, ugly and hazardous.
- Decoupling cap parked centimetres away — at transient frequencies, centimetres are kilometres.
- Base resistor too large; the transistor never saturates and the load voltage never arrives.
- Button pin left floating; the input flickers and so does the program.
- 5 V signals into 3.3 V-only inputs — check the absolute maximum ratings first; "directly compatible" is not universal.
- Digital and power grounds never tied — the loop "looks closed" and is air.
- Abuse of the C rating, continuous over-discharge — the pack runs hotter and hotter until you regret it.

Before moving on, you should be able to answer "yes" to all of these:

- I can point at each symbol — resistor, capacitor, diode, LED, NPN transistor, GND — and state its job.
- I can explain junction dots vs dot-less crossings, and why two identical net labels are the same wire.
- I have computed τ = R × C by hand and know the 63 % and 99 % milestones.
- I can say why the decoupling cap must hug the chip, and what 100 nF vs bulk electrolytic each handle.
- I have sized an LED resistor and know to round up to the standard value.
- I clicked the Falstad transistor switch, and I can explain why the LED dimmed with Rb = 100 kΩ.
- I know why a floating input is dangerous and what `INPUT_PULLUP` does.
- I can recite the LiPo voltage windows, the meaning of S and C, and the safety rules.
- I can explain, with V = I × R, why a stall can reset the MCU.
