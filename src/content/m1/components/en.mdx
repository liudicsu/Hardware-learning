## Reading a schematic: connections, not physical layout

A schematic describes who connects to whom, not where parts sit on the physical board. On a real PCB parts are packed tight and traces run across several layers, but the schematic only cares about one thing: the electrical connection. So don't read it as a spatial map ("input on the left, output on the right"); read it as a list of "which pin goes to which".

Learning the symbols is step one. You will meet these over and over:

| Symbol name | What it does | How it's labelled |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resistor | Limits current, divides voltage | R1, R2…; drawn as a zigzag or a box |
| Capacitor | Stores charge, smooths voltage | C1, C2…; a polarised electrolytic carries a + mark |
| Diode | Lets current flow one way | D1, D2…; the bar side is the cathode |
| LED | Emits light when current flows | Labelled D or LED, with two arrows pointing away |
| NPN transistor | A small current controls a big current | Q1…; the emitter's arrow points **out** of the symbol |
| Switch / button | Opens or closes a circuit | SW or S |
| Ground | The 0 V reference for everything | GND |
| Supply labels | Name the power nets | VCC, VDD, 5V, 3V3, VBAT |

Three reading rules; keep them handy and you avoid most misreads:

- **Junction dot**: a filled dot where wires cross means those wires are actually connected.
- **Crossing without a dot = not connected**: the wires merely cross; electrically they may live on different layers.
- **Same net label = connected**: two places marked `3V3` or `SDA` are one net even if no wire is drawn between them; big schematics stay tidy thanks to this.

Bench tip: do not try to read the whole sheet at once. Pick one net (say GND or a signal), trace every pin on it, understand that one fully, then move to the next. Read a schematic one net at a time.

## Capacitors: storing charge, resisting sudden change

A <Term id="capacitor">capacitor</Term> is two metal plates placed very close together; charge "sits" between them. Two behaviours are enough to carry with you:

- It **resists sudden voltage change**: to jump the voltage across a capacitor instantly you would need an infinite current. More current charges it faster, but "instant" is impossible.
- Hence the classic motto: **block DC, pass changes**. A steady DC eventually cannot get through, while fast changes pass. Run it once in the simulator to observe it directly.

How fast it charges or discharges is set by the <Term id="rc-time-constant">time constant (τ)</Term>, the product of the resistance and the capacitance:

<Formula label="RC time constant">τ = R × C</Formula>

Rules of thumb (charging from zero):

- After **1 τ** the capacitor reaches **≈ 63 %** of its target voltage;
- After **5 τ** it reaches **≈ 99 %** — engineering-wise, "charged".

Both numbers come from the charging curve V(t) = V_final × (1 − e^(−t/τ)). At t = τ the fraction is 1 − e^(−1) = 0.632; at t = 5τ it is 1 − e^(−5) = 0.993.

**Worked example**: the embedded Falstad RC example uses R = 180 Ω and C = 33 µF.

```text
τ = 180 Ω × 33 µF = 5940 µs ≈ 5.9 ms
```

So roughly 5.9 ms after charging starts it is at 63 %, and about 5 × 5.94 ≈ 29.7 ms gets it to 99 %.

<Falstad title="RC charge/discharge (official Falstad example)" circuit="capac" height={420} />

How to play with it:

1. A square-wave source repeatedly charges and discharges the capacitor. Watch the scope trace: the voltage climbs and decays.
2. Compare against the numbers above: each climb passes 63 % around 1 τ and 99 % around 5 τ — the curve is steep at first, then flattens as the voltage approaches its final value exponentially.
3. **Predict, then act**: double-click the capacitor and make it 10× bigger (330 µF). Write your prediction first, then run: the time constant grows 10× and charging visibly slows. A whole ×10 is chosen so the change is obvious, not a subtle shift.

<Callout type="warn" title="Electrolytics have polarity!">
The aluminium electrolytic (the metal can) is **polarised**: a stripe or − mark on the shell marks the negative lead. Reversed, it heats and vents at best; at worst it **swells and pops** — the pre-scored cross on top is the vent. And never exceed its voltage rating: a 6.3 V cap on 5 V is fine, on 12 V is self-destruction. Check polarity and rating before applying power.

## Decoupling capacitors: supplying transient current locally

Inside a digital IC, gates switch extremely fast, and each switching event draws a short current pulse from the supply (a current spike). Supply traces have resistance and inductance; a changing current produces an L·di/dt drop. If the pulse must come from a distant source, the current loop is large and its inductance is high, so the voltage at the chip pins dips and can cause glitches or resets.

The fix is **a 100 nF ceramic capacitor (<Term id="decoupling-capacitor">decoupling capacitor</Term>) directly across each IC's supply pin (VCC) and GND, as physically close to the chip as possible**. The capacitor is a local charge store: the supply charges it continuously, and during a switching event the pulse flows through the capacitor and ground in a short loop with low inductance, so the L·di/dt voltage dip stays small. Hobby boards and volume production alike are wired this way.

- **100 nF ceramic**: one per chip, hugging its supply pins. If the board has multiple supply domains (say 3.3 V and 5 V coexisting), each domain needs its own.
- **Bulk electrolytic (100–470 µF)**: placed near motor drivers and other loads that draw large transient currents, providing a larger charge reserve for the whole board.

A real capacitor is not ideal: in series with its capacitance it has a small resistance, the <Term id="esr">equivalent series resistance (ESR)</Term>. When the capacitor supplies a current pulse I, the ESR alone drops ΔV = I × ESR, before any charge has been used. Multilayer ceramic capacitors have a much lower ESR than aluminium electrolytics, which is one reason the 100 nF next to each chip is ceramic, while the electrolytic provides the bulk charge. Capacitor datasheets list ESR (or the dissipation factor from which it follows); for a motor driver, multiply it by the current step you expect, for example the stall current from the brownout section below.

A phrase worth knowing: 100 nF is often quoted as “0.1 µF”; when a schematic or parts list says “add a 0.1”, that is what it means.

## Diodes and LEDs: one-way conduction and current limiting

A <Term id="diode">diode</Term> is a PN junction: with forward bias the barrier lowers and current flows from the anode to the cathode; with reverse bias the barrier rises and only a small leakage current flows. The bar in the symbol is the cathode. Conduction begins after the <Term id="led-forward-voltage">forward voltage (Vf)</Term> is overcome:

- Ordinary silicon diode: **≈ 0.6–0.7 V**;
- An LED's Vf depends on colour: **red ≈ 1.8–2.2 V**, **green/blue/white ≈ 2.8–3.3 V**.

An LED is a current-driven device: push its voltage just a little above Vf and the current shoots up steeply — a few millivolts too much can destroy it. That is why **an LED must always have a series current-limiting resistor**, the number-one cause of beginner LED failures. The resistor follows straight from Ohm's law:

<Formula label="LED current-limiting resistor">R = (Vsupply − Vf) / I</Formula>

where Vsupply is the supply voltage, Vf the LED's forward voltage, I the target current.

**Worked example**: 5 V supply, red LED (Vf = 2 V), target 10 mA.

```text
R = (5 V − 2 V) / 10 mA = 3 V / 0.01 A = 300 Ω
```

300 Ω is not an E12 standard value (…270, 330, 390…), so pick **up** to the nearest: **330 Ω**. Choose the larger value: a larger resistor only dims the LED, while a smaller one over-currents it and shortens its life until it fails.

<Falstad title="LED current limiting: 5 V, 330 Ω, red LED" circuit="ledLimit" height={380} />

The ammeter in series reads about 9.8 mA. Falstad's default LED model drops about 1.78 V rather than the 2 V used in the worked example, so the current is a little higher than the 9.1 mA you get from (5 − 2) / 330. That difference is normal — real LEDs vary too, which is why we design with margin. Try it: double-click the resistor, change 330 Ω to 100 Ω, predict the new current before looking, and notice how quickly the current rises.

### Inductors: why switching off a coil produces a spike

An <Term id="inductor">inductor</Term> (a coil; every motor and relay winding is one) opposes changes in its current. Its voltage is

<Formula label="Inductor voltage">V = L × dI/dt</Formula>

L in henries (H), dI/dt in amperes per second. Opening a switch tries to make the current fall to zero almost instantly, so dI/dt is huge and so is V.

<Falstad title="Inductive kick (official Falstad example)" circuit="inductkick" height={400} />

The circuit file (`inductkick.txt`) contains a 5 V source, a 1 H inductor, a 100 Ω resistor and a switch with 0.5 nF across its contacts.

1. Close the switch. The current settles at 5 V / 100 Ω = 50 mA.
2. Open the switch and watch the voltage across it. The 50 mA can only flow into the 0.5 nF capacitor, so the voltage rises until the inductor's energy has moved into it. The theoretical peak is I × √(L/C) = 0.05 A × √(1 H / 0.5 nF) ≈ 2.24 kV.
3. Repeat with the second example below, the same circuit with a diode across the inductor. When the switch opens, the current circulates through the diode, and the switch sees only the 5 V supply plus one diode drop.

<Falstad title="Inductive kick with a flyback diode (official Falstad example)" circuit="inductkickBlock" height={400} />

This is what the flyback diode described next does for motors and relays.

One more protective use: the **flyback diode**. Motors and relays are inductive loads; the instant you switch them off, the inductor — which cannot change its current abruptly — produces a high-voltage spike (tens of volts are common) that can damage the driving transistor. Wire a diode anti-parallel across the load; when the switch opens, the inductor current continues through the diode and the spike is limited to the supply voltage plus one diode forward drop. Month 2 applies this to motors.

## The transistor as a switch: small current controls a large current

Why a transistor? A microcontroller GPIO pin can **safely source only about 10–20 mA** (exact figure: read the datasheet), and its logic level is often just 3.3 V (ESP32 and most modern chips; the classic Arduino Uno is 5 V). Motors, LED strips and relays want hundreds of milliamps or more; wired straight to a pin, the pin or the chip can be damaged. The idea: **the GPIO provides only a control signal; the transistor carries the load current**.

The most common arrangement is the NPN low-side switch: the load sits between the supply and the collector, the emitter goes to ground, and the GPIO feeds a small current into the base through a base resistor. Transistor on, load circuit complete.

Sizing the base resistor: say target collector current Ic = 20 mA (lighting a small LED strip), and your NPN's current gain <Term id="beta-hfe">β (hFE)</Term> ≈ 100.

```text
Minimum base current: Ib(min) = Ic / β = 20 mA / 100 = 0.2 mA
```

But a switch must be **fully on** (saturated) — half-on wastes heat. Practice is to overdrive the base 5–10×; taking ×10:

```text
Ib ≈ 10 × 0.2 mA = 2 mA
Rb = (Vgpio − Vbe) / Ib = (3.3 V − 0.7 V) / 2 mA ≈ 1.3 kΩ
```

1.3 kΩ isn't standard either, so go **down** to 1 kΩ: a bit more base current, deeper <Term id="transistor-saturation">saturation</Term>, safer margin. Direction matters: when sizing the base resistor, err small (more saturation), not large.

**The same switch from a 5 V Uno pin.**

```text
Ib ≈ 2 mA (same Ic = 20 mA, β = 100, ×10 overdrive)
Rb = (5 V − 0.7 V) / 2 mA = 2.15 kΩ
```

Going down to the next E12 value gives 1.8 kΩ, Ib = 4.3 V / 1.8 kΩ = 2.4 mA.

**Operating regions of an NPN**

| Region | Condition | Vce | Power in the transistor P = Vce × Ic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cut-off | Vbe below about 0.6 V, Ib ≈ 0 | close to the supply | ≈ 0 (Ic ≈ 0) |
| Active (linear) | Ic = β × Ib, less than the load needs | several volts | large: Vce × Ic |
| Saturation | Ib well above Ic / β | about 0.2 V | small: at 20 mA, 0.2 V × 20 mA = 4 mW |

A switch must sit in cut-off or saturation; step 3 of the practice circuit below, with a 100 kΩ base resistor, leaves it in the active region.

<Falstad title="Transistor switch driving an LED (practice circuit)" circuit="transistorLed" height={420} />

Circuit: on the left a 3.3 V source through a switch (playing the role of a GPIO output HIGH) and a 1 kΩ base resistor drive the NPN; the collector side is 5 V → 220 Ω → LED. Steps:

1. **Click the switch** — the GPIO "goes HIGH" — and watch the LED light up.
2. Note the currents the sim reports: the **base branch carries only about 2–3 mA while the collector branch carries well over 10 mA** — a small current controls a large one.
3. **Break it**: double-click the 1 kΩ base resistor and set it to 100 kΩ. The LED dims or goes out. Why? Now Ib = (3.3 − 0.7) / 100 000 Ω ≈ 26 µA, so even with β = 100 the transistor can pass at most 2.6 mA — far below the 10+ mA the LED draws when fully on. The transistor **fails to saturate** and stays partly on: it drops voltage while carrying insufficient current, so the LED glows dimly. Conclusion: an oversized Rb limits the base current and prevents saturation.

For genuinely bigger loads (real motors at hundreds of mA to amps), practice trades the BJT for a **logic-level N-channel MOSFET**: its gate draws almost no current and its on-drop is smaller. Month 2 covers that in detail.

## Pull-up and pull-down resistors: holding a defined level

An input pin with nothing attached is floating: its input impedance is very high and its leakage current is typically below 1 µA, so a small charge coupled from nearby signals or from a hand can move its voltage across the VIH/VIL thresholds and the reading flips at random — high, low, high — and the program behaves erratically. This is a common cause of a circuit that "sometimes works and sometimes recovers when touched".

The cure: with a large resistor (10 kΩ is the habit) hold the input at a known level —

- **Pull-up**: 10 kΩ from the pin to VCC; it reads HIGH at rest. Add a button from the pin to GND: **pressed**, the pin is dragged straight to GND and reads LOW; released, the <Term id="pull-up-pull-down">pull-up</Term> restores HIGH. This "reads low when pressed" wiring is called **active low**.
- **Pull-down**: the complementary arrangement. 10 kΩ from the pin to GND; it reads LOW at rest; a button from the pin to VCC makes it read HIGH when pressed.

Why 10 kΩ? The resistor only needs to hold the level, not supply current. Too large and noise can push the pin around; too small and pressing the button wastes current for nothing. 10 kΩ is the decades-old compromise.

A digital input does not read every voltage as a clean 0 or 1. The datasheet gives two <Term id="logic-thresholds">logic thresholds</Term>: VIH, the lowest voltage guaranteed to read HIGH, and VIL, the highest voltage guaranteed to read LOW. Between them the result is undefined, and a floating pin can sit there.

```text
ATmega328P (Uno), VCC = 5 V:   VIH = 0.6 × VCC = 3.0 V   VIL = 0.3 × VCC = 1.5 V
ESP32-S3, VDD = 3.3 V:          VIH = 0.75 × VDD = 2.475 V   VIL = 0.25 × VDD = 0.825 V
ESP32-S3 output HIGH:            VOH ≥ 0.8 × VDD = 2.64 V (datasheet minimum, high-impedance load)
ESP32-S3 HIGH into an Uno input: 2.64 V < VIH = 3.0 V, so the Uno is not guaranteed to read HIGH
```

An unloaded ESP32-S3 output is typically close to 3.3 V, but the datasheet guarantees only 2.64 V, and the HIGH level falls further as the pin sources current. Under load the HIGH may therefore not register. Use a 3.3 V → 5 V level shifter for every ESP32-S3 signal into an Uno input. The other direction needs one too: a 5 V Uno output exceeds the ESP32-S3's maximum input voltage of VDD + 0.3 = 3.6 V.

The pull-up's other job is to keep the pin far from that band. On the Uno at 5 V, with the ATmega328P's maximum input leakage of 1 µA, a 10 kΩ pull-up drops only 1 µA × 10 kΩ = 10 mV. The pin sits at 4.99 V, 1.99 V above VIH, and pressing the button costs 5 V / 10 kΩ = 0.5 mA. A 1 MΩ pull-up drops up to 1 µA × 1 MΩ = 1 V, so the pin sits at 4.0 V. It still reads HIGH, but the margin to VIH halves to 1.0 V, and with 100 times the source resistance, the same coupled noise current shifts the pin 100 times further (ΔV = I × R). 10 kΩ keeps the pin within 10 mV of the rail for 0.5 mA while pressed.

Sources: ATmega48A/PA/88A/PA/168A/PA/328/P datasheet (DS40002061B), Table 30-1, Common DC characteristics; ESP32-S3 datasheet v2.2, Table 5-4, DC characteristics (3.3 V, 25 °C).

Many MCUs have configurable internal pull-ups, enabled with one setting (`INPUT_PULLUP` on Arduino), which removes the external resistor — but this also means "pressed reads LOW".

## Batteries and LiPo safety

The lithium-polymer (LiPo) packs powering DIY robots come with parameters and rules you simply memorise:

- **One cell is 3.7 V nominal, 4.2 V full**; discharge **no lower than ≈ 3.0–3.3 V per cell**. Over-discharge damages the cell permanently — and a damaged cell is also a hazardous one to recharge.
- **"S" is the count of cells in series**: 2S = 2 × 3.7 V = 7.4 V nominal, 3S = 11.1 V. Check the S-count first when matching a battery to a design.
- **Capacity in mAh**: 2200 mAh means it can theoretically deliver 2200 mA for an hour (a manufacturer's estimate; treat it as such).
- **C rating × capacity = maximum continuous current**. Example: a 2200 mAh, 25C pack → 25 × 2200 mAh = 55 A.

<Quiz id="q6" question="A 1500 mAh, 20C LiPo: what is the maximum continuous current in A?" answer={30} unit="A" explanation="20 × 1500 mAh = 30 000 mA = 30 A." />

<Callout type="danger" title="LiPo safety rules">
**Never** charge unattended; only use a balance charger and charge inside a **LiPo-safe bag**. Overcharge, short circuit, puncture or mechanical damage can start a fire that is difficult to extinguish. Retire any **puffy** pack immediately — do not repair it, sell it, or continue using it. For long storage, hold each cell at **≈ 3.8 V** (many chargers have a one-button storage mode). These rules address the thermal-runaway risk of LiPo cells.
</Callout>

## Why a stalling motor resets your microcontroller (brownout)

A typical sequence: the robot catches a wall corner, the motor stalls, and in that instant the MCU resets and the program starts over. This follows directly from Ohm's law and the internal resistance of the supply.

**Step 1: how big is the stall current?** While spinning, a motor generates <Term id="back-emf">back-EMF</Term> that cancels part of the applied voltage, so current stays moderate. Stall the rotor completely and back-EMF vanishes; the motor becomes a small resistance and draws its **stall current — often several amps, 5–10× normal**.

**Step 2: how does that collapse the rail?** The battery has internal resistance; wires and connectors add more; all of it sits **in series** with the motor in the same loop. By Ohm's law, V = I × R — whatever voltage those resistances drop is voltage your devices never receive.

**Worked example**: 2 A stall current, 0.5 Ω total series resistance (battery + wires + connectors).

```text
Drop = I × R = 2 A × 0.5 Ω = 1 V
```

A nominal 5 V rail sags to 4 V in that instant. If the 3.3 V regulator's input is already near its drop-out, its output falls right behind.

**Step 3: the MCU defensively resets.** MCUs contain a **brown-out detector**: when VCC dips below a safe threshold it forces a reset rather than letting the CPU execute incorrectly. So the "reboot" you saw was the protection working. Worse is a case where it does not reset but executes incorrectly: drifting sensor readings, garbled serial, random freezes.

**Fixes (roughly in order of effectiveness):**

1. Give the logic its **own regulated supply**: the MCU runs off its own <Term id="ldo">LDO</Term> or buck, not the motor's noisy supply rail.
2. **Bulk electrolytic near the motor driver** (say 220–470 µF): absorbs the surge locally and buys the regulator time.
3. **Thicker, shorter power wires**: shrink that series resistance R directly.
4. **Common ground**: digital ground and power ground must meet at one point — but don't route motor current through the logic ground path.
5. **Soft-start / current limiting**: ramp <Term id="pwm">PWM</Term> from zero or cap current in software, avoiding the shock at its source.

## Common mistakes and a before-you-move-on checklist

Common mistakes, in no particular order:

- No current-limiting resistor on an LED, or one chosen too small — it overheats and fails quickly.
- Electrolytic reversed — the case can swell and rupture, which is hazardous.
- Decoupling cap placed centimetres away — the trace inductance of a few centimetres already produces a significant L·di/dt drop during transients.
- Base resistor too large; the transistor never saturates and the load voltage never arrives.
- Button pin left floating; the input level changes at random and program behaviour becomes erratic.
- 5 V signals into 3.3 V-only inputs — check the absolute maximum ratings first; "directly compatible" is not universal.
- Digital and power grounds never tied — the circuit appears connected but has no electrical path.
- Exceeding the C rating, continuous over-discharge — the pack keeps heating up, causing overheating and permanent damage.

Before moving on, you should be able to answer "yes" to all of these:

- I can point at each symbol — resistor, capacitor, diode, LED, NPN transistor, GND — and state its job.
- I can explain junction dots vs dot-less crossings, and why two identical net labels are the same wire.
- I have computed τ = R × C by hand and know the 63 % and 99 % milestones.
- I can say why the decoupling cap must hug the chip, and what 100 nF vs bulk electrolytic each handle.
- I have sized an LED resistor and know to round up to the standard value.
- I clicked the Falstad transistor switch, and I can explain why the LED dimmed with Rb = 100 kΩ.
- I know why a floating input is dangerous and what `INPUT_PULLUP` does.
- I can recite the LiPo voltage windows, the meaning of S and C, and the safety rules.
- I can explain, with V = I × R, why a stall can reset the MCU.
