## Defining voltage, current and resistance

Electrical quantities are not directly visible, but they can be defined by measurable quantities. The three definitions below support every calculation in this lesson.

- **Voltage** is the electric potential difference between two points, measured in volts (V). A 9 V battery maintains about 9 V between its terminals. Voltage is always between two points.
- **Current** is the rate of charge flow: charge passing a point each second. Its unit is the ampere (A), and 1 A means 1 coulomb per second. Small circuits use milliamps (mA).
- **Resistance** is the ratio of the voltage across a component to the current through it, measured in ohms (Ω). A resistance of 1 Ω carries 1 A when 1 V is across it.

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Typical in a small robot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Voltage | V | volt (V) | 3.3 V, 5 V, 12 V |
| Current | I | ampere (A) | 5 mA to 2 A |
| Resistance | R | ohm (Ω) | 100 Ω to 1 MΩ |

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| m (milli) | 10^-3 | 20 mA = 0.020 A |
| k (kilo) | 10^3 | 4.7 kΩ = 4700 Ω |
| M (mega) | 10^6 | 1 MΩ = 1,000,000 Ω |

Ground (GND) is the node the circuit defines as 0 V. Every voltage in this lesson, Vout included, is measured from GND: the meter's black (COM) lead goes on GND, the red lead on the node of interest.

<Term id="current">Current</Term> only flows around a **closed loop**. Break the path and it stops, though <Term id="voltage">voltage</Term> remains across the break; a battery on a table has voltage but no current. We draw **conventional current** leaving the positive terminal and returning to the negative one; electrons drift the other way. Schematics use it too.

These definitions do not depend on a mechanical model: electrons already fill the wire and the field establishes almost instantly, a <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> converts electrical energy to heat, and voltage can exist with no current. Use the definitions and the equations directly.

## Ohm's law

One simple law ties them together:

<Formula label="Ohm's law">V = I × R</Formula>

With volts and ohms, current comes out in amps. Rearranged, `I = V / R` and `R = V / I`: know any two quantities and you can find the third. For fixed <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term>, doubling the voltage doubles the current; for fixed voltage, doubling the resistance halves it. Large resistance is nearly an open circuit, small resistance nearly a short.

**Worked example.** A 9 V battery is connected across a 1 kΩ resistor. How much current flows?

1. Convert first: 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω.
2. Apply the law: I = 9 V / 1000 Ω.
3. Divide: I = 0.009 A.
4. Convert: 0.009 A × 1000 = 9 mA.

Reverse check: 0.009 × 1000 = 9 V. Make that a bench habit.

## Power: a resistor converts electrical energy to heat

Current through resistance does work, which in a resistor becomes heat. <Term id="power">Power</Term> is energy per second, in watts (W):

<Formula label="Power in a resistor">P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R</Formula>

Substitute V = I × R into P = V × I to get I² × R, or substitute I = V / R to get V² / R. Use the form you know.

Every resistor has a **power rating**, the maximum it can dissipate safely. Kits commonly contain 1/4 W through-hole parts; 1/8, 1/2, 1 and 2 W also exist. The rating is a limit, not a target.

**Worked example.** A 100 Ω resistor has 5 V across it. The current is I = 5 / 100 = 0.05 A = 50 mA, so P = 5 × 0.05 = 0.25 W. The V² / R form agrees: 25 / 100 = 0.25 W.

That is exactly the 1/4 W rating. At 100 % it runs very hot and ages quickly. Use at most about 50 %, so fit a 1/2 W part here: 0.25 / 0.5 = 50 %. In a closed box, go one size larger.

## Series and parallel

**Series** means one path. The same current flows through every part, the voltages add, and the resistances add:

R(total) = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

For example, 220 Ω + 330 Ω = 550 Ω; on 5 V the current is 5 / 550 = 0.00909 A ≈ 9.1 mA.

**Parallel** means every component shares two nodes. All see the same voltage and the current splits. Resistances combine as reciprocals:

1 / R(total) = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + 1 / R3 + ...

For two resistors, R = R1 × R2 / (R1 + R2). Two equal resistors give half, so 10 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ is 5 kΩ. Check: 1 / R = 1 / 10000 + 1 / 10000 = 2 / 10000, so R = 5000 Ω.

Sanity checks: a parallel result is always smaller than the smallest resistor, and a series result always larger than the largest. If not, you inverted the wrong thing.

## The voltage divider

A <Term id="voltage-divider">voltage divider</Term> uses two resistors to scale a voltage down. R1 goes from the input to the output node, R2 from the output to <Term id="gnd">ground</Term>, and the output is across R2:

<Formula label="Voltage divider">Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)</Formula>

It follows from series rules in three lines, since both resistors carry the same current:

1. I = Vin / (R1 + R2)
2. Vout = I × R2, because the output is the voltage across R2
3. Substitute I: Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Check the extremes: R2 = 0 Ω gives Vout = 0 V, a very large R2 passes almost all of Vin, and equal resistors give half. The formula agrees.

**The practice task.** Take Vin = 5 V, R1 = 10 kΩ on top, R2 = 4.7 kΩ to ground. Compute by hand first.

1. Add them: R1 + R2 = 10 kΩ + 4.7 kΩ = 14.7 kΩ.
2. Find the current: I = 5 V / 14,700 Ω = 0.0003401 A.
3. Multiply by R2: Vout = 0.0003401 A × 4700 Ω = 1.5986 V.
4. Round: Vout ≈ 1.6 V.

The output probe should read about 1.6 V. Then double-click R2 and change it to 10 kΩ. Predict first: equal resistors give half of 5 V, or 2.5 V. Check afterwards. If you were wrong, redo the calculation instead of nudging the value until it looks right.

Dividers are everywhere in robotics. A 12 V battery often feeds an <Term id="adc">ADC</Term> that accepts only 5 V or 3.3 V, so a divider scales it down, and a 5 V sensor signal may need shifting to 3.3 V. A potentiometer is a divider with a moving wiper: its ends sit across the supply, and the wiper voltage reports shaft angle — the basis of many joysticks and position sensors.

**Worked example: battery voltage into an ADC.**

```text
Uno A0, ADC reference 5 V. A 2S LiPo is 8.4 V when full. R1 = R2 = 10 kΩ:
Vout = 8.4 × 10 / (10 + 10) = 4.20 V, below 5 V.
The divider draws 8.4 V / 20 kΩ = 0.42 mA all the time.
analogRead returns about 4.20 / 5 × 1023 = 859.

ESP32-S3: a 3S LiPo is 12.6 V when full. R1 = 100 kΩ, R2 = 27 kΩ:
Vout = 12.6 × 27 / 127 = 2.68 V; the divider draws 12.6 V / 127 kΩ = 99 µA.
```

The Uno ADC formula V = raw × 5 / 1023 is used in Month 2, Arduino I/O. The usable input range of the ESP32-S3 ADC depends on the attenuation setting, so check it in [Espressif's ADC documentation](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/peripherals/adc_oneshot.html) before choosing R2.

Larger resistors waste less battery current; the next section shows why the ADC input must still not be loaded heavily.

## Loading: a divider is not a power supply

The formula assumes nothing is connected to the output. A real load draws current and sits in parallel with R2, making the effective bottom resistance smaller than R2. A smaller bottom resistance takes a smaller share of the input, so Vout drops.

Take the previous divider and add a 10 kΩ load across R2. The bottom becomes 4.7 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ: (4.7 × 10) / (4.7 + 10) = 47 / 14.7 = 3.197 kΩ ≈ 3.2 kΩ. Recalculate: Vout = 5 × 3.2 / 13.2 = 1.212 V. Instead of 1.6 V you get about 1.2 V, a 25 % drop. A motor would pull far more and collapse the output completely.

<Falstad title="Loaded divider: a switch adds a 10 kΩ load" circuit="loadedDivider" height={420} />

The same 5 V, 10 kΩ over 4.7 kΩ divider as above. With switch S open the probe reads 1.60 V; click S to connect the 10 kΩ load RL and the probe drops to about 1.21 V, matching the hand calculation of 1.212 V. The simulator also shows the current through R1 now splitting: about 0.26 mA through R2 and 0.12 mA through RL — KCL, which the next section explains. Try it: double-click RL and change it to 100 kΩ, but predict first. It should drop much less, the 10× rule.

As a rule of thumb, keep the load at least 10× R2; even then the output sags slightly. For a stiff voltage, use a lower-resistance divider, or better, a regulator.

<Callout type="warn" title="Never power a load from a divider">
A divider is a **signal**, not a supply. It cannot deliver current without its output collapsing. Do not power a motor, relay, LED strip or microcontroller from one — use a linear regulator or a buck converter. If a divider feeds an ADC, check the loaded voltage, not just the open-circuit value.
</Callout>

## Kirchhoff in brief

**Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)** says the signed voltages around any closed loop add to zero: every rise is balanced by drops. Around the divider loop, Vin − I × R1 − I × R2 = 0, which rearranges to I = Vin / (R1 + R2). That is where the divider formula comes from.

**Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)** says current into a node equals current out; charge does not pile up. At the divider output, the current arriving through R1 splits between R2 and any load: I(R1) = I(R2) + I(load). Add a load and it takes a share, which is the loading effect in one sentence.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing mA and A, or kΩ and Ω.** 5 V across 220 Ω is 0.0227 A = 22.7 mA, not 22.7 A. Write units every step and convert prefixes before dividing.
- **Swapping R1 and R2.** R1 is the top resistor from Vin to the output; R2 is the bottom one to ground. A larger R2 gives a higher Vout; check R2 = 0 and R2 huge to see whether the formula is the right way up.
- **Forgetting the power rating.** A resistance value does not tell you whether a resistor survives; calculate P and choose at least twice the dissipation.
- **Using a divider as a supply.** Fine for an ADC input or a reference, not for anything drawing real current.
- **Blaming the simulator.** Check for a missing ground, an open switch, a wrong value or a unit error before doubting the tool; most big discrepancies come from the wiring.
