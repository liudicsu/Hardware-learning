## Defining voltage, current and resistance

Electrical quantities are not directly visible, but they can be defined by measurable quantities. The three definitions below support every calculation in this lesson.

- **Voltage** is the electric potential difference between two points, measured in volts (V). A 9 V battery maintains about 9 V between its terminals. Voltage is always between two points.
- **Current** is the rate of charge flow: charge passing a point each second. Its unit is the ampere (A), and 1 A means 1 coulomb per second. Small circuits use milliamps (mA).
- **Resistance** is the ratio of the voltage across a component to the current through it, measured in ohms (Ω). A resistance of 1 Ω carries 1 A when 1 V is across it.

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Typical in a small robot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Voltage | V | volt (V) | 3.3 V, 5 V, 12 V |
| Current | I | ampere (A) | 5 mA to 2 A |
| Resistance | R | ohm (Ω) | 100 Ω to 1 MΩ |

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| m (milli) | 10^-3 | 20 mA = 0.020 A |
| k (kilo) | 10^3 | 4.7 kΩ = 4700 Ω |
| M (mega) | 10^6 | 1 MΩ = 1,000,000 Ω |

Ground (GND) is the node the circuit defines as 0 V. Every voltage in this lesson, Vout included, is measured from GND: the meter's black (COM) lead goes on GND, the red lead on the node of interest.

<Term id="current">Current</Term> only flows around a **closed loop**. Break the path and it stops, though <Term id="voltage">voltage</Term> remains across the break; a battery on a table has voltage but no current. We draw **conventional current** leaving the positive terminal and returning to the negative one; electrons drift the other way. Schematics use it too.

These definitions do not depend on a mechanical model: electrons already fill the wire and the field establishes almost instantly, a <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> converts electrical energy to heat, and voltage can exist with no current. Use the definitions and the equations directly.

## Ohm's law

One simple law ties them together:

<Formula label="Ohm's law">V = I × R</Formula>

With volts and ohms, current comes out in amps. Rearranged, `I = V / R` and `R = V / I`: know any two quantities and you can find the third. For fixed <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term>, doubling the voltage doubles the current; for fixed voltage, doubling the resistance halves it. Large resistance is nearly an open circuit, small resistance nearly a short.

**Worked example.** A 9 V battery is connected across a 1 kΩ resistor. How much current flows?

1. Convert first: 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω.
2. Apply the law: I = 9 V / 1000 Ω.
3. Divide: I = 0.009 A.
4. Convert: 0.009 A × 1000 = 9 mA.

Reverse check: 0.009 × 1000 = 9 V. Make that a bench habit.

<Falstad title="Ohm's law: 100 Ω and 1 kΩ on the same 5 V source" circuit="ohms" />

Predict before reading the numbers. Both resistors see 5 V, so the 100 Ω one carries 5 / 100 = 0.05 A = 50 mA, and the 1 kΩ one carries 5 / 1000 = 0.005 A = 5 mA: ten times the resistance, one tenth the current. Hover to check, then double-click, change the value, and predict again.

## Power: a resistor converts electrical energy to heat

Current through resistance does work, which in a resistor becomes heat. <Term id="power">Power</Term> is energy per second, in watts (W):

<Formula label="Power in a resistor">P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R</Formula>

Substitute V = I × R into P = V × I to get I² × R, or substitute I = V / R to get V² / R. Use the form you know.

Every resistor has a **power rating**, the maximum it can dissipate safely. Kits commonly contain 1/4 W through-hole parts; 1/8, 1/2, 1 and 2 W also exist. The rating is a limit, not a target.

**Worked example.** A 100 Ω resistor has 5 V across it. The current is I = 5 / 100 = 0.05 A = 50 mA, so P = 5 × 0.05 = 0.25 W. The V² / R form agrees: 25 / 100 = 0.25 W.

That is exactly the 1/4 W rating. At 100 % it runs very hot and ages quickly. Use at most about 50 %, so fit a 1/2 W part here: 0.25 / 0.5 = 50 %. In a closed box, go one size larger.

## Series and parallel

**Series** means one path. The same current flows through every part, the voltages add, and the resistances add:

R(total) = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

For example, 220 Ω + 330 Ω = 550 Ω; on 5 V the current is 5 / 550 = 0.00909 A ≈ 9.1 mA.

**Parallel** means every component shares two nodes. All see the same voltage and the current splits. Resistances combine as reciprocals:

1 / R(total) = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + 1 / R3 + ...

For two resistors, R = R1 × R2 / (R1 + R2). Two equal resistors give half, so 10 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ is 5 kΩ. Check: 1 / R = 1 / 10000 + 1 / 10000 = 2 / 10000, so R = 5000 Ω.

Sanity checks: a parallel result is always smaller than the smallest resistor, and a series result always larger than the largest. If not, you inverted the wrong thing.

## The voltage divider

A <Term id="voltage-divider">voltage divider</Term> uses two resistors to scale a voltage down. R1 goes from the input to the output node, R2 from the output to <Term id="gnd">ground</Term>, and the output is across R2:

<Formula label="Voltage divider">Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)</Formula>

It follows from series rules in three lines, since both resistors carry the same current:

1. I = Vin / (R1 + R2)
2. Vout = I × R2, because the output is the voltage across R2
3. Substitute I: Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Check the extremes: R2 = 0 Ω gives Vout = 0 V, a very large R2 passes almost all of Vin, and equal resistors give half. The formula agrees.

**The practice task.** Take Vin = 5 V, R1 = 10 kΩ on top, R2 = 4.7 kΩ to ground. Compute by hand first.

1. Add them: R1 + R2 = 10 kΩ + 4.7 kΩ = 14.7 kΩ.
2. Find the current: I = 5 V / 14,700 Ω = 0.0003401 A.
3. Multiply by R2: Vout = 0.0003401 A × 4700 Ω = 1.5986 V.
4. Round: Vout ≈ 1.6 V.

Dividers are everywhere in robotics. A 12 V battery often feeds an <Term id="adc">ADC</Term> that accepts only 5 V or 3.3 V, so a divider scales it down, and a 5 V sensor signal may need shifting to 3.3 V. A potentiometer is a divider with a moving wiper: its ends sit across the supply, and the wiper voltage reports shaft angle — the basis of many joysticks and position sensors.

**Worked example: battery voltage into an ADC.**

```text
Uno A0, ADC reference 5 V. A 2S LiPo is 8.4 V when full. R1 = R2 = 10 kΩ:
Vout = 8.4 × 10 / (10 + 10) = 4.20 V, below 5 V.
The divider draws 8.4 V / 20 kΩ = 0.42 mA all the time.
analogRead returns about 4.20 / 5 × 1023 = 859.

ESP32-S3: a 3S LiPo is 12.6 V when full. R1 = 100 kΩ, R2 = 27 kΩ:
Vout = 12.6 × 27 / 127 = 2.68 V; the divider draws 12.6 V / 127 kΩ = 99 µA.
```

The Uno ADC formula V = raw × 5 / 1023 is used in Month 2, Arduino I/O. The usable input range of the ESP32-S3 ADC depends on the attenuation setting, so check it in [Espressif's ADC documentation](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/peripherals/adc_oneshot.html) before choosing R2.

Larger resistors waste less battery current; the next section shows why the ADC input must still not be loaded heavily.

## Loading: a divider is not a power supply

The formula assumes nothing is connected to the output. A real load draws current and sits in parallel with R2, making the effective bottom resistance smaller than R2. A smaller bottom resistance takes a smaller share of the input, so Vout drops.

Take the previous divider and add a 10 kΩ load across R2. The bottom becomes 4.7 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ: (4.7 × 10) / (4.7 + 10) = 47 / 14.7 = 3.197 kΩ ≈ 3.2 kΩ. Recalculate: Vout = 5 × 3.2 / 13.2 = 1.212 V. Instead of 1.6 V you get about 1.2 V, a 25 % drop. A motor would pull far more and collapse the output completely.

<Falstad title="Loaded divider: a switch adds a 10 kΩ load" circuit="loadedDivider" height={420} />

The same 5 V, 10 kΩ over 4.7 kΩ divider as above. With switch S open the probe reads 1.60 V; click S to connect the 10 kΩ load RL and the probe drops to about 1.21 V, matching the hand calculation of 1.212 V. The simulator also shows the current through R1 now splitting: about 0.26 mA through R2 and 0.12 mA through RL — KCL, which the next section explains. Try it: double-click RL and change it to 100 kΩ, but predict first. It should drop much less, the 10× rule.

As a rule of thumb, keep the load at least 10× R2; even then the output sags slightly. For a stiff voltage, use a lower-resistance divider, or better, a regulator.

<Callout type="warn" title="Never power a load from a divider">
A divider is a **signal**, not a supply. It cannot deliver current without its output collapsing. Do not power a motor, relay, LED strip or microcontroller from one — use a linear regulator or a buck converter. If a divider feeds an ADC, check the loaded voltage, not just the open-circuit value.
</Callout>

## Kirchhoff in brief

**Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)** says the signed voltages around any closed loop add to zero: every rise is balanced by drops. Around the divider loop, Vin − I × R1 − I × R2 = 0, which rearranges to I = Vin / (R1 + R2). That is where the divider formula comes from.

**Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)** says current into a node equals current out; charge does not pile up. At the divider output, the current arriving through R1 splits between R2 and any load: I(R1) = I(R2) + I(load). Add a load and it takes a share, which is the loading effect in one sentence.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing mA and A, or kΩ and Ω.** 5 V across 220 Ω is 0.0227 A = 22.7 mA, not 22.7 A. Write units every step and convert prefixes before dividing.
- **Swapping R1 and R2.** R1 is the top resistor from Vin to the output; R2 is the bottom one to ground. A larger R2 gives a higher Vout; check R2 = 0 and R2 huge to see whether the formula is the right way up.
- **Forgetting the power rating.** A resistance value does not tell you whether a resistor survives; calculate P and choose at least twice the dissipation.
- **Using a divider as a supply.** Fine for an ADC input or a reference, not for anything drawing real current.
- **Blaming the simulator.** Check for a missing ground, an open switch, a wrong value or a unit error before doubting the tool; most big discrepancies come from the wiring.

## Thévenin and Norton equivalents

The loading section above recomputed `R2 ∥ RL` by hand for one load. When the load changes (a sensor, an ADC input, a test resistor), it is faster to collapse everything behind the output into **two numbers** once. This section is that step. It is **not** the Wheatstone bridge from the next lesson, and it is **not** just the "read the open-circuit voltage" tip from the multimeter lesson.

<Resource id="wiki-thevenin" />

**Sources actually opened (HTML body read).**

- **Wikipedia — Thévenin's theorem:** any linear network of voltage sources, current sources and resistances can be replaced at two terminals by one voltage source `Vth` in series with one resistance `Rth`. `Vth` is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals; `Rth` is the resistance seen with ideal voltage sources replaced by shorts and current sources by opens, or equivalently `Vth` divided by the short-circuit current. Numbers 以原文为准.
- **Wikipedia — Norton's theorem:** the dual form, a current source `In` (the short-circuit current) in parallel with `Rn`; `Rn = Rth`, `Vth = In × Rn`. Its worked example (a 15 V network that becomes 3.75 mA in parallel with 2 kΩ) 以原文为准.
- **Wikipedia — Maximum power transfer theorem:** load power is maximum when the load resistance equals the source resistance, but efficiency `η = RL / (RL + RS)` is then only 50 %; maximum efficiency needs the source resistance as close to zero as possible. 以原文为准.

### Two numbers for this lesson's divider

Take the practice divider again: 5 V, R1 = 10 kΩ on top, R2 = 4.7 kΩ to ground.

1. Open circuit (no load): `Vth = 5 × 4.7 / 14.7 = 1.5986 V`, the familiar 1.6 V.
2. Zero the source (replace the 5 V source by a wire): seen from the output, R1 and R2 both go to ground, so `Rth = 10 kΩ ∥ 4.7 kΩ = 3197 Ω ≈ 3.2 kΩ`.

<Formula label="Thévenin loaded voltage">V_L = Vth × RL / (Rth + RL)</Formula>

With RL = 10 kΩ: `1.5986 × 10 / 13.197 = 1.2113 V`, the same ≈ 1.21 V the loading section found with `R2 ∥ RL`. The Norton twin is `In = Vth / Rth = 0.5000 mA` in parallel with `Rn = 3197 Ω`, and `In × (Rn ∥ RL)` gives the same 1.2113 V. The formula also explains the 10× rule: reading only `Vth` is wrong by the fraction `Rth / (Rth + RL)`. With RL = 47 kΩ (10 × R2) that is `3.197 / 50.2 ≈ 6.4 %`; because `Rth` is always smaller than R2, "10 × R2" is a safe stand-in, but the ratio that actually sets the error is RL against **Rth**.

**Maximum power transfer:** for a fixed `Rth`, the load power `V_L² / RL` is largest at `RL = Rth` (here `Vth² / (4·Rth) ≈ 0.20 mW` at 3.2 kΩ), but the efficiency `RL / (RL + Rth)` is then only 50 % — half the power is lost in the source resistance — so power supplies and battery-powered systems do not aim for impedance matching; they keep the source resistance far below the load (Wikipedia — Maximum power transfer theorem, 以原文为准).

### Measuring Rth on the bench

- **Two readings with a known test resistor.** Read `Voc`, then connect a known `Rtest` and read `Vtest`. Since `Vtest = Voc × Rtest / (Rth + Rtest)`, `Rth = Rtest × (Voc / Vtest − 1)`.
- **Short-circuit current.** `Rth = Voc / Isc`. An ammeter is not a perfect short: its burden resistance sits in series, so it reads `Voc / (Rth + R_burden)`. Never short a battery or a supply that can deliver large current just to measure this.

### Fair setup

`thevenin_loaded_divider_toy.py` is **synthetic** and stdlib only. **Model assumptions:** linear resistors; an ideal 5 V source with no source resistance and no drift; every resistor (R1, R2, RL, Rtest) is a 1 % part drawn uniformly; meter readings have Gaussian noise of 1 mV (voltage) or 1 µA (current) and are otherwise ideal (no input loading); the ammeter adds a **synthetic 100 Ω burden** (an exaggerated stand-in, real burden depends on the meter and range); `Rtest` is used at its nominal value. Truth is an independent KCL solve at the output node with the actual parts.

| Arm | Prediction of the loaded voltage |
|---|---|
| A_voc_only | `V_L = Voc` (ignores Rth) |
| B_thevenin | `Vth = Voc`, `Rth` from the two-reading method, then the formula above |
| C_norton | `In` = short-circuit reading through the 100 Ω burden, `Rn = Voc / In`, `V_L = In × (Rn ∥ RL)` |

**Common random numbers:** for each (seed, board) the actual R1, R2, RL and Rtest and the meter-noise draws are made once and shared by all three arms; all arms use the same `Voc` reading. Five seeds × 32 boards; metric = mean `|V_pred − V_true|` per seed, reported as mean±sd over seeds. The metric scores only the loaded voltage. It is **blind** to the risk of the short-circuit reading (a real supply can be damaged), to the time each method takes, and to the power in the test resistor.

```python
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Synthetic Thevenin / Norton equivalent of a loaded divider. Stdlib only.

Network (linear resistors, ideal 5 V source, no source resistance):
  Vs --R1-- out --R2-- GND, load RL from out to GND. Nominal R1 = 10 kOhm,
  R2 = 4.7 kOhm (the lesson's divider), so Vth = 1.5986 V, Rth = R1||R2 = 3197 Ohm.

Truth: KCL at node out with the ACTUAL (toleranced) parts:
  (Vs - V)/R1 = V/R2 + V/RL   ->   V = (Vs/R1) / (1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/RL)

Every arm predicts the loaded voltage from bench measurements of the unloaded
network, then is scored against that truth:
  A_voc_only   V_L = Voc                       (ignores Rth)
  B_thevenin   Vth = Voc; Rth from a second reading with a known 10 kOhm test
               resistor: Rth = Rtest_nominal * (Voc/Vtest - 1);
               V_L = Vth * RL / (Rth + RL)
  C_norton     In = short-circuit current read through an ammeter with a
               100 Ohm burden; Rn = Voc / In; V_L = In * (Rn || RL)

Common random numbers: per (seed, board) the actual R1, R2, RL and Rtest (1 %
parts, uniform) and the meter noise draws z1 (Voc), z2 (Vtest / Isc) are drawn
once and shared by every arm. The meter is otherwise ideal (no input loading).

Regimes (5 seeds x 32 boards): heavy_load RL = 3.3 kOhm (about Rth),
lesson_load RL = 10 kOhm, light_load RL = 10 MOhm (B and C: near-tie / small gap;
A-B: small but consistent gap, not a tie). "Consistent" means only |paired
mean| > paired sd over seeds; the sign count says how many seeds disagree.
No presumed winner. Real DMM / bench Thevenin lab: 本页未运行.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random

VS = 5.0  # V, ideal source
R1_NOM = 10_000.0  # Ohm
R2_NOM = 4_700.0  # Ohm
RTEST_NOM = 10_000.0  # Ohm, known test resistor for the two-reading Rth method
TOL = 0.01  # 1 % parts, uniform
V_NOISE = 1e-3  # V, meter reading sd (about one count on a 2 V range)
I_NOISE = 1e-6  # A, meter reading sd (about one count on a 2 mA range)
R_BURDEN = 100.0  # Ohm, ammeter burden in series during the short-circuit reading
SEEDS = (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)
BOARDS = 32
ARMS = ("A_voc_only", "B_thevenin", "C_norton")
REGIMES = (("heavy_load", 3_300.0), ("lesson_load", 10_000.0), ("light_load", 10_000_000.0))


def thevenin(vs: float, r1: float, r2: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Open-circuit voltage and resistance seen at `out` with the source zeroed."""
    return vs * r2 / (r1 + r2), r1 * r2 / (r1 + r2)


def truth_kcl(vs: float, r1: float, r2: float, rl: float) -> float:
    """Loaded node voltage from KCL, independent of the equivalent circuits."""
    return (vs / r1) / (1.0 / r1 + 1.0 / r2 + 1.0 / rl)


def parallel(a: float, b: float) -> float:
    return a * b / (a + b)


def shared_board(seed: int, board: int, rl_nom: float) -> dict:
    """Common random numbers: one draw per (seed, board), shared by every arm."""
    rng = random.Random(1_000_003 * seed + 7_919 * board + int(rl_nom) % 100_003)
    tol = lambda nom: nom * (1.0 + rng.uniform(-TOL, TOL))  # noqa: E731
    return {
        "r1": tol(R1_NOM),
        "r2": tol(R2_NOM),
        "rl": tol(rl_nom),
        "rtest": tol(RTEST_NOM),
        "z1": rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0),
        "z2": rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0),
    }


def predict(arm: str, b: dict) -> float:
    vth, rth = thevenin(VS, b["r1"], b["r2"])
    voc = vth + V_NOISE * b["z1"]  # same reading for every arm
    if arm == "A_voc_only":
        return voc
    if arm == "B_thevenin":
        vtest = truth_kcl(VS, b["r1"], b["r2"], b["rtest"]) + V_NOISE * b["z2"]
        rth_hat = RTEST_NOM * (voc / vtest - 1.0)  # uses the NOMINAL test value
        return voc * b["rl"] / (rth_hat + b["rl"])
    if arm == "C_norton":
        isc = vth / (rth + R_BURDEN) + I_NOISE * b["z2"]  # burden lowers the reading
        rn_hat = voc / isc
        return isc * parallel(rn_hat, b["rl"])
    raise ValueError(arm)


def mean_sd(xs: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
    mu = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    var = sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1)
    return mu, math.sqrt(var)


def sign_note(diffs: list[float], m: float) -> str:
    """How many seeds disagree in sign with the paired mean (zeros listed apart)."""
    n, opp, zero = len(diffs), sum(1 for d in diffs if d * m < 0), sum(1 for d in diffs if d == 0)
    if opp == 0 and zero == 0:
        return f"same sign on all {n} seeds"
    note = f"{opp} of {n} seeds {'has' if opp == 1 else 'have'} the opposite sign"
    return note + (f", {zero} of {n} equal" if zero else "")


def run_regime(rl_nom: float) -> dict:
    """Per arm: per-seed mean |V_pred - V_true| over the boards, in mV."""
    out = {arm: [] for arm in ARMS}
    for seed in SEEDS:
        boards = [shared_board(seed, i, rl_nom) for i in range(BOARDS)]
        for arm in ARMS:
            errs = [abs(predict(arm, b) - truth_kcl(VS, b["r1"], b["r2"], b["rl"])) for b in boards]
            out[arm].append(1e3 * sum(errs) / len(errs))
    return out


def main() -> None:
    vth, rth = thevenin(VS, R1_NOM, R2_NOM)
    print("Synthetic Thevenin/Norton toy; shared parts and meter-noise draws; not a bench lab")
    print(
        f"nominal Vs={VS}V R1={R1_NOM:.0f}ohm R2={R2_NOM:.0f}ohm -> Vth={vth:.4f}V Rth={rth:.1f}ohm; "
        f"tol={TOL * 100:.0f}% V_noise={V_NOISE * 1e3:.0f}mV I_noise={I_NOISE * 1e6:.0f}uA "
        f"burden={R_BURDEN:.0f}ohm seeds={len(SEEDS)} boards={BOARDS}"
    )
    print(
        f"nominal check: RL=10k -> V_L = Vth*RL/(Rth+RL) = {vth * 1e4 / (rth + 1e4):.4f} V; "
        f"KCL = {truth_kcl(VS, R1_NOM, R2_NOM, 1e4):.4f} V; Norton In=Vth/Rth={vth / rth * 1e3:.4f} mA"
    )
    print("regime RL_ohm arm |V_pred-V_true|_mV (mean+-sd over seeds)")
    summary = {}
    paired = {}
    for name, rl in REGIMES:
        res = run_regime(rl)
        ab = [x - y for x, y in zip(res["A_voc_only"], res["B_thevenin"])]
        cb = [x - y for x, y in zip(res["C_norton"], res["B_thevenin"])]
```

**Key points**

- Ohm's law V = I × R: 9 V across 1 kΩ gives I = 9 V / 1000 Ω = 0.009 A = 9 mA.
- Power P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R: 5 V across 100 Ω dissipates 0.25 W, so a 1/2 W part runs it at the recommended 50 % of rating.
- Series resistances add (220 Ω + 330 Ω = 550 Ω) and parallel ones combine as reciprocals (10 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ).
- Divider Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2): 5 V with R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 = 4.7 kΩ gives Vout ≈ 1.6 V.
- Loading matters: a 10 kΩ load across R2 drops that output to about 1.2 V, a 25 % fall, so keep the load at least 10× R2.
- Battery into an ADC: 8.4 V with 10 kΩ / 10 kΩ gives 4.20 V and 0.42 mA standing current.
- Thévenin / Norton: the practice divider collapses to `Vth = 1.5986 V` behind `Rth ≈ 3.2 kΩ` (Norton 0.5 mA ∥ 3.2 kΩ), so `V_L = Vth × RL / (Rth + RL)` reproduces 1.21 V at 10 kΩ; when RL ≫ Rth, Thévenin and Norton are a **near-tie / small gap** and `Voc` alone trails by only a small but consistent gap (not a tie) — not a bench lab.

**Common mistakes**

- Mixing mA with A, or kΩ with Ω: 5 V across 220 Ω is 0.0227 A = 22.7 mA, not 22.7 A.
- Swapping R1 and R2: R1 is the top resistor from Vin to the output, R2 is the bottom one to ground.
- Forgetting the power rating: calculate P first and choose a rating of at least twice the dissipation.
- Powering a load from a divider: it is a signal, not a supply.
