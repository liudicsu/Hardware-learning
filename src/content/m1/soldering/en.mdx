## Why solder at all

A breadboard holds components by friction: jumper pins grip spring contacts, and a firm tug pulls a wire out. On a desk that is an advantage — rewiring takes seconds; inside a robot it becomes a liability, because motor vibration gradually loosens friction fits. A jumper can go from secure to half-seated; a half-seated contact does not fail completely but produces an **intermittent fault**: it works on the bench, fails under vibration, and works again when touched.

Soldering replaces friction with a permanent joint. An alloy — 63/37 tin-lead, or lead-free SAC305 — is melted so it wets the pad and the lead, then solidifies into one piece with negligible resistance that cannot vibrate loose. **Solder what must not come loose.**

Two families of parts, and this lesson uses only the first:

- **Through-hole**: the lead passes through a hole and is soldered on the far side. The hole holds the part while you work, the joint is millimetres across and forgiving, and it can be desoldered later.
- **Surface-mount (SMD)**: the part sits on a flat pad, held only by its own solder. Pads are very small, so this comes much later — but the heat-control habits transfer.

## Safety first

One temperature value governs the safety rules on this page: a working tip sits at about **350 °C**, roughly 170 °C above the melting point of leaded solder. An idle iron is safe, but contact with skin burns immediately.

<Callout type="danger" title="Hot tip, hot smoke, lead">
- **The tip runs at about 350 °C.** A touch burns instantly. Reach for the solder, never the metal.
- **Always return the iron to its stand**, even for three seconds. An iron left on the bench rolls off and drags its cable.
- **Wear eye protection.** Clipped leads can fly off at high speed, and hot solder can spit.
- **Ventilate.** Flux smoke is the irritant — the smoke, not the metal, makes your eyes water. Use a fume extractor, or a fan pulling fumes **away** from your face.
- **With leaded solder, wash your hands** before eating or touching your face, and never eat at the bench.
- **Unplug when you are done.** A stand is not a switch.
- **Keep a damp sponge or brass wool on the stand**, so cleaning the tip is never a reason to hold a hot iron.
- **Mind the cable.** A cable swept off the bench edge drags the hot iron with it.
</Callout>

Ten seconds of setup — glasses on, extractor on, stand in reach — prevents burns.

A good first iron is that temperature-controlled pencil iron: it reaches temperature in seconds, holds it, and shows you the number.

**Starting temperatures:** **≈ 320–350 °C** for leaded 63/37, **≈ 350–380 °C** for lead-free SAC305 — lead-free must pass 217 °C instead of 183 °C. Go hotter **only** if the joint will not wet within 2–3 s, and add flux before turning the dial: usually the fault is missing flux, not missing heat.

**Worked example — where those bands come from.** Soldering leaded wire at a 340 °C setpoint:

```text
Melting point of 63/37 leaded solder = 183 °C
Iron setpoint                        = 340 °C
Headroom above melting               = 340 − 183 = 157 °C
```

The pad and lead are heat sinks draining heat as fast as the tip supplies it, so the joint must sit well above its own melting point. Keep the same margin for lead-free:

```text
Melting point of SAC305          = 217 °C   (fully liquid at about 220 °C)
Extra heat needed over leaded    = 217 − 183 = 34 °C
Setpoint with the same headroom  = 340 + 34 = 374 °C
```

374 °C lands inside the recommended 350–380 °C band — **the melting point of your solder, plus enough headroom that the joint, not just the tip, reaches temperature.**

## Tinning and tip care

The working face of a tip is plated iron. At 350 °C, bare plating oxidises in seconds and turns matte black; black oxide will not take solder, so the bead balls up, heat stops flowing, and the beginner's instinct — turn the temperature up — only grows more oxide.

- **Wipe the hot tip** on brass wool (or a damp sponge) until the face is bright.
- **Tin it at once**: melt a thin bead of fresh solder on the face; that film is what the air attacks.
- **Tin before storing.** After the last joint of a session, wipe, tin generously, let it cool, then unplug.
- **A black tip can usually be recovered.** Clean it while hot on brass wool, add flux and fresh solder, and let it re-wet. Never file or sand it — once the thin plating is gone, the bare iron oxidises permanently.
- Never apply mechanical force with the tip; its function is to transfer heat.

## Making one good through-hole joint

One good joint takes about **2–4 seconds** from cold metal to finished fillet. The order of operations matters more than the iron you own.

1. **Clean and tin the tip** — brass wool, then a fresh bead. A bright tip transfers heat; a grey one does not.
2. **Heat both partners.** Touch the tip so it bridges the pad and the lead together, and hold for **1–2 s**. You are heating the joint, not the solder.
3. **Feed solder into the joint, on the far side from the tip**, so it melts because the *joint* is hot, not because the tip touches it. Feed until it wets the pad and climbs the lead into a small concave cone.
4. **Remove the solder, then the iron**, or the wire sticks as it cools.
5. **Hold still while it cools** (about 2 s); the solder is in a solid–liquid state for a moment, and moving it forms a disturbed joint.
6. **Trim the lead** flush with the cutters, end pointing down into a box. Offcuts fly — glasses on.

A small amount of solder on the tip before contact fills the microscopic air gap between tip and pad; heat conducts through metal much better than through air. That is a **thermal bridge**, and it determines whether the joint wets within 2 s.

**Worked example — the time budget.** A 40-pin header:

```text
Iron-on time per joint  = 2 s heat + 1 s feed + 1 s off = 4 s
Iron-on for the header  = 40 × 4 s = 160 s = 2 min 40 s
Cleaning and inspection  ≈ 6 s per joint
Total per joint          = 4 s + 6 s = 10 s
Whole board              = 40 × 10 s = 400 s = 6 min 40 s
```

So one breakout costs about seven minutes of bench time, only two and a half with the tip on the board. If your iron sits on a joint past 4 s, you are heating rather than soldering — stop, add flux.

## Recognising good and bad joints

Memorise this table; you will use it every time you put the iron down.

| Joint | Looks like | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Good | Shiny, smooth, a concave cone that wets the pad and climbs the lead | Right heat, order and flux | None — this is the target |
| Cold joint | Dull, grainy, lumpy; may crack later | Too little heat, or moved while cooling | Reheat 2–3 s with a little flux |
| Too much solder | A ball or blob hiding the lead | Kept feeding after it was full | Lift the excess with braid |
| Too little solder | Pad partly bare, no cone climbing the lead | Fed too little wire | Add solder and flux, reheat |
| Bridge | One sweep joins two pads or pins | Too much solder, or the tip dragged across | Drag away with a clean tip and flux, or braid |
| Lifted pad | The copper pad has peeled off the board | Overheating, or prying while hot | Wire the lead to the trace |
| Disturbed joint | Torn, smeared surface | Moved as the solder set | Reheat until it flows, then hold still |

## The header-pin exercise

The right practice task: headers are cheap, a breakout gives you a row of identical joints, and the meter provides an objective continuity result.

1. **Jig it in a breadboard.** Press the header straight into a row; the breadboard holds every pin upright and evenly spaced.
2. **Place the breakout on top** so each pin comes through its hole and the board sits flat against the spacer.
3. **Solder ONE pin.** Everything else stays loose on purpose.
4. **Check alignment** from two sides: flat against the board, square to its edges, pins straight?
5. **Reheat to straighten if needed**: reheat that joint, press the header flat, remove the iron, hold still about 2 s.
6. **Solder the rest**, now that the part cannot move.
7. **Inspect with a light and a magnifier** — a lamp at a low angle shows each fillet's shape, a phone macro shot shows cracks.
8. **Continuity-test it.** On the beeper, **each pin to its own pad or trace must beep**, and **adjacent pins to each other must not beep** — the first proves the joint conducts, the second that you joined nothing by accident.
9. **Do the whole thing three times.** The first board is for familiarisation, the second removes the hesitation, the third should reach a consistent quality.

No beep on a pin-to-pad test means a cold joint, a lifted pad or a forgotten pin; a beep between neighbours means a bridge — the next question.

## Wires and motor leads

Wire choice follows how the wire is used. **Solid-core wire** suits breadboards and plated through-holes, where it holds its shape and seats in a contact. **Stranded wire** suits every place that moves or flexes, such as the robot arm or a wheel module, because stranded wire is much more flexible than solid wire of the same size.

Wire gauge sets current capacity: thicker wire carries more current, so motor and battery leads are thicker than signal leads. The SparkFun [Working with Wire](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/working-with-wire/all) tutorial uses 22 AWG for prototyping.

Soldering a lead to a Month 2 gearmotor terminal tab:

1. **Strip about 6 mm (1/4 in)** with the stripper notch that matches the gauge. A nicked or cut strand weakens the wire; cut it off and strip again.
2. **Twist the strands and tin the end.**
3. **Slide heat-shrink onto the wire before soldering**, away from the joint.
4. **Heat the tab and the tinned wire together**, feed solder, and hold still while it sets.
5. **Slide the heat-shrink over the joint and shrink it.**
6. **Run a continuity test** from the wire end to the tab (The bench, tool budget & the multimeter), and check that no strand touches the neighbouring tab.

<Callout type="warn" title="Strain relief on stranded leads">
A solder joint at the end of a tinned stranded wire is rigid and can fracture under repeated bending and thermal cycling. Add strain relief, fixing the cable close to the joint, on anything that moves.
</Callout>

## Desoldering and rework

Removal is how you correct joints that are soldered incorrectly, so it is a core skill.

- **Braid (wick):** flux the joint, lay the wick on it, press the tip on top and hold. The solder melts, is drawn up into the weave, and you lift wick and iron together. Peel the wick away and check the hole.
- **Pump (solder sucker):** melt the joint, pull the iron away and trigger the pump in the same motion, so the nozzle catches the solder while it is still liquid. Two or three attempts are normal, not failure.
- **A multi-pin header:** cut through the plastic spacer with your cutters, then heat one pin at a time and pull it out with pliers. Heating one pin at a time avoids lifting pads under mechanical load.
- **Let the board cool between attempts.** Heat builds up, the adhesive under the pads softens, and repeated cycles are what finally release a pad from the fiberglass.
- **Excessive heat or mechanical force damages boards.** If a joint will not release its part in a couple of attempts, stop, let it cool, add flux, and come back.

## Inspection checklist and common mistakes

Hold each finished board to the light and check every joint:

- **Shape:** a concave cone, not a ball, not a crater.
- **Surface:** smooth and unbroken — shiny for leaded, satin for lead-free, never grainy.
- **Wetting:** solder climbs the lead and spreads to the pad's edge, so the lead's outline still reads through the fillet.
- **Coverage:** no bare copper left on the pad ring.
- **Separation:** nothing bridges to a neighbouring pad or pin.
- **Mechanical:** the pad lies flat, the lead is trimmed, and the joint does not move when you nudge it.
- **Electrical:** continuity from each pin to its own net beeps; adjacent pins do not.

Common mistakes that produce bad boards:

- Feeding solder onto the tip instead of into the joint, so the flux burns off before it reaches the pad.
- Cranking the temperature up to fix what flux would have fixed, and oxidising the tip instead.
- Moving the joint while it cools, which tears the surface.
- Pressing hard with the tip — heat transfers by contact area, not by force.
- Leaving the iron on a joint for ten seconds, which easily lifts the pad.
- Skipping the tinning routine, after which the tip oxidises and turns black.
- Trusting your eyes alone: inspect under a light and confirm with the meter before powering up.

## The thermal window: cold joints, lifted pads and reflow zones

The joint table above lists the cold joint and the lifted pad as two separate rows. They are really the two edges of **one thermal window**: the pad and lead must stay hot enough, long enough, for the solder to wet, but not so hot for so long that the adhesive under the copper gives up. This section puts time and temperature on that window. It is **not** another pass over the inspection quiz, and it is **not** the Wheatstone or Thévenin material from the other Month 1 lessons.

### A two-node heat model

Treat the tip and the joint (pad + lead + plated barrel) as two lumps. While the iron touches, heat flows through a contact conductance `G_c`; the joint loses heat to the board through `G_loss`:

<Formula label="Joint node">C_j × dTj/dt = G_c × (Tt − Tj) − G_loss × (Tj − T_amb)</Formula>

With the tip held at `Tt`, the joint settles at `Tj∞ = (G_c × Tt + G_loss × T_amb) / (G_c + G_loss)` with time constant `τ = C_j / (G_c + G_loss)`. Tip oxide multiplies `G_c` by `(1 − oxide)`; the tin bridge from the lesson is what keeps `G_c` high.

**Worked example — why oxide turns a 3 s joint cold.** A ground-plane pad with `C_j = 0.4 J/K`, `G_loss = 0.075 W/K`, tip held at 340 °C, 63/37 solder (liquidus 183 °C), wetting needs the joint above 183 + 20 = 203 °C:

```text
Clean tinned tip, G_c = 0.30 W/K:
  Tj∞ = (0.30 × 340 + 0.075 × 25) / 0.375 = 277.0 °C,  τ = 0.4 / 0.375 = 1.07 s
  time to 203 °C = −τ × ln(1 − 178 / 252) = 1.31 s   → a 1 s dwell never wets
Half-oxidised tip, G_c = 0.15 W/K:
  Tj∞ = (0.15 × 340 + 0.075 × 25) / 0.225 = 235.0 °C, τ = 0.4 / 0.225 = 1.78 s
  time to 203 °C = −τ × ln(1 − 178 / 210) = 3.34 s   → even the 3 s lesson dwell can stay cold
```

That is the same advice as the lesson, now with numbers: clean and tin the tip before you reach for the temperature dial. The toy also lets the tip sag when it touches a heavy pad (the station recovers at up to 60 W), which makes the heavy-pad case slightly worse than this hand calculation.

**Integration step and its stability criterion.** The toy steps both nodes with explicit Euler. For a linear system `dx/dt = −M x`, explicit Euler is stable only when `DT < 2 / λ_max(M)`. The stiffest joint in the toy (small pad, clean tip, the heater's proportional gain counted as a conductance) has `λ_max = 4.367 1/s`, so the limit is 0.458 s; the toy uses `DT = 1 ms` and asserts the bound. When the heater saturates at 60 W its gain drops out, which only lowers `λ_max`. The independent tests check that a free two-node system decays at 0.98× the limit and grows at 1.02×, and that halving `DT` changes the peak temperature by under 0.1 °C.

```python
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Synthetic thermal window of a hand-soldered through-hole joint. Stdlib only.

Lumped RC stand-in (two nodes, linear conductances, explicit Euler):
  tip   C_TIP dTt/dt = P - G_c (Tt - Tj) - G_AIR (Tt - T_AMB)
        P = clip(K_HEAT (T_set - Tt), 0, P_MAX)   (station regulates on the tip node)
  joint C_j   dTj/dt = G_c (Tt - Tj) - G_loss (Tj - T_AMB)   while the iron touches,
        C_j   dTj/dt = -G_loss (Tj - T_AMB)                  after it is lifted.
  G_c = G_CLEAN * (1 - oxide) * flux: tip oxide cuts contact conductance; the tin
  bridge / flux is a scalar. Latent heat of the solder, wetting chemistry and
  intermetallic growth are not modelled.

Cold joint: the joint spends less than its wetting time t_wet above T_liq + 20 C.
Pad lift: the damage dose, integral of max(0, Tj - T_DMG) dt, exceeds the pad's
adhesion budget D_crit. Both thresholds are toy stand-ins, not IPC criteria.

Arms differ ONLY in iron-on dwell: A_short 1 s, B_lesson 3 s, C_long 8 s.
Common random numbers: per (regime, seed, joint) the pad heat capacity, pad loss
to the board, tip oxide, t_wet and D_crit are drawn once and shared by all arms.

Stability: explicit Euler on dx/dt = -M x needs DT < 2 / lambda_max(M); the toy
prints lambda_max for the stiffest joint (heater gain counted as a conductance)
and asserts the bound. Labels: tie = identical on every seed; |paired mean| <= sd
= near-tie / small gap within seed noise; |m| > sd = consistent gap (small if
|m| < 20 % of the smaller arm mean, else clear), always with a sign count.
Real iron / reflow oven: 本页未运行. Wikipedia / SparkFun numbers: 以原文为准.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random

T_AMB = 25.0  # C
C_TIP = 1.2  # J/K, tip + heater lump
G_AIR = 0.01  # W/K, tip to air
K_HEAT = 4.0  # W/K, proportional heater gain on the tip node
P_MAX = 60.0  # W, heater limit (tip recovery)
G_CLEAN = 0.30  # W/K, clean tinned tip with a solder bridge
T_DMG = 260.0  # C, pad adhesive damage onset (toy stand-in)
DT = 0.001  # s, explicit Euler step
T_COOL = 6.0  # s simulated after the iron is lifted
SEEDS = (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)
JOINTS = 40
ARMS = (("A_short", 1.0), ("B_lesson", 3.0), ("C_long", 8.0))
# name, solder, T_liq, T_set, C_j range, G_loss range, oxide range, flux
REGIMES = (
    ("heavy_pad_underheat", "63/37", 183.0, 340.0, (0.30, 0.50), (0.05, 0.10), (0.0, 0.5), 1.0),
    ("small_pad_overheat", "SAC305", 217.0, 380.0, (0.10, 0.18), (0.02, 0.04), (0.0, 0.1), 1.0),
    ("matched_clean", "63/37", 183.0, 340.0, (0.12, 0.16), (0.02, 0.03), (0.0, 0.0), 1.0),
)


def shared_joint(regime: str, seed: int, j: int, spec: tuple) -> dict:
    """Common random numbers: one draw per (regime, seed, joint), shared by every arm."""
    _, _, _, _, cj, gl, ox, _ = spec
    rng = random.Random(f"{regime}|{seed}|{j}")
    return {
        "c_j": rng.uniform(*cj),
        "g_loss": rng.uniform(*gl),
        "oxide": rng.uniform(*ox),
        "t_wet": rng.uniform(0.3, 0.8),
        "d_crit": rng.uniform(300.0, 600.0),
    }


def lambda_max(c_j: float, g_c: float, g_loss: float) -> float:
    """Largest eigenvalue of M for the linearised two-node system (heater unsaturated)."""
    a = (g_c + G_AIR + K_HEAT) / C_TIP
    b = -g_c / C_TIP
    c = -g_c / c_j
    d = (g_c + g_loss) / c_j
    tr, det = a + d, a * d - b * c
    return tr / 2 + math.sqrt(max(tr * tr / 4 - det, 0.0))


def solder(dwell: float, t_liq: float, t_set: float, flux: float, jt: dict) -> dict:
    g_c = G_CLEAN * (1.0 - jt["oxide"]) * flux
    tt, tj = t_set, T_AMB
    above = dose = peak = 0.0
    n_on, n_tot = round(dwell / DT), round((dwell + T_COOL) / DT)
    for k in range(n_tot):
        touching = k < n_on
        q = g_c * (tt - tj) if touching else 0.0
        p = min(max(K_HEAT * (t_set - tt), 0.0), P_MAX)
        tt += DT * (p - q - G_AIR * (tt - T_AMB)) / C_TIP
        tj += DT * (q - jt["g_loss"] * (tj - T_AMB)) / jt["c_j"]
        if tj > t_liq + 20.0:
            above += DT
        dose += DT * max(0.0, tj - T_DMG)
        peak = max(peak, tj)
    return {"cold": above < jt["t_wet"], "lift": dose > jt["d_crit"], "dose": dose, "peak": peak}


def mean_sd(xs: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
    mu = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    return mu, math.sqrt(sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1))


def sign_note(diffs: list[float], m: float) -> str:
    """How many seeds disagree in sign with the paired mean (zeros listed apart)."""
    n, opp, zero = len(diffs), sum(1 for d in diffs if d * m < 0), sum(1 for d in diffs if d == 0)
    if opp == 0 and zero == 0:
        return f"same sign on all {n} seeds"
    note = f"{opp} of {n} seeds {'has' if opp == 1 else 'have'} the opposite sign"
    return note + (f", {zero} of {n} equal" if zero else "")


def fmt_pair(m: float, s: float) -> str:
    """Paired mean+-sd; extra decimals when 4 would print a signed zero such as -0.0000."""
    if m != 0.0 and abs(m) < 5e-5:
        return f"{m:.6f}+-{s:.6f}"
    return f"{m:.4f}+-{s:.4f}"


def verdict(xa: list[float], xb: list[float]) -> str:
    """Paired xa - xb per seed: mean+-sd, then the label and the sign count."""
    diffs = [a - b for a, b in zip(xa, xb)]
    if all(d == 0.0 for d in diffs):
        return "0.0000+-0.0000: tie: identical on every seed"
    m, s = mean_sd(diffs)
    if abs(m) <= s:
        label = "near-tie / small gap, within seed noise (|m| <= sd)"
    elif abs(m) < 0.2 * min(mean_sd(xa)[0], mean_sd(xb)[0]):
        label = "small but consistent gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    else:
        label = "clear gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: {label}; {sign_note(diffs, m)}"


def reflow_reading(name: str, pts: list[tuple[float, float]], t_liq: float) -> str:
    """Read a piecewise-linear oven profile (time s, temp C): the four zones."""
    def temp(t: float) -> float:
        for (t0, y0), (t1, y1) in zip(pts, pts[1:]):
            if t0 <= t <= t1:
                return y0 + (y1 - y0) * (t - t0) / (t1 - t0)
        return pts[-1][1]
    ts = [0.1 * i for i in range(int(pts[-1][0] / 0.1) + 1)]
    ys = [temp(t) for t in ts]
    ramp = max((y1 - y0) / 0.1 for y0, y1 in zip(ys, ys[1:]) if y0 < 150.0)
    soak = sum(0.1 for y in ys if 150.0 <= y < 200.0 and y < t_liq)
    tal = sum(0.1 for y in ys if y > t_liq)
    peak = max(ys)
    cool = -min((y1 - y0) / 0.1 for y0, y1 in zip(ys, ys[1:]))
    flags = []
    if ramp > 3.0:
        flags.append("ramp > 3 C/s")
    if not 60.0 <= soak <= 120.0:
        flags.append("soak outside 60-120 s")
    if tal < 30.0:
        flags.append("TAL < 30 s: cold/dull joint and void risk")
    if tal > 60.0:
        flags.append("TAL > 60 s: intermetallic / brittleness risk")
    if not 20.0 <= peak - t_liq <= 40.0:
        flags.append("peak not 20-40 C above liquidus")
    return (f"{name}: ramp {ramp:.2f} C/s, soak(150-200 C) {soak:.0f} s, TAL {tal:.0f} s, "
            f"peak {peak:.0f} C (+{peak - t_liq:.0f} over liquidus), cooling {cool:.2f} C/s -> "
            + ("; ".join(flags) if flags else "inside the guide windows"))
```

## Crimp or solder: gas-tight joints, wicking and strain relief

<Resource id="wiki-crimp" />

The wiring section above warns that a soldered, tinned stranded lead is rigid and can fracture under repeated bending, and it asks for strain relief on anything that moves. This section adds the other common way to terminate a stranded wire. Wikipedia's *Crimp (joining)* (body read, rev 1380146082; 以原文为准) describes an electrical crimp as a **solderless** connection: stripped stranded wire goes into the **correctly sized** opening of a connector, and a crimper squeezes that opening tightly against the wire. The article lists three benefits over soldering that matter here. A well-executed crimp is designed to be **gas-tight**, which keeps oxygen and moisture off the metals so they do not corrode. No alloy is used, so there is **no solder wicking** up the strands. It also calls the joint "mechanically stronger". That last claim is the article's, not something this page measured.

This section is **not** a redo of the thermal-window / cold-joint model above, and it does **not** claim that every crimp beats every solder joint. A crimp made with the wrong barrel or too little force is **not** gas-tight, and a soldered joint **with** strain relief is a good joint.

<Formula label="Strand bending strain and fatigue life">ε = d / (2 ρ);   N_i = N_REF × (EPS_REF / ε)^K_FAT × z_i;   strand intact iff N_i > cycles</Formula>

**Worked example — a labelled hand calculation, also printed by the toy.** A strand of diameter d = 0.16 mm bent to a radius ρ has outer-fibre strain ε = d / (2ρ). Solder that wicks into the strands makes a stiff section, and the bend gathers at its edge: with ρ = 3.0 mm, ε = 0.0267. The bellmouth of a good crimp spreads the bend over about ρ = 6.0 mm (ε = 0.0133), and a strain-relief clamp moves it to about ρ = 12.0 mm (ε = 0.0067). These radii are **declared toy parameters**, not measurements. With the toy's fatigue rule N = 2000 × (0.02 / ε)², the median strand life is 1125.0000, 4500.0000 and 18000.0000 cycles. Halving the strain quadruples the life. If only 3 of the 19 strands survive, the strand part of the joint's resistance is 19/3 = 6.3333 times its intact value.

**No integration step here.** Strand lives and contact resistance come from closed-form rules. Nothing is stepped in time.

```python
def shared_draws(regime, seed):
    """Common random numbers: one set of strands and one contact factor per (regime, seed)."""
    rng = random.Random(f"{regime}/{seed}")
    z = [math.exp(rng.gauss(0.0, SIGMA_LIFE)) for _ in range(N_STRANDS)]
    u = rng.uniform(U_LOW, U_HIGH)
    return z, u


def run_arm(arm, regime, seed, k_fat=K_FAT, rho=None, ox=OX_PER_KHOUR):
    cycles, hours = REGIMES[regime]
    z, u = shared_draws(regime, seed)
    r = RHO[arm] if rho is None else rho
    eps = D_STRAND / (2.0 * r)
    lives = sorted(N_REF * (EPS_REF / eps) ** k_fat * zi for zi in z)
    intact = sum(1 for n in lives if n > cycles)
    if arm == "C_crimp_under":
        growth = FRET_PER_KCYCLE * cycles / 1000.0 + ox * hours / 1000.0
    else:
        growth = 0.0
    contact = R_C0 * u * BASE[arm] * (1.0 + growth)
    return {"intact": intact, "life_half": lives[9],
            "contact_mohm": contact, "gas_tight": GAS[arm]}


def mean_sd(xs):
    if all(x == xs[0] for x in xs):
        return xs[0], 0.0  # identical values: no floating residue in mean or sd
    mu = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    return mu, math.sqrt(sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1))


def num(x):
    """Half-up rounding on the shortest decimal repr; tiny non-zero values in scientific form."""
    if x != 0.0 and abs(x) < 5e-4:
        return format(Decimal(repr(x)), ".3e")
    s = format(Decimal(repr(x)), ".4f")
    return "0.0000" if s == "-0.0000" else s


def sign_note(diffs, m):
    n, opp, zero = len(diffs), sum(1 for d in diffs if d * m < 0), sum(1 for d in diffs if d == 0)
    if opp == 0 and zero == 0:
        return f"same sign on all {n} seeds"
    if opp == 0:
        return f"no seed has the opposite sign, {zero} of {n} equal"
    note = f"{opp} of {n} seeds {'has' if opp == 1 else 'have'} the opposite sign"
    return note + (f", {zero} of {n} equal" if zero else "")


def fmt_pair(m, s):
    return f"{num(m)}+-{num(s)}"


def verdict(xa, xb):
    diffs = [a - b for a, b in zip(xa, xb)]
    if all(d == 0.0 for d in diffs):
        return "0.0000+-0.0000: tie: identical on every seed"
    m, s = mean_sd(diffs)
    if abs(m) < NUM_ZERO and s < NUM_ZERO:
        return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: numerically zero (floating-point residue), effectively a tie"
    if abs(m) <= s:
        label = "near-tie / small gap, within seed noise (|m| <= sd)"
    elif abs(m) < 0.2 * min(abs(mean_sd(xa)[0]), abs(mean_sd(xb)[0])):
        label = "small but consistent gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    else:
        label = "clear gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: {label}; {sign_note(diffs, m)}"
```
