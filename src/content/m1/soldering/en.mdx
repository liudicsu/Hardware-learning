## Why solder at all

A breadboard holds everything by friction: jumper pins bite into spring contacts, and a firm tug pulls a wire out. On a desk that is a feature; inside a robot it is a liability, because motors vibrate and vibration undoes friction fits. A half-seated jumper does not fail cleanly — it gives an **intermittent fault**: fine on the bench, dead on the floor, alive again when you pick it up.

Soldering replaces friction with a permanent joint. An alloy — 63/37 tin-lead, or lead-free SAC305 — is melted so it wets the pad and the lead, then solidifies into one piece with negligible resistance that cannot vibrate loose. **Solder what must not come loose.**

Two families of parts, and this lesson uses only the first:

- **Through-hole**: the lead passes through a hole and is soldered on the far side. The hole holds the part while you work, the joint is millimetres across and forgiving, and it can be desoldered later.
- **Surface-mount (SMD)**: the part sits on a flat pad, held only by its own solder. Pads are smaller than a grain of rice, so this comes much later — but the heat habits transfer.

## Safety first

One number governs this page: a working tip sits at about **350 °C**, roughly 170 °C above the melting point of leaded solder. It does not hurt you by sitting there; it hurts in the fraction of a second it takes to touch it.

<Callout type="danger" title="Hot tip, hot smoke, lead">
- **The tip runs at about 350 °C.** A touch burns instantly. Reach for the solder, never the metal.
- **Always return the iron to its stand**, even for three seconds. An iron left on the bench rolls off and drags its cable.
- **Wear eye protection.** Clipped leads shoot off the cutters like tiny bullets, and hot solder can spit.
- **Ventilate.** Flux smoke is the irritant — the smoke, not the metal, makes your eyes water. Use a fume extractor, or a fan pulling fumes **away** from your face.
- **With leaded solder, wash your hands** before eating or touching your face, and never eat at the bench.
- **Unplug when you are done.** A stand is not a switch.
- **Keep a damp sponge or brass wool on the stand**, so cleaning the tip is never a reason to hold a hot iron.
- **Mind the cable.** A cable swept off the bench edge drags the hot iron with it.
</Callout>

Ten seconds of setup — glasses on, extractor on, stand in reach — costs less than one burn.

A good first iron is that temperature-controlled pencil iron: it reaches temperature in seconds, holds it, and shows you the number.

**Starting temperatures:** **≈ 320–350 °C** for leaded 63/37, **≈ 350–380 °C** for lead-free SAC305 — lead-free must pass 217 °C instead of 183 °C. Go hotter **only** if the joint will not wet within 2–3 s, and add flux before turning the dial: usually the fault is missing flux, not missing heat.

**Worked example — where those bands come from.** Soldering leaded wire at a 340 °C setpoint:

```text
Melting point of 63/37 leaded solder = 183 °C
Iron setpoint                        = 340 °C
Headroom above melting               = 340 − 183 = 157 °C
```

The pad and lead are heat sinks draining heat as fast as the tip supplies it, so the joint must sit well above its own melting point. Keep the same margin for lead-free:

```text
Melting point of SAC305          = 217 °C   (fully liquid at about 220 °C)
Extra heat needed over leaded    = 217 − 183 = 34 °C
Setpoint with the same headroom  = 340 + 34 = 374 °C
```

374 °C lands inside the recommended 350–380 °C band — **the melting point of your solder, plus enough headroom that the joint, not just the tip, reaches temperature.**

## Tinning and tip care

The working face of a tip is plated iron. At 350 °C, bare plating oxidises in seconds and turns matte black; black oxide will not take solder, so the bead balls up, heat stops flowing, and the beginner's instinct — turn the temperature up — only grows more oxide.

- **Wipe the hot tip** on brass wool (or a damp sponge) until the face is bright.
- **Tin it at once**: melt a thin bead of fresh solder on the face; that film is what the air attacks.
- **Tin before storing.** After the last joint of a session, wipe, tin generously, let it cool, then unplug.
- **A black tip is not dead.** Clean it while hot on brass wool, add flux and fresh solder, and let it re-wet. Never file or sand it — once the thin plating is gone, the bare iron oxidises forever.
- Never use the tip as a poker; it delivers heat, not force.

## Making one good through-hole joint

One good joint takes about **2–4 seconds** from cold metal to finished fillet. The order of operations matters more than the iron you own.

1. **Clean and tin the tip** — brass wool, then a fresh bead. A bright tip transfers heat; a grey one does not.
2. **Heat both partners.** Touch the tip so it bridges the pad and the lead together, and hold for **1–2 s**. You are heating the joint, not the solder.
3. **Feed solder into the joint, on the far side from the tip**, so it melts because the *joint* is hot, not because the tip touches it. Feed until it wets the pad and climbs the lead into a small concave cone.
4. **Remove the solder, then the iron**, or the wire sticks as it cools.
5. **Hold still while it cools** (about 2 s); the surface is slush for a moment, and nudging it makes a disturbed joint.
6. **Trim the lead** flush with the cutters, end pointing down into a box. Offcuts fly — glasses on.

A little solder already on the tip before you touch the joint is the bench trick: the molten bead bridges the microscopic air gap between tip and pad, and heat crosses metal far better than air. That is a **thermal bridge** — the difference between wetting in 2 s and never starting.

**Worked example — the time budget.** A 40-pin header:

```text
Iron-on time per joint  = 2 s heat + 1 s feed + 1 s off = 4 s
Iron-on for the header  = 40 × 4 s = 160 s = 2 min 40 s
Cleaning and inspection  ≈ 6 s per joint
Total per joint          = 4 s + 6 s = 10 s
Whole board              = 40 × 10 s = 400 s = 6 min 40 s
```

So one breakout costs about seven minutes of bench time, only two and a half with the tip on the board. If your iron sits on a joint past 4 s, you are heating rather than soldering — stop, add flux.

## Recognising good and bad joints

Memorise this table; you will use it every time you put the iron down.

| Joint | Looks like | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Good | Shiny, smooth, a concave cone that wets the pad and climbs the lead | Right heat, order and flux | None — this is the target |
| Cold joint | Dull, grainy, lumpy; may crack later | Too little heat, or moved while cooling | Reheat 2–3 s with a little flux |
| Too much solder | A ball or blob hiding the lead | Kept feeding after it was full | Lift the excess with braid |
| Too little solder | Pad partly bare, no cone climbing the lead | Fed too little wire | Add solder and flux, reheat |
| Bridge | One sweep joins two pads or pins | Too much solder, or the tip dragged across | Drag away with a clean tip and flux, or braid |
| Lifted pad | The copper pad has peeled off the board | Overheating, or prying while hot | Wire the lead to the trace |
| Disturbed joint | Torn, smeared surface | Moved as the solder set | Reheat until it flows, then hold still |

Two caveats keep beginners from trusting their eyes. **Lead-free joints look slightly duller naturally** — judge a SAC305 joint by its smooth concave shape before its shine. And **flux fixes most of what beginners blame on the iron**: a grey, grainy joint wants a dab of flux and a second pass, not a hotter dial.

## The header-pin exercise

The right practice task: headers are cheap, a breakout gives you a row of identical joints, and the meter hands down a verdict instead of your doubtful eyes.

1. **Jig it in a breadboard.** Press the header straight into a row; the breadboard holds every pin upright and evenly spaced.
2. **Place the breakout on top** so each pin comes through its hole and the board sits flat against the spacer.
3. **Solder ONE pin.** Everything else stays loose on purpose.
4. **Check alignment** from two sides: flat against the board, square to its edges, pins straight?
5. **Reheat to straighten if needed**: reheat that joint, press the header flat, remove the iron, hold still about 2 s.
6. **Solder the rest**, now that the part cannot move.
7. **Inspect with a light and a magnifier** — a lamp at a low angle shows each fillet's shape, a phone macro shot shows cracks.
8. **Continuity-test it.** On the beeper, **each pin to its own pad or trace must beep**, and **adjacent pins to each other must not beep** — the first proves the joint conducts, the second that you joined nothing by accident.
9. **Do the whole thing three times.** The first board teaches your hands, the second removes the hesitation, the third starts to look like workmanship.

No beep on a pin-to-pad test means a cold joint, a lifted pad or a forgotten pin; a beep between neighbours means a bridge — the next question.

## Desoldering and rework

Removing solder is how you fix the joints you will inevitably get wrong, so it is not a side skill.

- **Braid (wick):** flux the joint, lay the wick on it, press the tip on top and hold. The solder melts, is drawn up into the weave, and you lift wick and iron together. Peel the wick away and check the hole.
- **Pump (solder sucker):** melt the joint, pull the iron away and trigger the pump in the same motion, so the nozzle catches the solder while it is still liquid. Two or three attempts are normal, not failure.
- **A multi-pin header:** cut through the plastic spacer with your cutters, then heat one pin at a time and pull it out with pliers. One at a time is the only way to avoid taking the board with you.
- **Let the board cool between attempts.** Heat builds up, the adhesive under the pads softens, and repeated cycles are what finally release a pad from the fiberglass.
- **Too much heat or force is how beginners destroy boards.** If a joint will not give up its part in a couple of attempts, stop, let it cool, add flux, and come back.

## Inspection checklist and common mistakes

Hold each finished board to the light and check every joint:

- **Shape:** a concave cone or volcano, not a ball, not a crater.
- **Surface:** smooth and unbroken — shiny for leaded, satin for lead-free, never grainy.
- **Wetting:** solder climbs the lead and spreads to the pad's edge, so the lead's outline still reads through the fillet.
- **Coverage:** no bare copper left on the pad ring.
- **Separation:** nothing bridges to a neighbouring pad or pin.
- **Mechanical:** the pad lies flat, the lead is trimmed, nothing moves when you nudge it.
- **Electrical:** continuity from each pin to its own net beeps; adjacent pins do not.

Mistakes that produce nearly every bad board:

- Feeding solder onto the tip instead of into the joint, so the flux burns off before it reaches the pad.
- Cranking the temperature up to fix what flux would have fixed, and oxidising the tip instead.
- Moving the joint while it cools, then wondering why the surface looks torn.
- Pressing hard with the tip — heat transfers by contact area, not by force.
- Leaving the iron parked on a joint for ten seconds: the fastest route to a lifted pad.
- Skipping the tinning routine, then blaming the iron when the tip goes black.
- Trusting your eyes alone: inspect under a light and confirm with the meter before powering up.
