## Trade speed for torque

A small electric motor is the opposite of what most robot joints need. It spins fast and produces very little <Term id="torque">torque</Term> at the shaft. A joint wants the other trade: slow motion and enough torque to hold the arm, lift a payload and survive a bump. A transmission is how you make that trade, and understanding <Term id="gear-ratio">gear reduction</Term>, <Term id="backlash">backlash</Term> and torque density is much of what separates a robot that works once in a video from one that works every time.

Three quantities describe what a joint does, and the rest of this lesson uses all three. Torque τ is a force multiplied by its distance from the axis, τ = F × r, in newton-metres (N·m): 10 N pushing at the end of a 0.1 m link is 1 N·m, and the same 1 N·m at 0.2 m pushes with only 5 N. <Term id="angular-velocity">Angular velocity</Term> ω is how fast the shaft turns, in radians per second (rad/s); motor datasheets usually give rpm, so convert with ω = rpm × 2π / 60, which makes 6000 rpm equal to 628.3 rad/s. Mechanical power in watts is the product of the two, P = τ × ω, with τ in N·m and ω in rad/s.

A reduction of n:1 means the input turns n times for one turn of the output. Ignoring friction, that multiplies torque by n and divides speed by n. <Term id="power">Power</Term> is not created, only traded: the same power passes through with more torque and less speed. Because P = τ × ω, if ω falls by n and τ rises by n, the product is unchanged. Real gears add friction, so the output torque is always a little less than the ideal n times the input, and the difference is what the efficiency number describes.

Three things are worth putting in a text file next to your design, because you will use them over and over:

```text
output torque = motor torque × n × efficiency
output speed = motor speed / n
stages multiply: ratios multiply, efficiencies multiply
```

The last line matters because almost no useful robot joint is a single gear pair. A two-stage gearbox with 5:1 in the first stage and 5:1 in the second gives 25:1 overall. Each stage also loses some torque to friction, so two stages at 90 % each leave 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81, not 0.9. This is the arithmetic behind a worked example you can check by hand:

```text
motor: 0.05 N·m at 6000 rpm
two 5:1 stages → n = 5 × 5 = 25
efficiency = 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81
output torque = 0.05 × 25 × 0.81 = 1.0125 ≈ 1.01 N·m
output speed = 6000 / 25 = 240 rpm
```

The motor gave 0.05 N·m at 6000 rpm; the gearbox output gives about 1.01 N·m at 240 rpm. Notice what did not happen: the gearbox did not create energy. It converted a fast, weak input into a slow, strong output, and friction ate a small part of the power along the way. If the efficiency of printed gears is worse than 90 %, the torque drops further; if it is better, the torque rises. The number 90 % per stage is a guess for printed gears, not a measurement. You should replace it with your own measurement once you print a reducer, and the practice section at the end of this lesson shows how.

### Check it with a power budget

The same example can be checked with power, which catches mistakes that the torque line alone hides. Convert both speeds to rad/s and multiply each by its torque:

```text
input:  ω = 6000 × 2π / 60 = 628.3 rad/s    P = 0.05 × 628.3   = 31.4 W
output: ω = 240 × 2π / 60  = 25.13 rad/s    P = 1.0125 × 25.13 = 25.4 W
ratio:  25.4 / 31.4 = 0.81
lost:   31.4 − 25.4 = 6.0 W, turned into heat in the teeth and bearings
```

The output power is 81 % of the input power, exactly the efficiency used in the torque line. That is the check: a gearbox can never deliver more power than it receives, so an output power above the input power means a units or ratio mistake somewhere. The 6.0 W is not an abstraction either. It heats the gear teeth, and PLA softens at 55–60 °C (Lesson 2), so a printed reducer that runs continuously at this load needs better efficiency, a way to shed the heat, or a material with a higher glass transition temperature.

Torque density is the reason robot arms are built this way. Torque density means newton-metres of output torque per kilogram of actuator, and a small high-speed motor with a good reduction beats a large slow motor on that measure. A big direct-drive motor large enough to hold an arm would weigh a lot and cost a lot; the same job done by a small motor and a gearbox is lighter, cheaper and easier to package. In Lesson 5 the SO-101 arm uses six Feetech STS3215 bus servos, and each one is small for the torque it produces because there is a gear train inside. The servo is not a powerful motor; it is a modest motor plus a ratio.

## Planetary and cycloidal

Two reducer geometries dominate small robot joints: planetary and cycloidal. They look different, but both solve the same problem, which is getting a large ratio out of a small package without relying on a single pair of tiny teeth.

### Planetary

A planetary stage has four parts. The sun gear sits in the middle. Several planet gears orbit around it. The ring gear is the toothed circle on the outside. The carrier is the plate that holds the planet gears and rotates with them. By choosing which part is held still and which part is the input and the output, you get different ratios; the common robot configuration is the ring fixed, the sun as input and the carrier as output. In that configuration the ratio is:

```text
ratio = 1 + ring / sun
```

It is worth seeing where the plus one comes from. First hold the carrier still: the gears become an ordinary train, and turning the ring once (backwards) turns the sun ring / sun times. Now turn the whole stage once forwards as a solid block: every part, sun included, gains one turn, and the ring is back where it started, so it has in effect been held still. Add the two motions: the carrier made one turn while the sun made 1 + ring / sun turns. That free extra turn is why a planetary gives a useful ratio in a very short package.

Tooth counts alone do not fix a gear's size; the <Term id="gear-module">module</Term> m does. The module is the pitch diameter divided by the number of teeth, in millimetres, so a gear with z teeth has a pitch diameter d = m × z. The pitch circle is the circle on which two meshing gears roll on each other without slipping. Two gears mesh only if they share the same module and the same pressure angle (20° for standard involute gears). A standard tooth reaches one module beyond the pitch circle, and the distance between the shafts of a meshing pair, the <Term id="centre-distance">centre distance</Term>, is the sum of the two pitch radii:

```text
pitch diameter       d = m × z
tip diameter         = (z + 2) × m
centre distance      a = m × (z1 + z2) / 2     (two external gears)
inside a ring gear   a = m × (z_ring − z_planet) / 2
```

With module 1 the stage used in this lesson is easy to lay out. The 12-tooth sun has a 12 mm pitch diameter and a 14 mm tip diameter; each 18-tooth planet has 18 mm and 20 mm; the 48-tooth ring has a 48 mm pitch diameter. The sun-to-planet centre distance is 1 × (12 + 18) / 2 = 15 mm, and the planet-to-ring centre distance is 1 × (48 − 18) / 2 = 15 mm. The two must be equal, because the planet's centre can only be in one place; setting them equal gives ring − planet = sun + planet, which is the first assembly rule below. The planet centres therefore lie on a circle of 30 mm diameter, Each planet reaches 15 + 20 / 2 = 25 mm from the centre, a 50 mm circle that is already larger than the ring's 48 mm pitch circle. With the standard dedendum of 1.25 modules, the ring's tooth roots lie on a circle of (48 + 2 × 1.25) × 1 = 50.5 mm, so the planet tips clear the roots by 0.25 mm, and the stage's outside diameter is that 50.5 mm root circle plus twice the ring's wall thickness.

Geometry imposes three assembly rules that you must satisfy on paper before you print anything. First, the planets must fill the gap between the sun and the ring, so the planet tooth count must be a whole number:

```text
planet = (ring − sun) / 2 must be a whole number of teeth
```

If ring minus sun is odd, the planets would need half a tooth and the stage will not mesh. Second, if you want N planets spaced equally around the sun, the sun and ring tooth counts must divide evenly by that number:

```text
(sun + ring) / N must be a whole number
```

If it is not, the planets cannot be spaced equally, and unequal spacing leaves the stage unbalanced, so it vibrates and loads the planets unevenly. Third, neighbouring planets must not touch. With module m, each planet centre sits at (sun + planet) × m / 2 from the sun's axis, so two neighbours of N planets are (sun + planet) × m × sin(180° / N) apart, centre to centre. A standard gear's tip diameter is (teeth + 2) × m, so the planets clear each other only if:

```text
(sun + planet) × sin(180° / N) > planet + 2
```

For sun 12 and ring 48 (planet 18), three planets give 30 × sin 60° = 25.98 > 20 and four give 30 × sin 45° = 21.21 > 20, but five give 30 × sin 36° = 17.63, less than 20: (12 + 48) / 5 = 12 passes the spacing rule, yet the five planets overlap. When all three rules are satisfied, several planets share the load, which is why a planetary is compact and strong for its diameter: the same torque passes through three or four sets of teeth at once instead of one.

### Cycloidal

A cycloidal reducer works by rolling instead of by ordinary meshing. An eccentric input shaft carries a lobed disc. The disc rolls around a ring of pins, and because the disc has one fewer lobe than the ring has pins, the disc steps backward by one lobe for each input revolution. With N pins and N − 1 lobes, the ratio is N − 1 : 1, and the output turns the opposite way to the input:

```text
ratio = N − 1 : 1, output turns the opposite way
```

The number of lobes that touch the pins at the same time is large, which is where the reputation of cycloidal drives comes from. They are stiff, they tolerate shock loads well, and they have low backlash because the load is spread across many contacts instead of sitting on one or two teeth. The cost is in the details: the eccentric mass must be balanced or the actuator will vibrate, and the output pins or holes must be accurate, or their looseness becomes play at the output. A cycloidal reducer is harder to design than a planetary, but for a compact, stiff joint it is often the better answer.

```python
"""gear_calc.py: the arithmetic behind a gearbox, before you print one.

1. Planetary stage (ring fixed, sun in, carrier out): ratio = 1 + ring / sun.
   It only assembles if planet = (ring - sun) / 2 is a whole number of teeth and
   (sun + ring) / planets is a whole number (equally spaced planets), and neighbouring
   planets do not touch: (sun + planet) x sin(180 deg / planets) > planet + 2.
2. Cycloidal stage with N ring pins and N - 1 lobes: ratio N - 1, output turns backwards.
3. What a ratio n does: torque x n (minus losses), speed / n, and what you feel when you
   turn the output by hand: friction x n and rotor inertia x n^2.
4. Backlash at the joint becomes a position error at the tip: arc = reach x angle.
"""
import math


def planetary(sun, ring, planets):
    gap = ring - sun
    if gap % 2:
        return f"sun {sun:2d}, ring {ring:2d}: no whole planet size ({gap / 2} teeth) -> will not mesh"
    planet = gap // 2
    if (sun + ring) % planets:
        return (f"sun {sun:2d}, ring {ring:2d}, planet {planet:2d}: {planets} planets cannot be spaced "
                f"evenly ((sun + ring) / {planets} = {(sun + ring) / planets:.2f})")
    spacing = (sun + planet) * math.sin(math.pi / planets)   # planet centre to centre, in modules
    if spacing <= planet + 2:                                # planet tip diameter, in modules
        return (f"sun {sun:2d}, ring {ring:2d}, planet {planet:2d}: {planets} planets collide "
                f"(centre spacing {spacing:.2f} <= tip diameter {planet + 2})")
    return f"sun {sun:2d}, ring {ring:2d}, planet {planet:2d}, {planets} planets: ratio {1 + ring / sun:.3f} : 1"


def cycloidal(pins):
    lobes = pins - 1
    return f"{pins} ring pins, {lobes} lobes: ratio {lobes / (pins - lobes):.0f} : 1, output turns the opposite way"


def stack(ratios, efficiency_per_stage=0.9):
    total = math.prod(ratios)
    eff = efficiency_per_stage ** len(ratios)
    return total, eff


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("-- planetary stages")
    print(planetary(12, 48, 3))
    print(planetary(12, 46, 3))
    print(planetary(13, 47, 3))
    print(planetary(14, 46, 4))
    print(planetary(12, 45, 3))

    print("-- cycloidal stages")
    print(cycloidal(10))
    print(cycloidal(16))

    print("-- two 5:1 planetary stages, 90 % per stage (a guess for printed gears; measure yours)")
    total, eff = stack([5, 5])
    motor_torque, motor_rpm = 0.05, 6000           # N*m and rpm of a small brushless motor
    print(f"ratio {total}:1, efficiency {eff * 100:.0f} %: output {motor_torque * total * eff:.2f} N*m "
          f"at {motor_rpm / total:.0f} rpm (motor: {motor_torque} N*m at {motor_rpm} rpm)")

    print("-- turning the output by hand (backdriving), relative to a 147:1 gearbox")
    for n in (147, 191, 345):                      # the STS3215 ratios in the SO-101 leader arm
        print(f"{n:3d}:1  friction felt x{n / 147:4.2f}   rotor inertia felt x{(n / 147) ** 2:4.2f}")

    print("-- backlash at the joint -> error at the tip of a 0.30 m arm")
    for deg in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
        print(f"{deg:3.1f} deg -> {0.30 * math.radians(deg) * 1000:4.1f} mm")
```

## Belts, gears or direct drive

Once you know the ratio you want, you still have to choose how to get it. Each option has a different balance of compactness, cost, backlash and backdrivability. A short comparison:

| Drive | Good at | Costs you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spur gear stage | Simple, cheap to print, any ratio in steps | Backlash at every mesh, noise |
| Planetary | High ratio in a small diameter, load shared by several planets | Backlash adds up over stages, printed planets wear |
| Cycloidal | Stiff, shock tolerant, low backlash | More parts, the eccentric must be balanced, accurate pins needed |
| Belt (for example GT2, 2 mm pitch: a 20-tooth pulley driving a 60-tooth one gives 3:1) | Almost no backlash, quiet, lets you put the motor far from the joint | The belt stretches (a compliant joint), needs tensioning, ratio per stage is limited by pulley size |
| Direct drive (motor on the joint, no reduction) | No backlash at all, perfectly backdrivable, the joint can feel force | Needs a big, heavy, expensive motor for the same torque |

A spur gear stage is the simplest thing you can print and the easiest to reason about. It is also the noisiest and the loosest, because every mesh contributes its own backlash, and a train of several spur pairs accumulates all of it at the output. Planetary stages pack a high ratio into a small diameter, at the cost of more meshes and planets that wear; printed planets are usually the first parts to show slop. Cycloidal drives are the stiff, low-backlash option, but they are the hardest to get right because the eccentric and the pins must be accurate.

Belts occupy a different point on the map. A GT2 belt with a 2 mm pitch, with a 20-tooth pulley driving a 60-tooth pulley, gives 3:1. A belt has almost no backlash, runs quietly, and lets you put the motor away from the joint, which is a gift when the joint is small and the motor is not. The cost is stretch: the belt is the elastic element in the drive train, storing torque while it is loaded and releasing it when the load changes, so the joint has some compliance, and it needs tensioning. The ratio per stage is also limited by how large a pulley you can fit, so a belt alone rarely gives the hundreds-to-one of a servo gearbox.

Direct drive is the cleanest answer and the most expensive. Mount the motor directly on the joint with no reduction at all and you get no backlash, perfect backdrivability, and a joint that can feel the force applied to it. The catch is torque density: to get the same torque as a small motor with a gearbox, a direct-drive motor must be physically large, heavy and costly. For a small educational arm, direct drive is usually out of reach for the shoulder and elbow, and often used only on a wrist where the load is small.

### Backdriving

Backdriving is what happens when you push the output of a gearbox by hand and the input turns. The faster you can push the output by hand, the safer and more teachable the arm is: you can move it out of the way, and you can guide it through a motion by hand while the controller records. Through an n:1 gearbox you feel two things the motor contributes, both amplified by the reduction. You feel the motor's friction multiplied by n, and you feel the motor rotor's inertia multiplied by n², because the rotor spins n times faster than the output. A high ratio buys torque and costs backdrivability, and the inertia penalty is the square, so it grows faster than the friction penalty.

Both factors follow from energy. <Term id="moment-of-inertia">Moment of inertia</Term> J is the rotational counterpart of mass, J = Σ m × r² in kg·m², so mass far from the axis counts with the square of its distance. Turn the output through a small angle θ and the motor shaft turns through n × θ. If the motor and the first gear stage have a friction torque T_f, overcoming it takes the work T_f × n × θ, and the work your hand does at the output is T_out × θ; setting the two equal gives T_out = n × T_f. For the inertia, a rotor of inertia J_r spinning at n × ω stores the kinetic energy ½ × J_r × (n × ω)² = ½ × (n² × J_r) × ω², so at the output it feels like a body of inertia n² × J_r turning at ω. A real gearbox adds its own losses on top: driven from the output side, the teeth also lose power to friction, with a backdriving efficiency η_b below 1, so the torque you have to apply is n × T_f / η_b. At η_b = 0.5 that is twice the ideal value, and a gearbox whose η_b falls to zero cannot be backdriven at all, which is the self-locking behaviour of a worm drive or a fine lead screw.

That is why the SO-101 leader arm, the one you move by hand in Lesson 5, uses STS3215 servos with lower ratios than the follower. The follower uses 1/345 everywhere. The leader uses 1/191 and 1/147 on several joints, and 1/345 only on the shoulder lift, which carries most of the arm's own weight, so the leader can hold itself up there. The last part of the program prints exactly this comparison against 1/147: 345:1 against 147:1 gives friction ×345 / 147 = ×2.35 and inertia ×2.35² = ×5.51. The 191:1 row shows the middle ground, ×1.30 friction and ×1.69 inertia.

## Backlash

Backlash is the free play between meshing teeth. Turn the input back and forth by a small amount and the output does not move at all: the teeth have to cross a gap before they touch on the other side. Every mesh in a train adds its share, and what matters is the total measured at the output, because that is what the arm feels. A small angle at the joint is a large distance at the tip, and the relationship is simple:

```text
arc = reach × angle in radians
```

A worked example makes the size of the problem obvious. One degree is π / 180 = 0.01745 radians, and a 0.30 m arm multiplies that into millimetres:

```text
1° = π / 180 = 0.01745 rad
arc = 0.30 m × 0.01745 = 0.00524 m = 5.2 mm
```

The last lines of the program show the pattern: 0.5° → 2.6 mm, 1° → 5.2 mm, 2° → 10.5 mm. Backlash that sounds small in degrees is millimetres at the tool, and millimetres are what decide whether a gripper closes on a part or a pen lands on a line.

Backlash creates two different errors depending on where the encoder sits. If the encoder is on the motor, before the gears, as in most hobby servos, the controller knows the motor angle and is therefore sure the arm is on target, while the arm can hang anywhere on the far side of the gap. The controller sees no error at all, so it does not correct, and the arm rests at an angle that depends on the load. If the encoder is on the arm, after the gears, the controller sees the error and tries to fix it, but it has to cross the dead zone to do so. With no load holding the teeth together, it overshoots, reverses, crosses the gap again and keeps hunting back and forth in a small limit cycle. Software cannot fully fix this, because inside the gap the motor has no grip on the arm at all: no control law can push on a load that is not connected to the actuator.

<CodeSource path="tools/sim/models/backlash_sim.py" />

```python
"""backlash_sim.py: what gear backlash does to a position loop, and why gravity hides it.

Toy model of one joint: motor -> gearbox -> arm. The gearbox has a dead zone (backlash)
of BACKLASH degrees at the output: inside it the motor turns without moving the arm.
A PID loop at 200 Hz moves the arm to a target. We read either the motor's encoder
(angle seen before the gears) or an encoder on the arm itself (after the gears).
"""
import math

DT = 0.005               # 200 Hz control loop
JM, BM = 0.002, 0.03     # motor side (referred to the output): inertia, friction
JA, BA = 0.004, 0.02     # arm side: inertia, friction
STIFF, DAMP = 40.0, 0.2  # gear teeth once in contact: N*m/rad, N*m*s/rad
GRAV = 0.4               # N*m of gravity with the arm horizontal (0 deg)
MAX_TORQUE = 1.0
STICTION = 0.03         # N*m of dry friction in the motor and first gear stage


def run(backlash_deg, sensor, target, preload=0.0, kp=0.1, ki=0.2, kd=0.004, seconds=4.0):
    """preload: N*m from a spring that always pulls the arm the same way."""
    half = math.radians(backlash_deg) / 2
    qm = wm = qa = wa = 0.0
    integral, prev, t = 0.0, None, 0.0
    tail = []
    while t < seconds:
        meas = math.degrees(qm if sensor == "motor" else qa)
        prev = meas if prev is None else prev
        error = target - meas
        unclipped = kp * error + ki * integral - kd * (meas - prev) / DT
        prev = meas
        torque = max(-MAX_TORQUE, min(MAX_TORQUE, unclipped))
        if torque == unclipped:
            integral += error * DT
        for _ in range(10):                                  # physics at 0.5 ms
            gap = qm - qa
            if abs(gap) > half:                              # teeth touching
                push = STIFF * (gap - math.copysign(half, gap)) + DAMP * (wm - wa)
            else:
                push = 0.0
            wm += (torque - BM * wm - STICTION * math.tanh(wm / 0.02) - push) / JM * 0.0005
            wa += (push - BA * wa - GRAV * math.cos(qa) - preload) / JA * 0.0005
            qm += wm * 0.0005
            qa += wa * 0.0005
        t += DT
        if t > seconds - 1.0:
            tail.append(math.degrees(qa))
    swing = max(tail) - min(tail)
    return f"arm at {math.degrees(qa):6.2f} deg (target {target}), swinging {swing:4.2f} deg peak to peak in the last second"


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for target in (20, 90):
        where = "gravity loads the teeth" if target == 20 else "arm straight up, no gravity load"
        print(f"target {target} deg ({where}):")
        print("  no backlash, arm encoder:       ", run(0.0, "arm", target))
        print("  2 deg backlash, motor encoder:  ", run(2.0, "motor", target))
        print("  2 deg backlash, arm encoder:    ", run(2.0, "arm", target))
    print("  ...same, plus a spring preload: ", run(2.0, "arm", 90, preload=0.1))
```

That 1.55° of error has two parts. Half the 2° gap is 1°, and the teeth themselves bend under the load: gravity torque is 0.4 × cos 20° = 0.376 N·m, and dividing by a contact stiffness of 40 N·m/rad gives 0.0094 rad, which is about 0.54°. ## Bearings and preload

A bearing lets a shaft turn while it carries load. A robot joint puts three kinds of load on its bearings. Radial load pushes sideways against the shaft. Axial load pushes along the shaft. A tipping moment tries to tilt the shaft out of line, and it is usually what breaks a joint that has only one bearing. A deep-groove ball bearing handles radial load and some axial load, and two common sizes are cheap and everywhere: the 608, which is 8 × 22 × 7 mm and is the skateboard bearing, and the 625, which is 5 × 16 × 5 mm.

One bearing lets the shaft tilt: it can pivot around the bearing, so the far end of the shaft wobbles. Two bearings spaced apart turn the tipping moment into two opposite radial forces, one at each bearing, and the farther apart the bearings are, the smaller those forces and the less the shaft wobbles. When you design a joint, space the bearings along the shaft as far as the geometry allows instead of stacking them next to each other.

The size of those forces follows from a moment balance. Suppose the shaft carries a sideways load F at a distance L beyond the nearer bearing, and the two bearings are d apart. Taking moments about the nearer bearing, the far bearing has to push back with F × L / d, and the nearer bearing carries that force plus F itself:

```text
far bearing   R_far  = F × L / d
near bearing  R_near = F × (L + d) / d
```

Take a 10 N sideways load, about the weight of 1 kg, acting 100 mm beyond the nearer bearing. With the bearings 10 mm apart, the far bearing carries 10 × 100 / 10 = 100 N and the near one 10 × 110 / 10 = 110 N. Space them 40 mm apart and the forces fall to 25 N and 35 N. The steel bearing is rarely the limit: SKF lists a basic static load rating of 1.37 kN and a basic dynamic load rating of 3.45 kN for the 608. The printed seat is. The 110 N load presses on a projected seat area of 22 × 7 = 154 mm² for a 608, about 0.7 MPa, far below PLA's yield strength of about 60 MPa, but a printed seat creeps under a sustained load and loosens over time, and the larger the force, the sooner that happens.

Preload is the second half of the bearing problem. Squeezing the bearings axially with a nut, a wave washer or a printed spring removes their internal play, exactly the same idea as the spring that removed backlash in the model above. Preload makes the shaft stiff and removes the small free movements that show up as wobble at the tool. Too much preload is a problem too: a large contact force between the rolling elements and the raceways raises friction and temperature, and the bearing wears out quickly, so the preload has to be sized against the bearing's rating. Angular-contact and tapered bearings are made to be preloaded; ordinary deep-groove bearings accept a light preload but are not designed for heavy axial loads.

The effect of preload is something to measure, not to assume, because it depends on your seat, your bearings and how hard you tighten. Clamp the housing, hang a known mass from the end of the shaft (1 kg gives 9.81 N), and read how far the shaft end moves, with a dial indicator if you have one or with a long pointer and the paper method from the practice section. The radial stiffness is the force divided by the deflection, k = F / δ, in N/mm. Measure once without preload, add the wave washer or tighten the nut, and measure again; the ratio of the two stiffnesses is what the preload bought you. The course does not quote a figure here because it has not measured one.

Printed bearing seats follow the same rules as the <Term id="fit">fits</Term> in Lesson 2. Use your press-fit clearance from the tolerance gauge, and remember that a bearing seat is usually a small negative or near-zero per-side number, not the sliding clearance you would use for a shaft that must turn. Add a chamfer to the entry so the bearing starts square, and never hammer a bearing in through its balls: press on the ring you are fitting, which is the outer ring for a seat in a housing and the inner ring for a shaft.

### Why servo gears fail first

In a cheap hobby servo the last gear stage carries the full output torque on small plastic teeth. That is already the hardest-working part of the gearbox, and a crash, a dropped arm or a joint stalled against a hard stop turns it into the part that takes the shock. A sudden stop puts the whole load on one or two teeth at the output stage, and plastic softens as the motor heats up, so the teeth get weaker just as the load peaks. That is why the gearset is usually the first thing to fail on an arm, long before the motor or the electronics. The STS3215 in the SO-101 already has copper gears (Feetech's specification, item 6-4, lists the gear material as copper), so the plastic-tooth failure described here mainly concerns cheap SG90-class micro servos.

The numbers show where the load sits. The force on a tooth is the torque divided by the pitch radius of the gear that carries it, F = τ / r. In an n:1 train each stage carries only the output torque divided by the ratios of the stages after it, so torque and tooth force grow stage by stage toward the output. The standard SO-101 follower servo, an STS3215 at 345:1, has a stall torque of 19.5 kg·cm at 7.4 V (Lesson 5; Feetech's specification, item 5-4), which is 19.5 × 0.0981 = 1.91 N·m at the output. Its rated load, the torque it is meant to carry continuously, is only 5 kg·cm (item 5-8), and the standard build runs it from 5 V, below the 7.4 V of that stall figure, so the numbers below are an upper bound. At the motor pinion that is 1.91 / 345 = 0.0055 N·m before losses, while the output gear carries the full 1.91 N·m. If the output gear's pitch radius is 10 mm (an assumed value; the gear sits inside the servo), the tooth force is 1.91 / 0.010 = 191 N, carried by one or two teeth at a time. That is the stall case at 7.4 V; at the 5 kg·cm rated load it is 5 × 0.0981 / 0.010 = 49 N. A crash adds the arm's momentum on top.

You can watch the result on a servo you own. Fix a long pointer to the horn, keep the servo powered and holding a fixed position, and rock the pointer gently both ways, marking the tip travel on paper as in the practice section. The reading combines gear backlash with the controller's position dead band, so its absolute value is not pure backlash, but its growth is: measure when the servo is new and again after a few weeks of use, and a rising number at the same pointer length is tooth wear.

Three things help. Metal-gear servos, such as the MG996R used in the EEZYbotARM, replace the plastic teeth with metal ones, whose strength and wear resistance are higher, so they fail far less often under the same load. The STS3215 already belongs to this group. Servos with a smarter controller that limits current reduce the load a stall can apply to the teeth. And keeping the arm short and light lowers the torque every joint has to carry, which lowers the load on the last stage. If you are choosing servos for a build, this is the reason the specification that matters is not only the torque number but also the gear material.

## Practice: a printed reducer

Your practice task for this lesson is to design and print a simple planetary or cycloidal reducer for a NEMA 17 stepper or a hobby motor. Expect the first one to be bad. That is not pessimism; it is how printed gearboxes work, because every tooth is a small part and small parts come out with the errors you measured in Lesson 2. Print it, measure its backlash by holding the input still and rocking the output, then redesign to reduce it. Reference geometries for both types are on Instructables and Hackaday; OpenCycloid, linked above, is a complete printed cycloidal actuator worth studying before you design one. Use gear_calc.py to choose tooth counts that assemble before you draw anything, so you do not spend an evening on a planetary with 16.5 teeth.

### Measure the backlash

To measure backlash, hold the input still and rock the output through its free travel. If the input is a stepper, power it so it holds; if it is a DC motor, clamp or lock the shaft. Fix a long pointer to the output, such as a printed arm or a ruler taped on, and let the tip sweep across a piece of paper. Rock the output gently both ways until it stops, and mark the two end points of the tip. The angle comes from the tip travel and the pointer length:

```text
pointer length 150 mm, marks 4.0 mm apart
angle = 4.0 / 150 = 0.0267 rad
angle in degrees = 0.0267 × 57.3 = 1.53°
```

Write that number in your print log next to the clearance and the material you used, because it is the measurement that tells you whether your redesign worked. A longer pointer gives a more accurate result: the same gearbox with a 300 mm pointer would move the tip twice as far, and the arithmetic divides the extra distance back out.

### Make it tighter

Redesign is a loop of one change at a time, and each change has a reason. Try these in small steps and record what each one does:

- Reduce the centre-distance clearance or the per-side clearance on the gear teeth in small steps, about 0.05 mm at a time, and change one thing per print. Reason: moving the gears closer together removes free play between the teeth, but going too tight adds friction and heat and the teeth may not turn at all.
- Add more planets, or use a cycloidal disc, so more contact points share the load and the play. Reason: the load is spread over several contacts instead of one, so each contact can be made tighter without jamming.
- Use herringbone teeth so the gears cannot slide apart along the shaft. Reason: the two opposing helixes cancel the axial force, keeping the teeth aligned and reducing play under load.
- Preload the output with a spring or a bearing nut. Reason: a constant force keeps the teeth on one flank, exactly as the preload did in the backlash model above.
- Print the gears in the material and orientation from Lesson 2, PETG or nylon, with the teeth lying in the layer plane. Reason: layer adhesion and wear resistance decide how long the teeth stay tight, and teeth that delaminate get loose quickly.
- Put the encoder on the output if you can. Lesson 4 uses an encoder on the wheel for the same reason: measuring after the gears measures the real angle instead of the motor angle, and the controller can then correct the error that backlash creates.

The editor has not printed one of these reducers or measured its backlash. Your first measurement is the result, and it belongs in your log.

<Callout type="tip" title="Run it in first">
Run the gearbox in for a few minutes under light load before you measure the backlash. New printed teeth wear in, the surfaces polish each other, and the backlash changes from the number you measured straight off the printer. Then measure, and write down the worn-in number.
</Callout>

Lesson 4 puts this to work. PID and cascade control are what make a joint follow a position or a trajectory, and the loop you will tune there has to live with the backlash you just measured. The cleaner your reducer, the easier that tuning becomes: reduction, backlash and torque density set the limits on how the control loop can be tuned.

**Key points**

- Output torque = motor torque × n × efficiency: 0.05 N·m at 6000 rpm through two 5:1 stages at 90 % each gives 0.05 × 25 × 0.81 = 1.01 N·m at 240 rpm.
- Power check: P = τ × ω with ω = rpm × 2π / 60; the example's 31.4 W in becomes 25.4 W out, 81 %, and the 6.0 W difference heats the teeth.
- Module m: pitch diameter = m × z and centre distance = m × (z1 + z2) / 2; with m = 1, sun 12, planet 18 and ring 48 give 15 mm both sun-to-planet and planet-to-ring.
- Planetary ratio = 1 + ring / sun, with planet = (ring − sun)/2 a whole number: sun 12 with ring 48 gives 5.000 : 1, while sun 12 with ring 45 needs 16.5 teeth and will not mesh.
- Cycloidal: N pins with N − 1 lobes gives N − 1 : 1 and the output turns the opposite way (10 pins → 9 : 1).
- arc = reach × angle in radians: 1° on a 0.30 m arm is 0.30 × 0.01745 = 5.2 mm at the tip.
- Backdriving: friction scales with n and rotor inertia with n², so 345:1 against 147:1 is ×2.35 friction and ×5.51 rotor inertia.

**Common mistakes**

- Multiplying efficiencies as if they were one stage: two stages at 90 % leave 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81, not 0.9.
- Drawing a planetary stage that fails the assembly rules, such as sun 12 with ring 45 (16.5 teeth), instead of checking planet size and spacing on paper first.
- Believing software can fix backlash: with the encoder after the gears and no load, the model hunts 1.16° peak to peak forever.
- Reading backlash in degrees and ignoring the arm: 2° on a 0.30 m arm is 10.5 mm at the tip.
