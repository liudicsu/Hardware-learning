## Why the SO-101

This is the capstone of Month 3: after four lessons of CAD, printing, gearboxes and control loops, the pieces become a robot. The SO-101 is an open-source robot arm from TheRobotStudio and Hugging Face, released under the Apache-2.0 licence. It has five <Term id="degrees-of-freedom">degrees of freedom</Term> plus a gripper, driven by six small serial servos, and it is designed as a <Term id="leader-follower">leader and follower pair</Term>: you move the leader by hand, and the follower copies the motion. The five degrees of freedom are the axes that position the tool: base pan, shoulder lift, elbow flex, and the two wrist motions. The gripper is the sixth motor, not a positioning axis; it holds what the arm reaches. The follower does the work, while the leader is the input device, mechanically similar but geared to be pushed by hand. The course's buying guide ranks it as the most worthwhile hardware purchase in the entire roadmap, and this lesson is where that ranking has to prove itself.

That copying is called <Term id="teleoperation">teleoperation</Term>, and it is how demonstrations are recorded, not a demo trick. Moving the leader by hand while the follower repeats the motion is how you record demonstrations: the leader gives the motion, the follower gives the robot's own joint readings, and the pair together produce the data that Month 6 is built on. The arm is the platform for Months 5 and 6, and Month 6 records its demonstrations with it. It is not a one-off project, so the time you spend on a clean build and a careful calibration is time you get back later.

A note on verification before the shopping list: the editor has not built or run an SO-101. The steps below follow the official LeRobot SO-101 documentation, v0.6.x, checked in October 2026. The command-line tools change between versions, so re-check the commands on that page before you run them; where this lesson and the current docs disagree, the docs win.

The official bill of materials in the SO-ARM100 repository comes to $229.88 for a leader and follower pair, or $121.94 for one follower, with printing not included. The printed parts are not a small afterthought: the repository asks for PLA+, 15 % infill, 0.2 mm layers and a 0.4 mm nozzle, which is exactly the kind of PLA+ job from the material table in Lesson 2 (3D printing: materials, calibration & tolerances). A full build is roughly 1 kg of filament, which matters when you compare routes in the next section. That figure is the course's **estimate**, not a number the repository publishes. An upper bound: the pinned plate STL files for the two arms' printed parts, Prusa_Follower_SO101.stl and Prusa_Leader_SO101.stl (the Bambu Lab A1 mini plates give the same volumes; the Ender follower plate gives 571.3 cm³), have solid volumes of 571.4 cm³ and 614.6 cm³, together 571.4 + 614.6 = 1186.0 cm³, which at PLA's 1.24 g/cm³ ([Prusament PLA data sheet](https://prusament.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/PLA_Prusament_TDS_2021_10_EN.pdf)) would be about 1.47 kg if every part were printed solid. At 15 % infill most of each part's inside is empty, so the real figure is well below that; your slicer reports it for your settings, and that is the number to use.

<Resource id="so-arm100" />

The repository and LeRobot use the same six joint names and motor IDs. Learn them now, because every command and every calibration step refers to them:

- **1 `shoulder_pan`**: turns the whole arm left and right around the base.
- **2 `shoulder_lift`**: raises and lowers the upper arm; it carries most of the arm's own weight.
- **3 `elbow_flex`**: bends the forearm at the elbow.
- **4 `wrist_flex`**: tilts the hand up and down.
- **5 `wrist_roll`**: rotates the hand around the forearm axis.
- **6 `gripper`**: opens and closes the two fingers.

The numbering runs from the base out to the tool, while the wiring runs the other way: the serial line <Term id="daisy-chain">daisy-chains</Term> from the gripper back to the base, and the shoulder_pan motor (ID 1) plugs into the controller board. Knowing this while you assemble explains why the setup script starts at the gripper and walks back to the base.

## Bus servos and packets

A hobby servo from Month 2 takes a PWM pulse and holds an angle. It has no name on the bus, no way to say "I am here", and no way to report what it is doing. The SO-101 uses six Feetech STS3215 <Term id="bus-servo">bus servos</Term> per arm, and each one is a small closed-loop actuator. It has an ID, a 12-bit magnetic encoder with 4096 steps per turn, and its own position loop inside the case. That loop is the same idea as the PID loops in Lesson 4 (PID & control: windup and cascade loops), except it runs in the servo rather than on your microcontroller: you send a goal, and the servo drives its motor until the encoder says it has arrived. That 4096 is the encoder's resolution, not the arm's accuracy: backlash, horn mounting and flex in the printed parts all add error after the encoder.

The six servos share one <Term id="half-duplex">half-duplex</Term> serial line, daisy-chained from motor to motor; LeRobot runs that line at 1 Mbit/s. Half-duplex means one pair of wires carries traffic in both directions, but not at the same time: while the controller drives the line to send, the receiving side cannot read data, so the controller switches to receive after each frame and waits for the answer. The ID in every packet addresses the one servo it is meant for, and the <Term id="checksum">checksum</Term> exists because one corrupted bit can turn a safe goal into a collision. Every servo hears every packet, and only the servo whose ID matches the packet answers. A servo can also be asked for its position, speed, load, voltage and temperature, which is why a bus servo can tell you it is straining or getting hot instead of silently stalling.

The servo comes in different gear ratios, and the two arms do not use the same set. The follower uses 1/345 on all six joints. The leader mixes ratios so that it holds its own weight where it must, yet moves easily by hand everywhere else:

| Joint | Leader ratio |
| --- | --- |
| shoulder_pan | 1/191 |
| shoulder_lift | 1/345 |
| elbow_flex | 1/191 |
| wrist_flex | 1/147 |
| wrist_roll | 1/147 |
| gripper | 1/147 |

This is the backdriving arithmetic from Lesson 3 (Transmissions & backlash: gears, belts and bearings) in hardware form. Through a reduction n, the friction you feel at the output scales with n, and the <Term id="moment-of-inertia">rotor inertia</Term> scales with n². Compare the wrist's 1/147 with the follower's 1/345: 345 / 147 = 2.35, so the follower's 345:1 joint feels about 2.35 times more friction, and its rotor inertia feels (345/147)² ≈ 5.51 times larger. The leader's wrist is therefore much lighter to push, while the shoulder_lift keeps the 1/345 ratio because that joint has to hold the arm up. The leader is not a weaker arm; its ratios are chosen for an arm moved by hand.

The three ratios are three Feetech part codes, all at $13.89 in the BOM: C001 is 1/345, C044 is 1/191 and C046 is 1/147. The leader bundle the README links is 3 × C046, 2 × C044 and 1 × C001, so the pair's 7 C001 servos are the follower's six plus the leader's shoulder_lift. The servos look the same from outside. Identify them by the part code the seller lists for each bag and keep each servo in its bag until it goes into its joint; the course has not checked what each servo's own label shows. If you check only one, check shoulder_lift: it carries the largest gravity torque, because it holds up everything beyond it, so it must be a C001 on both arms.

The servo's own specification also sets a floor on how precisely a joint can be placed. Two numbers matter: the encoder step and the gear backlash, the free play between the gear teeth. An angle error becomes a position error at the tool equal to the radius times the angle in radians:

```text
encoder resolution (C001 spec, 7-8):  360 / 4096 = 0.088 deg per step
gear backlash (C001 and C018 spec, 6-11):  <= 0.5 deg
at an example radius of 200 mm from the joint axis:
  one step:  200 x 0.088 x pi / 180 = 0.31 mm
  backlash:  200 x 0.5   x pi / 180 = 1.75 mm
```

The 200 mm radius is an example, not the SO-101's actual reach; use the distance from your joint to your tool. The backlash is the larger term by a factor of about 6, and it is an upper limit from the data sheet, not a measurement. Flex in the printed links adds to both and has to be measured on your own arm, for example by pushing the tool with a known force and reading how far it moves.

The wire protocol is simple enough to read by eye. Every message is a packet:

```text
0xFF 0xFF  ID  LENGTH  INSTRUCTION  PARAMS...  CHECKSUM
LENGTH = number of params + 2
CHECKSUM = ~(ID + LENGTH + INSTRUCTION + PARAMS) & 0xFF   (the low byte of the sum, inverted)
two-byte values are little-endian (low byte first)
```

The registers used in this lesson are from the STS control table LeRobot uses: Torque_Enable 40, Goal_Position 42 and Present_Position 56. Torque_Enable is one byte; the two position registers are two bytes each, which is where <Term id="little-endian">little-endian</Term> matters. The program below builds packets by hand and checks a reply. It is an ordinary Python program on your computer, not a simulation and not a servo: no hardware is connected, and it only prints bytes.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-3/so101-arm/sts_packet.py" />

```python
"""sts_packet.py: build and check Feetech STS3215 bus-servo packets by hand.

The SO-101 arm's six STS3215 servos share one serial wire (half duplex, 1 Mbit/s in LeRobot).
Every message is a packet:
    0xFF 0xFF  ID  LENGTH  INSTRUCTION  PARAMS...  CHECKSUM
LENGTH = number of params + 2, CHECKSUM = ~(ID + LENGTH + INSTRUCTION + PARAMS) & 0xFF.
A servo answers with  0xFF 0xFF ID LENGTH ERROR PARAMS... CHECKSUM.
Two-byte values are little-endian (low byte first). Register addresses are from the STS
control table used by LeRobot: Torque_Enable 40, Goal_Position 42, Present_Position 56.
No hardware needed: this only prints bytes.
"""
PING, READ, WRITE = 0x01, 0x02, 0x03
TORQUE_ENABLE, GOAL_POSITION, PRESENT_POSITION = 40, 42, 56
STEPS_PER_TURN = 4096                       # 12-bit magnetic encoder


def checksum(body):
    return ~sum(body) & 0xFF


def packet(servo_id, instruction, params=()):
    body = [servo_id, len(params) + 2, instruction, *params]
    return bytes([0xFF, 0xFF, *body, checksum(body)])


def hexs(data):
    return " ".join(f"{b:02X}" for b in data)


def parse_status(data):
    """Check a reply and return (id, error byte, params)."""
    if data[:2] != b"\xff\xff":
        raise ValueError("no 0xFF 0xFF header")
    servo_id, length, error = data[2], data[3], data[4]
    params = data[5:5 + length - 2]
    if checksum(data[2:5 + length - 2]) != data[5 + length - 2]:
        raise ValueError("bad checksum: the bytes were corrupted on the wire")
    return servo_id, error, params


if __name__ == "__main__":
    goal = 2048                                     # half a turn = the middle of the range
    print(f"{'ping servo 1:':32s}", hexs(packet(1, PING)))
    print(f"{'torque on, servo 1:':32s}", hexs(packet(1, WRITE, [TORQUE_ENABLE, 1])))
    print(f"{'goal position 2048, servo 1:':32s}", hexs(packet(1, WRITE, [GOAL_POSITION, goal & 0xFF, goal >> 8])))
    print(f"{'read present position, servo 6:':32s}", hexs(packet(6, READ, [PRESENT_POSITION, 2])))

    reply = bytes([0xFF, 0xFF, 0x06, 0x04, 0x00, 0x2C, 0x0C])   # a made-up answer from servo 6
    reply += bytes([checksum(reply[2:])])
    sid, err, params = parse_status(reply)
    steps = params[0] | params[1] << 8
    print(f"reply {hexs(reply)} -> servo {sid}, error {err}, position {steps} "
          f"= {steps * 360 / STEPS_PER_TURN:.1f} deg")
    broken = reply[:-2] + bytes([reply[-2] ^ 0x01]) + reply[-1:]  # flip one bit on the wire
    try:
        parse_status(broken)
    except ValueError as e:
        print(f"reply {hexs(broken)} -> {e}")
```

Real output:

```text
$ python3 sts_packet.py
ping servo 1:                    FF FF 01 02 01 FB
torque on, servo 1:              FF FF 01 04 03 28 01 CE
goal position 2048, servo 1:     FF FF 01 05 03 2A 00 08 C4
read present position, servo 6:  FF FF 06 04 02 38 02 B9
reply FF FF 06 04 00 2C 0C BD -> servo 6, error 0, position 3116 = 273.9 deg
reply FF FF 06 04 00 2C 0D BD -> bad checksum: the bytes were corrupted on the wire
```

<Diagram id="sts-packet-fields" />

Walk through the first four lines. The ping is addressed to servo 1, carries no parameters, so LENGTH is 2 (instruction plus checksum), and the checksum is ~(1 + 2 + 1) & 0xFF = 0xFB. The torque-on packet has two parameters, the register address 40 and the value 1, so LENGTH is 4 and the checksum is ~(1 + 4 + 3 + 40 + 1) & 0xFF = ~49 & 0xFF = 0xCE. The goal packet has three parameters, address 42 and the two bytes of 2048. The read packet asks servo 6 for two bytes starting at register 56.

Here is the goal-position checksum by hand:

```text
ID 01 + LENGTH 05 + WRITE 03 + address 2A + low byte 00 + high byte 08 = 0x3B (decimal 1 + 5 + 3 + 42 + 0 + 8 = 59)
invert: 0xFF − 0x3B = 0xC4 → matches the last byte
2048 = 0x0800 → low byte 00, high byte 08
reply params 2C 0C → 0x0C2C = 3116 steps → 3116 × 360 / 4096 = 273.9°
```

**Guided experiment.** This experiment shows how the STS3215 packet encodes ID, instruction and parameters, and how the checksum detects a corrupted byte.

1. Select the goal packet, set servo ID = 1 and goal position = 2048, then read the packet bytes = FF FF 01 05 03 2A 00 08 C4.
2. Set goal position = 1024 and read the packet bytes = FF FF 01 05 03 2A 00 04 C8 (0x0400 little-endian = 00 04; checksum = ~(sum) low byte).
3. Set servo ID = 2 and goal position = 2048, then read the packet bytes = FF FF 02 05 03 2A 00 08 C3.
4. Paste the reply FF FF 06 04 00 2C 0C BD into the reply box and read the parsed reply "servo 6, error 0, position 3116 = 273.9°".
5. Change 0C to 0D in the reply and read "bad checksum: the bytes were corrupted on the wire". Conclusion: checksum = 0xFF − (sum of ID…last parameter & 0xFF); one bit flipped makes it mismatch.

The reply line decodes the same way. Its LENGTH is 4, because it carries two data bytes plus the error byte and the checksum. The error byte is 0, so the servo reports no error. The parameters 2C 0C are little-endian: 0x0C2C = 3116, and 3116 / 4096 of a turn is 273.9°. The final line flips one bit in the high data byte, 0C becomes 0D, and the checksum catches it before anyone can mistake 0x0D2C for a real position. That is the point of a checksum: it does not repair the message, it refuses it.

Feetech's own Python SDK can cross-check the hand arithmetic. With the SDK installed (`pip install feetech-servo-sdk`), the next program writes the same four packets into a fake serial port, and compares the bytes with `sts_packet.py`. Again, no servo is connected; the fake port simply records what the SDK would have sent.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-3/so101-arm/compare_sdk.py" />

```python
"""compare_sdk.py: check sts_packet.py against Feetech's own Python SDK, no servo needed.

The SDK writes its packets to a fake serial port that just records the bytes.
Needs:  pip install feetech-servo-sdk   (tested with 1.0.0; it also installs pyserial)
"""
import scservo_sdk as scs

import sts_packet as mine


class FakePort:
    """Just enough of scservo_sdk.PortHandler for the SDK to send a packet."""
    is_using = False
    baudrate = 1_000_000

    def __init__(self):
        self.sent = b""

    def clearPort(self):
        pass

    def setPacketTimeout(self, length):
        pass

    def writePort(self, packet):
        self.sent = bytes(packet)
        return len(packet)


def sent_by_sdk(call):
    port = FakePort()
    call(scs.PacketHandler(0), port)          # 0 = STS byte order (little-endian)
    return port.sent


cases = [
    ("ping 1", lambda h, p: h.txPacket(p, [0, 0, 1, 2, scs.INST_PING, 0]), mine.packet(1, mine.PING)),
    ("torque on 1", lambda h, p: h.write1ByteTxOnly(p, 1, 40, 1), mine.packet(1, mine.WRITE, [40, 1])),
    ("goal 2048 on 1", lambda h, p: h.write2ByteTxOnly(p, 1, 42, 2048), mine.packet(1, mine.WRITE, [42, 0x00, 0x08])),
    ("read position 6", lambda h, p: h.readTx(p, 6, 56, 2), mine.packet(6, mine.READ, [56, 2])),
]
for name, call, expected in cases:
    got = sent_by_sdk(call)
    print(f"{name:16s} SDK {mine.hexs(got):27s} {'same' if got == expected else 'DIFFERENT'}")
```

Real output:

```text
$ pip install feetech-servo-sdk
$ python3 compare_sdk.py
ping 1           SDK FF FF 01 02 01 FB           same
torque on 1      SDK FF FF 01 04 03 28 01 CE     same
goal 2048 on 1   SDK FF FF 01 05 03 2A 00 08 C4  same
read position 6  SDK FF FF 06 04 02 38 02 B9     same
```

Every line says `same`. Feetech's SDK, writing into the fake port, produces exactly the same bytes as the hand-built packets, which is a good sign that both the length field and the checksum are right. LeRobot uses this SDK underneath, so the packets you send through LeRobot have the same shape. You will never need to build packets by hand to use the arm, but when a servo stops answering, knowing what should be on the wire is how you decide whether the problem is power, wiring, ID or corruption.

## Power

Power is the step where a wrong choice damages hardware, so the supply voltage has to match the servo version. The standard SO-101 build uses a 5 V supply (source A), and 5 V lies inside the 4 V–7.4 V input range that Feetech specifies for the 7.4 V STS3215 (source B). At 5 V the torque is slightly below the 16.5 kg·cm quoted at 6 V (source A). The optional 12 V follower servos need a 12 V supply of at least 5 A instead of the 5 V one (source A), and the leader arm is always built from 7.4 V servos (source A).

| Arm / servo version | Feetech model | Input voltage range (Feetech) | Supply per SO-ARM100 README | Stall torque (Feetech) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Follower, standard 7.4 V | ST-3215-C001 | 4–7.4 V | 5 V | 16.5 kg·cm @ 6 V, 19.5 kg·cm @ 7.4 V |
| Follower, optional 12 V | ST-3215-C018 | 4–14 V | 12 V, ≥ 5 A | 30 kg·cm @ 12 V |
| Leader (always 7.4 V) | 7.4 V STS3215 (C001/C044/C046 per the README BOM) | 4–7.4 V (C001 spec) | 5 V | — |

<Callout type="danger" title="A 12 V supply is only for 12 V servos">
Never connect a 12 V supply to an arm with 7.4 V servos; the leader is always such an arm. 12 V is far outside the 4–7.4 V input range in Feetech's specification for the 7.4 V version. Before plugging in, compare the voltage on the servo label with the rating plate of the supply; keep 7.4 V and 12 V servos on separate arms and separate supplies, because all servos of one arm share the same supply through the daisy chain.
</Callout>

Sources: A — [SO-ARM100 README · Sourcing Parts (IMPORTANT note)](https://github.com/TheRobotStudio/SO-ARM100/blob/a758567c3978dfeefe282ede0500085a48fe8f78/README.md#L72-L73); B — [Feetech STS3215 (C001) specification, §5](https://www.feetechrc.com/Data/feetechrc/upload/file/20200611/6372749961523760249976542.pdf); C — [Feetech ST-3215-C018 product page](https://www.feetechrc.com/en/525603.html) and [specification, §5](https://www.feetechrc.com/Data/feetechrc/upload/file/20240507/6385067068652648096680943.pdf).

The supply also has to deliver enough current. Add up what six servos draw from their specifications:

```text
12 V follower, six ST-3215-C018 (C018 spec, 5-9 and 5-5):
  rated   6 x 0.9 A = 5.4 A
  stall   6 x 2.7 A = 16.2 A
5 V follower, six ST-3215-C001 (C001 spec, 5-9 and 5-5, both given at 6 V):
  rated   6 x 0.5 A = 3.0 A
  stall   6 x 2.0 A = 12.0 A
```

The README asks for at least 5 A at 12 V; six C018 servos at their rated current already draw 5.4 A, so choose a 12 V supply rated above 5.4 A. The stall figure is the worst case, every joint blocked at once, and it does not last: both specifications say that a servo whose running current stays above 2 A for 2 s switches its output off and enters overcurrent protection (§7-11). For the standard arm, Feetech gives the C001's rated current at 6 V, not at 5 V, so the 3.0 A total is an estimate; it sits inside the 4 A rating of the 5 V supply the BOM links (Facmogu AL-0540, 5 V 4 A). The stall case does not fit. The C001 stalls at 2 A at 6 V and 2.5 A at 7.4 V (§5-5), so six blocked joints draw about 6 × 2 A = 12 A, an estimate from the 6 V figure and three times the AL-0540's 4 A. Nor can you count on the overcurrent protection here: at 5 V each C001 should stall at ≤ 5 V / 2.5 Ω ≈ 2.0 A (estimated from §5-10, the 2.5 Ω terminal resistance; Feetech gives no 5 V figure). As an upper bound this agrees with the specification: at 6 V the 2 A stall current of §5-5 is below 6 V / 2.5 Ω = 2.4 A. So the "above 2 A for 2 s" condition may never be met. The item that does apply is the Over Load protection next to it in the same table: a servo stalled at more than 80 % of its stall value for 2 s enters protection (§7-11). Both thresholds, 80 % for 2 s and 2 A for 2 s, are factory defaults, but two Feetech documents disagree on the current: the C001 specification gives 2 A for 2 s (§7-11), while Feetech's STS3215 memory table ([STS3215 Memory Table.xlsx, V3.6](https://github.com/matthieuvigne/STS_servos/blob/4d2278432a2b12027d04d0449e1e6f97df812d68/extras/STS3215%20Memory%20Table.xlsx)) gives Protection_Current a default of 500 in units of 6.5 mA, 500 × 6.5 mA = 3.25 A; to see which value your servo holds, read register 28 yourself. The overload side agrees: in the memory table Overload_Torque (address 36, 1 % units) defaults to 80 and the protection time (address 35, 10 ms units) to 200 × 10 ms = 2 s. The gripper is the exception: when LeRobot configures an SO follower it writes Max_Torque_Limit 500, Protection_Current 250 and Overload_Torque 25 to the gripper servo only ([so_follower.py, lines 169–171, LeRobot v0.6.1](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/v0.6.1/src/lerobot/robots/so_follower/so_follower.py#L169-L171)). In the memory table, Max_Torque_Limit (address 16) is in units of 0.1 %, so 500 is 50 % of the maximum torque; Protection_Current (address 28) is in units of 6.5 mA, so 250 is 250 × 6.5 mA = 1.625 A; and Overload_Torque (address 36) is the load that starts the overload timer, so once the gripper's load exceeds 25 % of its maximum torque for 2 s it enters protection. The torque it outputs after that is set by a different register, Protective_Torque (address 34, default 20 %), which LeRobot does not write; LeRobot's code comment calls Overload_Torque the torque when overloaded, which disagrees with Feetech's table. The gripper's 1.625 A limit is below the ≈ 2.0 A stall estimate at 5 V, so, unlike the other joints, a stalled gripper reaches its overcurrent threshold first and that protection trips. The memory table is for the STS3215 in general rather than the C001 variant, and Feetech's generic [online STS memory table](https://ftservo.github.io/ftservo-wiki/en/reference/parameter/memory-sts/) uses the same addresses and units but lists a Protection_Current default of 511. LeRobot does write other registers on every joint: Operating_Mode (position), the P/I/D coefficients 16/0/32 (the factory P is 32; [config_so_follower.py, lines 45–47](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/v0.6.1/src/lerobot/robots/so_follower/config_so_follower.py#L45-L47)), Return_Delay_Time 0 and the acceleration registers 254 ([feetech.py, lines 209–217](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/v0.6.1/src/lerobot/motors/feetech/feetech.py#L209-L217)), and, at calibration, Homing_Offset and the position limits ([feetech.py, lines 268–273](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/v0.6.1/src/lerobot/motors/feetech/feetech.py#L268-L273)). But the other five joints keep the factory protection thresholds (registers 16, 28 and 36 unchanged), and the 12 A stall total stays a conservative upper bound.

## Assemble and set IDs

The build has two halves: the software has to be installed and able to see the boards, and the motors have to be given IDs before any of it makes sense. Do the software first, because a motor with the wrong ID is much easier to diagnose when you know the port and the tools already work.

### Install LeRobot

Follow the LeRobot installation guide and put it in a separate Python environment. LeRobot pulls in a large stack of packages, and mixing it with your system Python is a good way to break something else. Once the repository is cloned and the environment is active, install the Feetech extra from inside the cloned folder:

```bash
pip install -e ".[feetech]"
```

The `-e` makes the install editable, so the commands come from your checkout; the `[feetech]` extra installs the serial SDK that talks to the STS3215. If the command fails, check that you are in the folder containing `pyproject.toml`, and re-check the installation page for the current Python version and any system packages.

<Resource id="lerobot-so101" />

### Find the ports

Each arm has its own controller board and its own USB port. Run:

```bash
lerobot-find-port
```

The tool lists the serial ports, asks you to unplug one board and press Enter, and then tells you which port disappeared. Plug it back in and repeat for the other board. Write down which port belongs to the leader and which to the follower, on paper or in a note next to the command you will use. On Linux the names look like `/dev/ttyACM0` and `/dev/ttyACM1`, but they can change when you replug, so check rather than assume. The examples below use `/dev/ttyACM0` for the follower and `/dev/ttyACM1` for the leader; replace them with your own.

### One motor at a time

New servos all come with ID 1. If you connect two of them at once, both will answer to the same packets, and the setup script cannot tell them apart. That is why the official procedure connects exactly one motor at a time. The ID and the bus baud rate are written to the servo's <Term id="eeprom">EEPROM</Term> once, and they stay there until you change them. A replacement motor arrives as ID 1 again, so keep a note of which servo sits at which joint.

```bash
lerobot-setup-motors \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=/dev/ttyACM0
```

The script asks for the gripper motor first and sets it to ID 6, then wrist_roll, wrist_flex, elbow_flex, shoulder_lift and finally shoulder_pan = 1. Connect ONLY the motor it asks for to the board each time, not daisy-chained to another motor. Before every Enter, check the power and the 3-pin cable; a loose cable at this stage looks exactly like a dead servo. Then do the same for the leader:

```bash
lerobot-setup-motors \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=/dev/ttyACM1
```

Keep the leader's differently geared servos apart from the follower's before you start, and label them. The two sets look identical from the outside, and putting a 1/147 servo in the follower's shoulder is an easy mistake: the arm will move, but it will not have the torque the follower was designed for.

### Assembly notes

The official guide's details are worth following literally:

- Remove all support material first; a small screwdriver under the supports is the tool the guide suggests. A leftover blob can stop a joint from seating.
- Fit one 3-pin cable into each motor as you place it, before the next part covers the socket. Once the parts are together, the socket is hard to reach.
- Most joints use two motor horns; the top one takes an M3×6 screw, the bottom one does not (the wrist roll motor gets only one horn).
- Motors are held with M2×6 screws, the smallest in the kit. Printed parts attach to the horns with M3×6 screws.
- Daisy-chain from the gripper back to the base, and plug shoulder_pan (ID 1) into the board.

Keep the two screw sizes apart and bag the printed parts by joint; M2×6 and M3×6 look similar in a pile. Two practical tips. Do not overtighten screws into printed plastic; the threads strip long before the screw feels tight, and a stripped boss cannot be fixed with more force. And turn each joint by hand after mounting it. If it binds, find out why before you go on: a cable trapped under a horn or a support fragment inside a bearing seat will not fix itself, and it will make calibration and teleoperation feel like a software problem when it is mechanical.

The horn is the toothed disc that fits on the servo's output shaft; the printed link is screwed to it. Feetech specifies the output shaft as 25T/OD5.9mm, a 25-tooth spline 5.9 mm across, and the horn screw as M3×6 (C001 and C018 specifications, §6-9 and §6-13). The second horn, on the far side of the servo, gives the link a second support so it is not held by the output shaft alone; the assembly guide notes that "No screws are required for the bottom horn" ([LeRobot SO-101 guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/so101)). The M2×6 screws hold the servo itself in its printed holder. Because the spline has 25 teeth, a horn can only be refitted in steps of 360° / 25 = 14.4°, which is 14.4 × 4096 / 360 = 163.84 encoder steps. Calibration records each joint's range with the horns as they were fitted, so if you take a horn off and put it back, even one tooth off, calibrate that arm again.

## Calibrate and teleoperate

Calibration makes the leader and follower report the same numbers in the same physical pose. The servos are mechanically identical in type, but the horns, the printed parts and the encoder zero are not; a raw count of 1830 on one arm can mean a different angle than 1830 on another. Calibration records the middle and the ends of each joint's travel and builds the mapping from raw counts to joint angles. Do it for both arms, and use the same saved name every time you start that arm.

This is also what lets a policy trained on one arm run on another, which is the whole point of Month 6. A demonstration recorded with one calibration is only comparable to another if both arms agree on what "middle" and "end" mean. Calibration is not a checkbox before the fun part; it is the coordinate system the fun part runs in.

Calibrate the follower:

```bash
lerobot-calibrate \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=/dev/ttyACM0 \
    --robot.id=my_follower
```

Then the leader:

```bash
lerobot-calibrate \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=/dev/ttyACM1 \
    --teleop.id=my_leader
```

The procedure is the same for both. Move the arm so every joint is in the middle of its range, then press Enter. Then move every joint slowly through its full range, so the tool sees both ends. Do not force a joint against its mechanical stop; reach the end of travel, then stop. The `id` is the name the calibration is saved under; it is not the motor ID, and it is not a serial number. Use the same name every time you start this arm, otherwise a different calibration file is loaded and the joint zero offsets no longer match the assembly.

With both arms calibrated, teleoperation is one command:

```bash
lerobot-teleoperate \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=/dev/ttyACM0 \
    --robot.id=my_follower \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=/dev/ttyACM1 \
    --teleop.id=my_leader
```

Move the leader slowly at first, and keep a hand near the follower's power switch. Clear the table around the arm before you start: the follower will copy whatever you do, including a fast move into a cup. The first minute is for checking directions and range, not for judging how well it tracks.

When it misbehaves, work from the outside in:

- **A motor is not found**: check power, USB and the 3-pin cable at that motor. The Waveshare board needs its two jumpers on the B (USB) channel; on the wrong channel the board is powered but the computer sees nothing.
- **The follower jumps on start**: it was calibrated in a different pose or under another id. Re-run calibration with the pose and the id you intend to use.
- **A joint moves the wrong way or is offset**: recalibrate that arm. Do not fix it by editing the command; the mapping is wrong, not the command.
- **A servo gets hot or the gripper buzzes when closed on an object**: it is pushing against something. The position loop keeps a steady error and keeps applying torque, exactly the steady-state behaviour from Lesson 4. Release the grip or lower the goal; do not let it buzz for minutes.

## Practice: build, calibrate, film

Your practice task for this lesson is the build itself. Assemble the SO-101, set IDs 1–6 on both arms, calibrate every servo, and teleoperate the follower with the leader. Then make a short video of the follower mirroring the leader. In the next lesson you will design and print your own TPU gripper fingers and test them on three differently shaped objects; the video and the calibration are what make that comparison meaningful.

A checklist, in the order the work happens:

- Route chosen, and the total cost written down before ordering.
- Parts printed with the repository's settings: PLA+, 15 % infill, 0.2 mm layers, 0.4 mm nozzle.
- IDs 1–6 set on both arms, with the leader's geared servos kept separate.
- Both arms calibrated, with the saved ids written next to the commands.
- A short video of the follower mirroring the leader, including one slow move and one move that uses the wrist.
- Notes on anything that went wrong and how you fixed it. The notes are the part you will reread when the next build misbehaves.

Film the whole arm and the table, not just the gripper: later you need to see whether a failure was the grasp or the path. If one joint lags in the video, check the mechanics and the calibration before blaming the software.

If the full pair is out of reach, the cheaper paths still teach most of the lesson. With a single follower you keep the whole software path: you can set it up, calibrate it, read its joints and command it from Python, and you can add a leader later. With MuJoCo you can run the same software before any hardware exists; what you miss is the hands, the cables and the sound of a servo straining. Neither shortcut is wasted time, as long as you record which part of the skill each path has practised.

<Callout type="warn" title="The follower is not a toy">
The follower is strong enough to pinch fingers and knock things off a desk. Keep the workspace clear, keep cables and cups out of the arm's reach, and power it off when you work on it. A moving arm does not know where your hand is.
</Callout>

Lesson 6 takes the next step: gripper fingers. They are small, they are cheap to iterate, and they are the part that proves you can design as well as assemble. A working arm with a gripper you designed for it is a much better answer to "can you build a robot?" than a kit that only runs the manufacturer's demo.

<Summary>

**Key points**

- Six STS3215 bus servos per arm, each with a 12-bit encoder of 4096 steps per turn: 1024 steps is 90°.
- Every packet is `0xFF 0xFF ID LENGTH INSTRUCTION PARAMS CHECKSUM` with CHECKSUM = ~(sum) & 0xFF; a ping to servo 1 is FF FF 01 02 01 FB, and to servo 2 the checksum is FA.
- The half-duplex bus runs at 1 Mbit/s, daisy-chained from the gripper back to the base, and the setup script gives the gripper ID 6 first, then walks back to shoulder_pan = 1.
- Ratios: the follower is 1/345 on all six joints while the leader mixes 1/191, 1/147 and 1/345, so 345 / 147 = 2.35 times the friction and (345/147)² ≈ 5.51 times the rotor inertia.
- Power and cost: the standard build uses 5 V inside the 4–7.4 V range, the BOM pair costs $229.88 (one follower $121.94), and the prints are an estimated 1 kg of PLA+ at 15 % infill and 0.2 mm layers (read the real figure from your slicer).

**Common mistakes**

- Connecting two new servos at once: both ship as ID 1, so the setup script cannot tell them apart.
- Mixing the leader's 1/147 servos into the follower, which moves but lacks the torque the follower was designed with.
- Connecting a 12 V supply to 7.4 V servos, far outside the 4–7.4 V input range.
- Editing the teleoperation command when a joint is offset or reversed; the mapping is wrong, so recalibrate that arm instead.

**Self-check**

- Can you work out the checksum of a ping to servo 2?
- Can you convert a reported position of 1024 steps into degrees?
- Can you say why each new servo must be connected alone while you set its ID?
