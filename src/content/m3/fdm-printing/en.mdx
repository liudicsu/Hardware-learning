## Choose a printer

In Lesson 1 you built a parametric servo bracket in OpenSCAD and checked the exported STL with `stl_info.py`. A CAD model is a promise: it says what the part should be, not what your machine can make. The next step is choosing the machine — or deciding not to buy one yet.

The Ender-3 V3 SE is the cheapest way in. It has an open frame and less automation than the Bambu machines, so you will still check the bed, adjust the Z offset and learn what a first layer should look like. That is either a cost or a feature, depending on your temperament: after a month you will understand your printer, because you will have fixed it.

The Bambu Lab A1 mini is the opposite trade. It works out of the box, calibrates itself, and its 180 mm bed fits most brackets and gripper fingers. The A1 is the same machine with a 256 mm bed, which matters when you print a chassis plate or a bracket that must stay in one piece. If your budget allows one printer and you would rather design than adjust the machine, the A1 is a reasonable pick.

The Creality K1C adds an enclosure and a hardened nozzle, so carbon-fibre-filled filaments are usable without changing hardware. The Bambu Lab P1S is the enclosed CoreXY option for ABS and ASA. Enclosures are not a status symbol: they keep hot air around the part so large ABS prints do not split, and they keep fumes away from the room. For this course, an open-frame printer that prints PLA, PETG and TPU reliably is enough; buy an enclosure only when you actually need ABS or ASA. The money is better spent on filament, calipers and a spare nozzle.

A practical warning: a printer is a machine that takes up space, needs a stable table and occasional maintenance. Do not buy a 300 mm machine for a hypothetical project you have not started; the parts in this course are small on purpose.

### No printer?

You do not need to own a printer to finish this lesson. Fab Labs are digital-fabrication workshops; the fablabs.io directory lists 2,885 of them worldwide (October 2026) and is searchable by country. Many public-library makerspaces in the US have 3D printers that are free or near-free; you usually pay only for filament. For a one-off part, a service is often the rational choice: Craftcloud compares quotes across a network spanning 95 countries and routes the job to a manufacturer near you, which makes it the right choice outside the US and EU. JLC3DP offers MJF nylon and FDM from $1.00 per part, with builds around three days.

The catch with services is the loop. Every revision becomes an order, a wait and a shipping cost, and you cannot check a fit the same afternoon. Use a service for a final part in a material you cannot print, not for the ten iterations that get you there.

### Breaking even on a printer

A full SO-101 arm needs roughly 1 kg of PLA+. On your own machine that is about $20 to $25 of filament; a ready-printed set costs $30.99. On those numbers alone, a printer does not pay for itself on one build. It pays for itself on iteration: the tenth revision of a gripper finger costs 40 cents and 25 minutes at home, against about $8 and a week from a service.

```text
10 revisions at home = 10 × $0.40 = $4.00 and 10 × 25 min ≈ 4.2 h
by service = 10 × $8 = $80 and about 10 weeks if you wait for each one
```

That is $4.00 of filament and about 4.2 hours of printing at home, against $80 and roughly ten weeks if you wait for each service order. The time difference is the real argument. A printer turns a design change into an afternoon; a service turns it into a project.

Do the break-even arithmetic: at $7.60 saved per revision, a $219 printer (the A1 mini) needs about 29 revisions before the hardware is paid for. Few people reach that on one project, and almost everyone reaches it across a year of small brackets, mounts and repairs. Add failed prints, electricity and your own time, and the case is not "printing is cheap"; it is "iterating at home is cheap".

## Pick the filament

Filament is not ink. Each polymer trades stiffness, toughness, heat resistance and printability, and the right choice depends on what the part must survive, not on what colour looks good.

| Material | Use it for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PLA and PLA+ | Prototype brackets, jigs, and the SO-101 arm itself: the SO-ARM100 repo specifies PLA+, 15 % <Term id="infill">infill</Term>, 0.2 mm <Term id="layer-height">layers</Term> with a 0.4 mm nozzle | Stiffest of the easy materials, but it <Term id="creep">creeps</Term> under sustained load and softens around 55–60 °C (a car dashboard in summer, a part touching a hot motor) |
| PETG | The default real robot part: chassis plates, gearbox housings, servo mounts, anything that must survive a drop | Tough, with much better layer adhesion than PLA; strings, and sticks very well to some beds (use glue stick as a release layer on smooth PEI) |
| ABS and ASA | Parts near hot motors; outdoor rovers (ASA resists UV) | Warp badly without an enclosure; fumes, so ventilate |
| Nylon | Gears and cable guides | Absorbs water from the air, must be dried; easier to order (JLC3DP's MJF nylon) than to print |
| Carbon-fibre filled (PLA-CF, PETG-CF, PA-CF) | Stiff structural links | Abrasive: needs a hardened nozzle (a brass nozzle wears out); slightly more brittle |
| TPU | Feet, bumpers, compliant gripper fingers (Lesson 6) | Print slowly, direct-drive extruder strongly preferred, little or no retraction |

PLA and PLA+ are the easiest starting point: stiff, dimensionally predictable and cheap. The SO-ARM100 repository specifies PLA+, 15 % infill and 0.2 mm layers with a 0.4 mm nozzle for its printed parts, which is a sensible default for a first robot arm. The limit is creep and heat. PLA creeps under a sustained load — a screw boss or a cantilever that stays loaded will slowly change shape — and it softens around 55–60 °C. A part that lives on a car dashboard in summer, or touches a motor that runs hot, is the wrong place for it. PLA+ is tougher than plain PLA but has the same heat limit.

PETG is the course's default for real robot parts: chassis plates, gearbox housings, servo mounts, anything that must survive a drop. It is less stiff than PLA but much tougher, and its layer adhesion is better, so a part is less likely to split along a layer line. It also strings, and it sticks very well to some beds; on a smooth PEI sheet use a glue stick as a release layer, or you may take a piece of the sheet with the part.

ABS and ASA are for parts near hot motors and for outdoor rovers; ASA resists UV better. Both warp badly without an enclosure, especially on large flat parts, and both produce fumes, so ventilate the room. Do not choose ABS because it sounds more "engineering" than PLA; choose it because the part will get hot or sit in the sun.

Nylon is for gears and cable guides: tough, slippery and fatigue-resistant. It also absorbs water from the air, so it must be dried before printing and kept dry while printing; wet nylon prints with bubbles and weak layers. For many people the practical route is to order MJF nylon from a service rather than handle moisture at home.

Carbon-fibre-filled filaments (PLA-CF, PETG-CF, PA-CF) are for stiff structural links. They are abrasive: a brass nozzle wears out, so you need a hardened nozzle. They are also slightly more brittle than the unfilled polymer, so "carbon fibre" does not mean "unbreakable".

TPU is for feet, bumpers and the compliant gripper fingers in Lesson 6. Print it slowly, use a direct-drive extruder if you have one, and keep retraction small or off; TPU buckles inside a long Bowden tube.

A rule of thumb: start in PLA to check fit, then make the real part in PETG unless heat or flexibility says otherwise. Buy small spools while you learn, store them with desiccant, and dry PETG and nylon if you live somewhere humid. A spool that has been open for a year is not the same material as the one you calibrated.

### Why the materials behave this way

The table compresses a few mechanisms. Knowing them lets you reason about a material that is not in the table.

**Heat: the glass transition.** As printed, PLA, PETG, ABS and ASA are mostly amorphous (PLA is semi-crystalline, but NatureWorks' guide [Crystallizing and Drying Ingeo Biopolymer](https://natureworksllc.com/getContentAsset/c31ce1f9-5412-48c8-a736-faf0faa11daf/53ffd608-340f-457b-b656-6b8cc0000bd4/ProcessingGuide_Crystallizing-and-Drying.pdf) notes that grades such as 4043D come out essentially amorphous after the fast cooling of normal extrusion, and printing cools faster still): their long polymer chains are tangled and frozen in place. Below the <Term id="glass-transition-tg">glass transition temperature (Tg)</Term> the chains cannot slide past one another and the part is stiff; through Tg the material turns soft and rubbery over a few degrees. Tg is not the melting point, and it is the number that limits a printed part in service. NatureWorks lists Tg = 55–60 °C for Ingeo 4043D, a common PLA filament resin, and a heat deflection temperature of 55 °C under a 0.45 MPa load. Eastman's [data sheet for Eastar 6763](https://productcatalog.eastman.com/tds/ProdDatasheet.aspx?pn=eastar-6763-copolyester&product=71040786), a PETG copolyester, gives Tg = 80 °C (by DSC) and a heat deflection temperature of 70 °C under 0.455 MPa. ABS and ASA get their stiffness from a glassy SAN (styrene–acrylonitrile) phase, and their vendors quote softening points rather than a Tg: INEOS lists a Vicat softening temperature (B/50) of 95 °C and a heat deflection temperature of 95 °C under 0.45 MPa (measured on specimens annealed for 4 h at 80 °C) for [Terluran GP-35](https://eshop.ineos-styrolution.com/INTERSHOP/web/WFS/Styrolution-Portal-Site/en_US/-/USD/ViewPDF-Print.pdf?SKU=300600120829&RenderPageType=ProductDetail&ShowISO=true), a general-purpose ABS, and 97 °C and 101 °C, under the same conditions, for [Luran S 777K](https://eshop.ineos-styrolution.com/INTERSHOP/web/WFS/Styrolution-Portal-Site/en_US/-/USD/ViewPDF-Print.pdf?SKU=400700190193&RenderPageType=ProductDetail&ShowISO=true), a standard ASA. These are data-sheet values (ISO 75 and ISO 306), and a printed part is not annealed: INEOS's ASTM D648 data for Terluran GP-35 give 101 °C annealed but about 89 °C unannealed under 0.45 MPa (66 psi; 192 °F on the [Terluran GP-35 product page](https://eshop.ineos-styrolution.com/Product/Terluran_TERLURAN-GP-35_SKU300600120829.html)), so a printed ABS part softens closer to 89 °C. A part should run well below these temperatures: next to a 70 °C motor, PLA is above its Tg, and PETG is already at its heat deflection temperature even though it is 10 °C below its Tg.

**Creep.** Even below Tg the chains rearrange slowly under a sustained <Term id="stress">stress</Term>, so a loaded part keeps deforming over hours and weeks. The rate rises steeply as the temperature approaches Tg, which is why PLA, with the lowest Tg of the group, creeps the most in a warm robot: a screw boss on a motor mount loosens and a cantilever that holds a sensor sags.

**Moisture.** Nylon's amide groups bind water from the air. BASF's datasheet for Ultramid B3S, a PA6, lists 2.6–3.4 % water at equilibrium in 23 °C air at 50 % relative humidity and 9–10 % at saturation in water, and the absorbed water changes the material: the tensile modulus (<Term id="youngs-modulus">Young's modulus</Term>, its stiffness) falls from 3500 MPa dry to 1200 MPa conditioned. In the hot end the water boils, which is where the bubbles and weak layers of wet nylon come from. PETG absorbs much less (Eastman lists 0.13 % for 6763 after 24 h in water), but enough to string and pop, so it is dried as well.

**UV.** ABS gets its toughness from particles of butadiene rubber, whose carbon–carbon double bonds are attacked by ultraviolet light and oxygen: the surface yellows and becomes brittle. ASA replaces the butadiene with an acrylate rubber that has no such double bonds, so it keeps its toughness outdoors.

**Layer adhesion.** Two printed layers bond only where the heat of the new bead lets polymer chains diffuse across the interface before it cools below Tg. The bond is therefore weaker than the material inside a line, and it depends on how wide the weld between the beads is and how long the interface stays hot. Anything that shortens that time weakens the bond for every material: a part-cooling fan at full speed, a lower nozzle temperature, a cold room.

Which material?" options={["PLA+", "TPU", "ABS or ASA", "PLA-CF with a brass nozzle"]} answer={2} explanation="PLA softens around 55–60 °C, so it is the wrong choice next to a 70 °C motor. ## Your first print

With a printer and a filament chosen, the next step is one successful print. It tests the machine before you spend time on calibration, and it is the routine you will repeat for every part. Read the safety rules first; they apply to every printer in the shortlist.

<Callout type="danger" title="Hot surfaces, molten plastic, fumes">
- **The nozzle and the bed cause burns.** The Bambu Lab A1 mini's hot end reaches up to 300 °C and its bed up to 80 °C, and Prusa's CORE One handbook warns that the print plate and heated bed may exceed 100 °C. Both stay hot after the print ends: wait until the screen shows that they have cooled, and keep your hands out of the print area while the printer is running.
- **Clear a clog only as the manual describes.** Some unclogging steps are done with the printer switched off and the nozzle fully cooled, others with the nozzle heated above the filament's printing temperature; the manual or the manufacturer's wiki says which, and which tool to use, such as the pin or needle supplied with the printer. When the nozzle is hot, wear heat-resistant gloves and keep your face away from it: Bambu Lab warns that pressure behind a clog can make molten filament spray out suddenly.
- **Do not leave a running printer unattended.** Thermal runaway protection is a firmware check that switches the heaters off when the measured temperature stops following the set value, for example when a temperature sensor comes loose and the heater would otherwise keep heating. It reduces the risk but does not remove it: Prusa's handbook states that misuse or an unexpected component failure can still pose a fire hazard, and tells you not to leave the printer unattended while it is on. Keep flammable material away from the printer.
- **Ventilate.** Printing releases ultrafine particles and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). UL Chemical Insights reports that emissions depend on the printer, the material, the operating conditions and the ventilation; PLA generally emits less, while ABS and nylon, printed at higher temperatures, release significantly more. Place the printer where the room is well ventilated or where an exhaust vents to the outside, and print at the lowest nozzle temperature that gives good quality.
- **Use the scraper away from your hand.** Let the plate cool first; many parts come off when you remove the flexible plate and bend it. If a part or a remnant stays, use the scraper or plastic spatula supplied with the printer, and push the blade away from your other hand and your body. Do not pick at remnants with your fingernails, and do not use a sharp metal tool on a sheet whose manual forbids it, as Prusa's does.
- **The insert iron is a hot soldering iron.** Setting heat-set inserts (later in this lesson) means a tip at about 225 °C or more. Return the iron to its stand between inserts, press the insert home with a tool rather than a finger, and switch the iron off when you finish.
</Callout>

### From CAD to G-code

Every print passes through the same chain:

```text
CAD model → STL or 3MF mesh → slicer → G-code → printer
```

Lesson 1 covered the first two steps and the difference between STL and 3MF. A <Term id="slicer">slicer</Term> such as OrcaSlicer, Bambu Studio or PrusaSlicer cuts the mesh into layers, plans the walls, infill, supports and travel moves, and writes G-code: a plain-text list of machine commands (move to a position, set a temperature, feed a length of filament) that the printer's firmware executes line by line. G-code contains absolute temperatures, speeds and positions for one machine, so select the printer profile that matches your printer and the filament profile that matches the spool before you slice, and do not send a file sliced for one printer model to another.

### Set up and run the first print

1. **Place the printer** on a stable table, with room for the bed and the print head to move through their full travel, and away from open windows and air-conditioning outlets, whose draughts cool the part unevenly.
2. **Run the first-start routine.** Current printers run a self-test or an initial calibration the first time they are switched on; on the A1 mini, vibration and noise during this calibration are normal. Let it finish before you print.
3. **Load the filament** with the printer's load function: it heats the nozzle to the filament's temperature, drives the filament into the extruder and purges until clean plastic of the new colour comes out. Cut the filament end at an angle to a sharp tip first, so it enters the extruder cleanly. To change material, use the unload function, which heats the nozzle before it pulls the filament back; filament cannot be pulled out cleanly while the plastic in the nozzle is solid.
4. **Clean the plate.** Grease from fingers is enough to stop a first layer from sticking. For its sheets, Prusa recommends isopropyl alcohol (IPA) of 90 % or more on a paper towel, and warns against products that contain IPA only as one ingredient, such as hand sanitiser, because their additives can affect the surface. For its textured PEI plate, Bambu Lab recommends dish soap and warm water, because alcohol may spread oil across the textured surface instead of removing it. Do not use acetone on a textured or satin sheet, dry the plate completely, and from then on hold it by its edges.
5. **Level the bed and set the Z offset.** These are two separate settings. Bed leveling measures the height of the bed at a grid of points, so the printer can follow the bed's shape; Bambu Lab recommends turning on "Bed leveling" when you start a print, and Prusa printers run mesh bed leveling before each print. The Z offset is the gap between the nozzle tip and the bed during the first layer. If it is too large, the filament is laid on top of the bed and does not stick; if it is too small, the plastic cannot flow out, which gives a rough layer and can clog the nozzle. Printers with automatic calibration measure the offset themselves; on others you adjust it while the first layer prints, which Prusa calls Live Adjust Z.
6. **Print the manufacturer's test model first.** The A1 mini comes with preloaded models on its SD card, and Prusa supplies sliced sample files on a USB drive. The manufacturer prepared these files for that printer, so they test the machine on its own. Prusa's handbook draws the conclusion: if the sample files print correctly and your own model fails, the problem is most likely in your slicing.
7. **Watch the first layer** from start to finish, and stop the print if it fails. The table below shows how to judge it.

### Judging the first layer

The first layer decides whether the rest of the print can succeed, and it is the cheapest point at which to stop. Prusa's first layer calibration guide describes three cases. Judge them by eye, because the correct offset value differs between printers and between print sheets.

| What you see | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rounded lines with gaps between them; lines that drag or come loose | Nozzle too high (Z offset too large) | Move the nozzle closer in small steps, or rerun the printer's calibration |
| Lines squished flat with ridges between them; edges curling up; areas so thin you can see through them | Nozzle too low (Z offset too small); in the extreme it clogs the nozzle | Raise the nozzle in small steps |
| An even surface with no gaps and no ridges | Correct | Let the print continue, and note the value in your print log |

If the first layer does not stick although the offset looks correct, clean the plate again before you change anything else. The troubleshooting table later in this lesson covers the other causes.

## Calibrate, then measure

A printer that assembles and homes is not calibrated. Dimensional accuracy is a chain: extruder steps, flow, temperature, pressure advance, retraction, cooling and shrinkage all affect the final size. OrcaSlicer's calibration guide assumes the extruder itself is already calibrated in the firmware (E-steps or rotation distance), and notes that most modern printers ship with this set correctly. Its recommended running order is temperature → maximum volumetric speed → <Term id="pressure-advance">pressure advance</Term> → flow ratio → <Term id="retraction">retraction</Term>, with <Term id="tolerance">tolerance</Term> last. The order is not arbitrary. Each test assumes the earlier ones are right. Temperature sets the viscosity of the plastic, so with the wrong temperature every later test measures the wrong material behaviour. Maximum volumetric speed is the largest volume of plastic per second that the hot end can melt with this filament; it sets a safe limit, so the later tests do not under-extrude or clog the nozzle. Pressure advance comes before flow because it is the value most often wrong, or never calibrated at all, while the flow ratio in most filament profiles is already close; if the flow test changes the flow ratio a lot, run a quick pressure advance test again. Retraction follows both, because both change the pressure in the nozzle. Wrong flow makes every wall thicker and every hole smaller, which corrupts a tolerance test; tolerance comes last because it depends on all of them. Many older guides put flow before pressure advance. The OrcaSlicer guide notes that the two affect each other only slightly, so either order works as long as you rerun pressure advance after a large flow change.

Teaching Tech's interactive calibration covers the same ground for any printer and generates the test prints for you. Use one of the two; do not invent your own sequence.

### Flow, pressure advance and retraction

These three settings appear in every slicer and in most troubleshooting advice, so learn what each one does before you run its test. OrcaSlicer generates each test print from its **Calibration** menu. Run the tests with the printer, filament and nozzle you will actually use, and save each result in the filament profile.

**Flow ratio** scales every extrusion in the G-code proportionally: lowering it feeds less filament than the slicer calculated, raising it feeds more. Too low, and the printer under-extrudes: gaps between lines and weak bonds between layers. Too high, and it over-extrudes: rough surfaces and parts larger than designed. OrcaSlicer recommends keeping it between 0.95 and 1.05. Its recommended test, Archimedean chords with the YOLO method, prints 11 blocks with flow modifiers from −0.05 to +0.05 in steps of 0.01. Pick the block with the smoothest top surface and no gaps between lines, and add its modifier to the old value: in the guide's example, 0.98 + 0.01 = 0.99. On Bambu Lab printers, do not also tick the printer's own "Flow calibration" option when you send the test.

<Term id="pressure-advance">**Pressure advance**</Term>, called Linear Advance in Marlin firmware, compensates for the delay between the extruder motor and the plastic leaving the nozzle. Molten plastic is compressed inside the hot end, so when the print head speeds up, the pressure, and with it the flow, builds up late; when the head slows down for a corner, the remaining pressure keeps pushing plastic out. Without compensation, lines start thin and end in a blob, and corners bulge. Pressure advance feeds extra filament as the head accelerates and less as it decelerates. OrcaSlicer offers three tests, tower, pattern and line, and each comes in one version for <Term id="direct-drive">direct-drive</Term> extruders and one for <Term id="bowden">Bowden</Term> extruders; choose the version that matches your printer. The tower test does not depend on the quality of the first layer, which makes it the safest choice on a new printer; the line test does. In the tower, the value rises by one step per millimetre of height. Find the height with the sharpest corners and compute start + step × height: in the guide's example, 0 + 0.002 × 8 = 0.016. On a Marlin printer, Linear Advance must be enabled in the firmware (M900) before the test means anything.

<Term id="retraction">**Retraction**</Term> pulls the filament back a short distance before a travel move, so the nozzle does not ooze and draw strings across the gap, and pushes it forward again before printing resumes. The OrcaSlicer guide notes that the default retraction values usually work well; if you calibrate them, do it after pressure advance and flow ratio, because both change the pressure in the nozzle. Too short, and strings remain; too long, and the extra movement can cause clogs or under-extrusion at the start of the next line. The retraction tower prints a notch at each length. Its defaults, 0 to 2 mm in steps of 0.1 mm, suit most direct-drive extruders; for a Bowden extruder the guide suggests 1 to 6 mm in steps of 0.2 mm. Choose the shortest length that gives a clean section: measure that section's height and find the matching `Calib_Retraction_tower` comment in the G-code preview. If the tower is clean from the bottom, 0.2 to 0.4 mm is enough. If it strings all the way to the top, dry the filament and check that the nozzle is installed without leaks before you add more retraction.

### The tolerance gauge

Print the Clearance and Tolerance 3D Printer Gauge once, in the material and orientation you will use for the real part. Try each gap with your fingers. Write down the smallest gap that turns or slides freely — that is your sliding <Term id="fit">fit</Term> — and the gap that pushes together firmly and holds — that is your press fit. Do this before you design anything that must fit.

Read the model page carefully: do its numbers mean the gap on each side, or the total difference in size? The clearance parameter in Lesson 1's bracket and in this lesson's case is per side. If you copy a total-difference number into a per-side parameter, every gap comes out twice as big as you measured: a sliding fit gets sloppy and a press fit stops holding.

A worked example:

```text
your gauge says parts with 0.3 mm total difference slide freely and 0.1 mm total press together
→ per side: sliding 0.3 / 2 = 0.15 mm, press 0.1 / 2 = 0.05 mm
a 3 mm pin that must turn freely gets a hole of 3 + 2 × 0.15 = 3.3 mm
```

The last line is the one to remember: a hole that must clear a shaft gets the shaft diameter plus twice the per-side clearance, because the gap appears on both sides. A 3 mm pin with 0.15 mm per side needs a 3.3 mm hole. If you draw 3.15 mm, the pin fits on one side and interferes on the other.

### Why holes come out small

A slicer turns a circle into many short straight segments. The nozzle has a round tip, so it cannot follow a sharp inside corner; it rounds it. Molten plastic is also squeezed slightly outward as it is laid down. The result is a pattern: inner sizes (holes, slots) shrink, outer sizes grow. The first layer is squashed wider still — the "elephant's foot" — which closes the bottom of a hole and makes a part sit proud. Add a small chamfer, about 0.4–0.5 mm, to bottom edges that must fit, and the squashed bead has somewhere to go.

This is why you measure the printed part, not just the CAD model. Keep a print log: printer, nozzle, material and brand, temperature, layer height, sliding and press clearances per side. When a part fits, you can repeat it; when it does not, the log tells you which number changed.

### When a print fails

Most failed prints come down to a few causes. Find the symptom in the left column and work through the checks in order; change one setting at a time and record each change in your print log. The causes and checks follow Prusa's knowledge base articles on each problem.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Check and fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Warping**: corners lift off the bed, the bottom of the part curves | The plastic shrinks as it cools from nozzle temperature to room temperature, and the upper layers pull on the lower ones; worst with high-temperature materials such as ABS, ASA and PC, and with large parts | Clean the plate; check the first layer; keep the printer away from open windows and air-conditioning; check that the part-cooling fan works for PLA and PETG; print ABS and ASA in an enclosure; add a brim; print more slowly |
| **Stringing**: thin hairs of plastic between separate parts of the print | Nozzle temperature too high, retraction too short, or wet filament; PETG strings more than PLA | Lower the nozzle temperature in small steps; run the retraction test above; dry the filament |
| **Under-extrusion or clog**: gaps in the walls, thin or missing layers, clicking from the extruder | Nozzle too close to the bed on the first layer, slicer settings beyond what the hot end can melt, a partly clogged nozzle, dirty extruder gears, poor hot-end cooling, or a tangled spool | Check the Z offset and the slicer profile; clear the nozzle as the manual describes, or replace it; clean the extruder gears; check that the hot-end fan runs; check that the spool unwinds freely |
| **Layer shift**: from a certain height on, the layers are offset sideways | The print head or the bed did not follow its commanded movement: wrong belt tension, a loose pulley set screw, an obstruction in the travel path, or the nozzle hitting an overhang that curled up | Check the belt tension and the pulley set screws as the manual describes; clear the travel path; fix the curling overhang with more cooling or a different orientation |
| **First layer does not stick** | Dirty plate, nozzle too far from the bed, or nozzle and bed temperatures that do not match the filament | Clean the plate as described above; adjust the Z offset; use the temperatures from the filament profile; add a brim; use a glue stick only where the printer's manual recommends it |

## Design for FDM

The milestone for this lesson is to design a part specifically for FDM, not a CAD shape that happens to export. Three things decide whether a printed part is strong and easy to make: orientation, overhangs and layer adhesion. FDM parts are <Term id="anisotropy">anisotropic</Term>: they are a stack of welded lines, and the bond between layers is weaker than the plastic inside a layer.

- **Orientation.** A print is weakest between layers, so lay the part so the main load runs along the layers, not across them. A hook printed standing up snaps at a layer line; the same hook printed lying down bends. Decide the orientation before you model, because it changes where you put chamfers and which face touches the bed.
- **Overhangs.** Up to about 45° from vertical usually prints without support; beyond that, add a chamfer instead of a sharp overhang, or plan support. The 45° number is a starting point, not a law: cooling, nozzle size and layer height change it.
- **Bridges.** A short horizontal span, a few centimetres depending on the printer, can bridge between two supports. Anchor both ends, cool the bridge well, and do not ask it to carry a load.
- **Horizontal holes.** A round hole lying on its side droops at the top. A teardrop or a flat-topped hole avoids support and keeps the top of the hole usable. For a precision bore, print undersize and drill or ream it.
- **Walls.** Make thin walls a whole number of extrusion widths: with a 0.4 mm nozzle, use 0.8, 1.2 or 1.6 mm. Strength comes mostly from walls (<Term id="perimeter-wall">perimeters</Term>), not infill. For most brackets, adding a wall or two is a cheaper way to gain strength than adding infill.
- **Fasteners.** Use heat-set brass inserts for threads you will open more than a few times. Printed threads are fine for coarse, rarely used parts, but they wear and strip. Size the insert hole from the insert datasheet and test one; the steps are in "Installing heat-set inserts" below.
- **Supports.** Supports cost time, leave rough surfaces and weaken the part where they touch. A design that needs none is usually a better design: chamfer the overhang, split the part, or choose a different orientation.

### Where the 45° and two-width rules come from

Both rules come from the geometry of a bead. Each new line is supported only where it rests on the line below it. If a wall leans out from vertical by an angle θ, every layer of height h steps outward by h × tan θ. A common working limit is that each bead must rest on at least half of the bead below, that is, a step of no more than half the extrusion width w:

```text
h × tan θ ≤ w / 2   →   tan θ ≤ w / (2 × h)
0.4 mm line, 0.2 mm layer:  tan θ ≤ 1.0  →  θ ≤ 45°
0.4 mm line, 0.1 mm layer:  tan θ ≤ 2.0  →  θ ≤ 63°
0.6 mm line, 0.2 mm layer:  tan θ ≤ 1.5  →  θ ≤ 56°
```

That is where the 45° figure comes from for the course's default 0.4 mm nozzle and 0.2 mm layers, and why thinner layers and wider lines tolerate steeper overhangs. Cooling decides whether the unsupported half of each bead freezes before it droops, so the same geometry prints cleaner with strong part cooling; PLA, which is printed with the fan on, typically handles overhangs better than ABS, which is printed with little or no fan. Print the overhang test from your calibration tool to find your own limit.

The two-width minimum comes from how the slicer builds a wall. A wall is printed as perimeters, at least one on each face, with infill or more perimeters between them. A wall exactly two extrusion widths thick, 0.8 mm with a 0.4 mm nozzle, gets one full perimeter on each face, bonded to each other along their length. Anything thinner leaves room for one line only. OrcaSlicer's default wall generator (Arachne) then prints a single line of variable width: features narrower than the minimum feature size, 25 % of the nozzle diameter (0.1 mm for a 0.4 mm nozzle), are skipped entirely, and features between that and the minimum wall width, 85 % of the nozzle diameter (0.34 mm), are widened to 0.34 mm, so the printed wall no longer matches the CAD. A single line has no neighbour to bond to, so it is weak across its width and its edges come out ragged. Check the slicer preview: a thin feature that shows one line, or none, will not be the part you drew.

CNC Kitchen publishes instrumented, repeatable strength tests of infill, walls, heat-set inserts and orientation. Use those tests instead of folklore.

Design for FDM also means designing for your machine's error. The clearance numbers from the gauge belong in the model as parameters, not in a note to yourself to "sand it later". Put fillets on inside corners, where stress concentrates, and avoid features thinner than two extrusion widths, because the slicer will either skip them or print them badly.

### Installing heat-set inserts

A heat-set insert is a brass sleeve with a metal thread inside and knurling on the outside. You press it into a printed hole with a hot soldering iron: the brass heats the plastic around it, the plastic melts and flows into the knurls, and when it cools the insert is locked in place. A screw then threads into metal instead of plastic, so it can be tightened and removed many times. Read the safety rule on the insert iron in "Your first print" before you start.

**Size the hole from the datasheet of the insert you bought.** [CNC Kitchen's insert dimensions and design guidelines](https://www.cnckitchen.com/s/CNCKitchen_Heat-Set-Insert-Dimensions-and-Design-Guidelines.pdf) give, for its M3 × 5.7 insert:

```text
M3 × 5.7 insert:  length L = 5.7 mm, outer diameter 4.6 mm
hole diameter     4.0 mm   (straight, not tapered; normally no chamfer)
minimum wall      1.6 mm   around the hole
blind hole depth  ≥ L + 1 mm = 6.7 mm
```

These are nominal sizes for a hole of exactly that diameter, such as a drilled one. Printed holes come out smaller than drawn (see "Why holes come out small" above). In [CNC Kitchen's test of printed holes](https://www.cnckitchen.com/blog/are-our-heat-set-insert-datasheets-wrong), they came out about 0.25 mm undersize, and drawing the hole at 4.2 mm in CAD gave the best result for holes used as printed, at about 90 % of the highest strength in the test. Your printer will differ, so print a test block with a few hole sizes around this value, in the same material and with the same wall and infill settings as the real part, and install one insert in each, from the smallest hole up. If the insert pushes a burr of plastic out of the bottom of the hole, the hole is still too small; the right size leaves no burr that could block the screw. Other makers use other sizes; [Ruthex](https://www.ruthex.de/en/products/ruthex-gewindeeinsatz-m3-100-stuck-rx-m3x5-7-messing-gewindebuchsen), for example, publishes CAD data for each insert on its product page. Always use the dimensions for the inserts you actually have.

**Install the insert.** The steps follow [CNC Kitchen's tips for heat-set inserts](https://www.cnckitchen.com/blog/tips-and-tricks-for-heat-set-inserts):

1. Set the iron 10 to 20 °C above the printing temperature of the part's material: about 225 °C for PLA, 245 °C for PETG and 265 °C for ABS.
2. Place the insert on the hole with its pilot (the short, smooth end) down. The hole should be just large enough for the pilot to sit in it easily.
3. Press the insert in with the iron tip, keeping it straight, until it is about 90 % of the way in.
4. Take the iron away and press the insert flush with a flat tool, such as the shaft of a screwdriver or a pair of tweezers. Hold it for a few seconds until the plastic has solidified; otherwise the insert tends to creep back out.
5. Return the iron to its stand before you pick up the next insert.

## Snap-fits that click

A cantilever snap-fit is a small beam with a hook at its tip. Pushing the parts together, the hook's lead-in presses against the other part and bends the beam sideways by the hook depth. Once the hook passes the catch, the beam springs back and the catch face holds the parts together. It is a spring, not a clamp, and it has two failure modes: the arm breaks at the root, or the force needed to close it is too high to be useful.

For a rectangular cantilever, the classic straight-beam formulas from snap-fit design guides are:

```text
strain at the root ε = 1.5 × y × t / L²
force to bend it P = b × t² × E × ε / (6 × L)
push-on force W = P × (μ + tan α) / (1 − μ × tan α)
```

Here y is the hook depth, t the arm thickness, L the arm length, b the arm width, E the material's Young's modulus, its stiffness (stress divided by strain in the elastic range), α the lead-in angle and μ the <Term id="friction-coefficient">friction coefficient</Term>. <Term id="strain">Strain</Term> tells you whether the plastic survives; push-on force tells you whether a person can close the case.

Worked example for the case's arm:

```text
L = 10, t = 1.2, y = 0.8 → ε = 1.5 × 0.8 × 1.2 / 10² = 1.44 / 100 = 0.0144 = 1.44 %
```

The key insight is the square: strain grows with 1/L², so halving the length quadruples the strain. A short, thick arm is the design people draw first, and it is usually the one that cracks. Making the arm thinner lowers the strain; making the hook shallower lowers it too. Making the arm longer helps a lot, but it takes space inside the case.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-3/fdm-printing/snap_fit.py" />

```python
"""snap_fit.py: will a printed cantilever snap-fit arm bend or break?

Classic straight-beam formulas for a rectangular cantilever arm with a hook at its tip
(as in the Bayer/Covestro snap-fit design guide):
    strain at the root   eps = 1.5 * y * t / L^2
    force to deflect it  P   = b * t^2 * E * eps / (6 * L)
    force to push it on  W   = P * (mu + tan a) / (1 - mu * tan a)
y = hook depth (how far the arm must bend), t = arm thickness, L = arm length,
b = arm width, E = stiffness of the plastic, a = lead-in angle of the hook, mu = friction.
The strain limits below are our cautious rules of thumb for FDM parts, not datasheet
values: a printed arm is weakest when it bends across its layers. Print a test arm before
you trust any of this.
"""
import math

MATERIALS = {             # E (MPa), strain we allow for a snap that is used many times
    "PLA":  (3500, 0.015),
    "PETG": (2000, 0.025),
}


def snap(L, t, y, b=6.0, material="PLA", lead_deg=30.0, mu=0.3):
    E, allowed = MATERIALS[material]
    eps = 1.5 * y * t / L ** 2
    P = b * t ** 2 * E * eps / (6 * L)
    tan_a = math.tan(math.radians(lead_deg))
    W = P * (mu + tan_a) / (1 - mu * tan_a)
    verdict = "ok" if eps <= allowed else f"too much (limit {allowed * 100:.1f} %)"
    return (f"L {L:4.1f}  t {t:.1f}  hook {y:.1f} mm, {material + ",":5s} lead-in {lead_deg:2.0f} deg: "
            f"strain {eps * 100:4.2f} % {verdict:22s} push-on {W:5.1f} N")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(snap(5, 1.5, 1.0))                   # short and thick: the first design people draw
    print(snap(10, 1.5, 1.0))
    print(snap(10, 1.2, 0.8))                  # the arm used in esp32_case.scad
    print(snap(10, 1.2, 0.8, material="PETG"))
    print(snap(10, 1.2, 0.8, lead_deg=60))     # steep lead-in: hard to push on
```

L 10.0  t 1.2  hook 0.8 mm, PLA,  lead-in 30 deg: strain 1.44 % ok                     push-on   7.7 N
L 10.0  t 1.2  hook 0.8 mm, PETG, lead-in 30 deg: strain 1.44 % ok                     push-on   4.4 N
L 10.0  t 1.2  hook 0.8 mm, PLA,  lead-in 60 deg: strain 1.44 % ok                     push-on  30.7 N

**Guided experiment.** This experiment shows how the snap-fit geometry sets root strain, and how material and lead-in angle change the push-on force at the same strain.

1. Set L = 10.0 mm, t = 1.2 mm, hook depth y = 0.8 mm, material PLA and lead-in angle 30°, then read Root strain = 1.44 % and Push-on force = 7.7 N.
2. Set L = 5.0 mm, t = 1.5 mm and y = 1.0 mm, then read Root strain = 9.00 % and Push-on force = 150.4 N.
3. Set L = 10.0 mm, keep t = 1.5 mm and y = 1.0 mm, then read Root strain = 2.25 % and Push-on force = 18.8 N.
4. Return to L = 10.0 mm, t = 1.2 mm and y = 0.8 mm with PLA, set lead-in angle 60° and read Root strain = 1.44 % and Push-on force = 30.7 N; then set lead-in angle 30° and material PETG and read Root strain = 1.44 % and Push-on force = 4.4 N.
5. Conclusion: strain ε = 1.5·t·y/L² depends only on geometry (1.5 × 1.2 × 0.8 / 10² = 0.0144, shown as 1.44 %); the force also depends on the material's modulus and the lead-in angle, and the allowed strain is 1.5 % for PLA in this lesson.

Walk through each line. The short thick arm, L = 5, is the first design people draw; it strains 9 % and would crack. Doubling the length cuts the strain to 2.25 %, still above the limit. The thinner arm with a shallower hook gets to 1.44 %, inside the 1.5 % we allow for PLA. PETG is less stiff, so the same arm pushes on with less force, 4.4 N against 7.7 N. A steep 60° lead-in quadruples the push-on force to 30.7 N — that is how you make a snap that is hard to close.

The limits in the script, 1.5 % for PLA and 2.5 % for PETG, are the editor's cautious rules of thumb for printed parts, not datasheet values. A printed arm is weakest when it bends across its layers, and a real part has a fillet, layer lines and friction that the formula ignores. Print one test arm and snap it 20 times before you trust it in a case.

Practical details: put a fillet at the root where the arm meets the body, because that is where stress concentrates. Keep the catch face around 0.4 mm so it holds but can be released. A 30° lead-in closes easily; a 60° lead-in is hard to push on, which may be what you want for a part that should not open by accident. Give the user a place to press the arm when opening the case.

## Practice: an ESP32 case

Your practice task is to print the tolerance gauge, write down your machine's real clearance numbers, then design and print a two-part snap-fit enclosure for your ESP32 that closes without glue. Iterate until it clicks properly: too tight, loosen one number; too loose, tighten it. That loop — model, print, measure, change one number, repeat — is what all mechanical design is made of.

The file below is the editor's example, not a required design. Use it as a reference to compare against, or print it first if you want to see a working snap before you draw your own.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-3/fdm-printing/esp32_case.scad" />

```scad
// esp32_case.scad: a two-part snap-fit case for an ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1 (headers pins down).
// Board size from Espressif's v1.1 drawing (62.74 x 25.40 mm); measure your own board,
// clones differ. The snap arms use the numbers checked by snap_fit.py (L 10, t 1.2, hook 0.8).
//   Render both parts:  openscad -o esp32_case.stl esp32_case.scad
//   Only one part:      openscad -D 'part="base"' -o base.stl esp32_case.scad
//   Interference check: openscad -D 'part="clash"' -o clash.stl esp32_case.scad
//                       (closes the lid on the base; "top level object is empty" = no clash)

part      = "both";   // "base", "lid", "both" (side by side for printing) or "clash"

// --- the board ---
board_l   = 62.74;
board_w   = 25.40;
pins_h    = 9.0;      // header pins below the board, plus a little air
above_h   = 6.0;      // tallest part on top (buttons, module shield) plus air
usb_w     = 12.0;     // opening at one end for the USB cable(s)
usb_h     = 7.0;

// --- the printer ---
clearance = 0.3;      // board-to-wall gap on each side: use your tolerance-gauge result
fit       = 0.2;      // gap between the snap arm and the base wall

// --- the case ---
wall      = 2.0;
floor_t   = 2.0;
lid_t     = 2.0;
ledge     = 2.5;      // the board rests on two ledges at the short ends (pins are on the long edges)
arm_L     = 10.0;     // snap arm length  } keep these in step with snap_fit.py
arm_t     = 1.2;      // snap arm thickness }
hook      = 0.8;      // hook depth        }
arm_b     = 6.0;      // snap arm width

in_l  = board_l + 2 * clearance;
in_w  = board_w + 2 * clearance;
in_h  = pins_h + 1.6 + above_h;           // 1.6 mm board
out_l = in_l + 2 * wall;
out_w = in_w + 2 * wall;
out_h = floor_t + in_h;
win_z = out_h - arm_L - 0.2;              // window in the base wall that the hook clicks into

echo("inside (mm)", in_l, "x", in_w, "x", in_h);
echo("outside, closed (mm)", out_l, "x", out_w, "x", out_h + lid_t);
assert(hook < wall, "the hook must be shallower than the wall it clicks into");

module base() {
  difference() {
    cube([out_l, out_w, out_h]);
    translate([wall, wall, floor_t]) cube([in_l, in_w, in_h + 1]);
    translate([-1, (out_w - usb_w) / 2, floor_t + pins_h]) cube([wall + 2, usb_w, usb_h]);
    for (y = [-1, out_w - wall - 1])                       // windows for the two hooks
      translate([(out_l - arm_b - 1) / 2, y, win_z]) cube([arm_b + 1, wall + 2, 2.2]);
  }
  for (x = [wall, out_l - wall - ledge])                   // ledges at the short ends
    translate([x, wall, floor_t]) cube([ledge, in_w, pins_h]);
}

module lid() {
  cube([out_l, out_w, lid_t]);
  translate([wall + fit, wall + fit, lid_t]) difference() {   // locating lip
    cube([in_l - 2 * fit, in_w - 2 * fit, 1.5]);
    translate([1.2, 1.2, -1]) cube([in_l - 2 * fit - 2.4, in_w - 2 * fit - 2.4, 4]);
  }
}

// The lid prints flat with the arms standing up, so they bend ACROSS the layers (the weak
// direction). If an arm snaps, print the lid on its side or switch to PETG (see the lesson).
module lid_with_arms() {                                  // modelled upside down, as it prints
  lid();
  x0 = (out_l - arm_b) / 2;
  translate([x0, wall + fit, lid_t]) mirror([0, 1, 0]) translate([0, -arm_t, 0]) snap_arm_up();
  translate([x0, out_w - wall - fit - arm_t, lid_t]) snap_arm_up();
}

module snap_arm_up() {                                    // arm along +z, hook on the +y face at the tip
  cube([arm_b, arm_t, arm_L]);
  // hook: 0.4 mm catch face, then a 30 degree lead-in (0.8 / tan 30 = 1.4 mm) down to the tip
  translate([0, arm_t, arm_L - 1.8])
    rotate([90, 0, 90]) linear_extrude(arm_b) polygon([[0, 0], [hook, 0.4], [0, 1.8]]);
}

if (part == "base" || part == "both") base();
if (part == "lid") lid_with_arms();
if (part == "both") translate([0, out_w + 8, 0]) lid_with_arms();
if (part == "clash") intersection() {                     // what the closed lid and base share
  base();
  translate([0, out_w, out_h + lid_t]) rotate([180, 0, 0]) lid_with_arms();
}
```

The board size comes from Espressif's v1.1 drawing: 62.74 × 25.40 mm. Clones differ, so measure your own board before you trust those numbers. The board sits pins-down on two ledges at the short ends, because the header pins run along the long edges; the ledges leave the pins in the air and support the board by its ends. One end has a 12 × 7 mm opening for the USB cable. The lid has a locating lip that drops inside the walls and two snap arms; their L, t and hook values match `snap_fit.py`. The windows in the base walls are where the hooks click in. Each hook has a 0.4 mm catch face and a 30° lead-in, as the comment in the file explains.

The comment above the lid module is the important one: the lid prints flat with the arms standing up, so the arms bend across the layers — the weak direction from the previous section. That is why the arm strain is kept at 1.44 %, below the cautious 1.5 % limit for PLA. If an arm snaps, print the lid on its side or switch to PETG; do not simply make the arm thicker, because that raises both the strain and the push-on force.

## Pressure advance on a corner: K = 0, a tuned K and too much K

The subsection on flow, pressure advance and retraction above says what pressure advance does and how to read the tower. This section puts numbers on one corner: the same toolpath and the same melt, with only `K` changed. It is **not** the snap-fit strain toy, and it is **not** Marlin's or Klipper's planner.

<Resource id="marlin-lin-advance" />

**Sources actually opened (HTML body read).**

- **Marlin — Linear Advance:** by default the extruder moves in linear proportion to the other axes, but an extruder is not a linear system, so extra material comes out at the end of each move and corners bleed; LIN_ADVANCE decouples extrusion from the other axes to control the pressure in the nozzle. In v1.5, K has the unit mm of filament compression per 1 mm/s of extrusion speed, and typical values are around 0.1–2.0. If the advance needs more extruder speed than the extruder jerk limit allows, Marlin lowers the print acceleration for that move. A K that is too low gives a starved line when the head speeds up and too much material when it slows down; a K that is too high reverses that picture. Slicer options such as "coast at end" should be disabled because they interfere. 以原文为准.
- **Klipper — Kinematics and Pressure advance:** pressure rises when filament is pushed in (Hooke's law) and the flow is set by the nozzle orifice, modelled with one linear coefficient: `pa_position = nominal_position + pressure_advance × nominal_velocity`; during deceleration the extra filament is retracted, so the extruder runs backwards; Klipper also smooths the extruder motion (not modelled here). The tuning tower raises the value per layer (`FACTOR=.005` for direct drive); layers below the ideal value blob at the corners, layers above it give rounded corners and poor extrusion leading up to the corner; typical values are 0.050–1.000, the high end mostly Bowden; temperature and extrusion rate affect the value, and the test runs at a high flow rate. 以原文为准.

### A melt that stores pressure

Treat the filament between the drive gear and the nozzle as a spring with a leak. `s` is how many millimetres of filament are compressed in the hot end; the feed pushes it up, the flow out of the nozzle `q(s)` drains it:

<Formula label="Melt state">ds/dt = feed(t) − q(s),   q = q_ref × (s / s_ref)^(1/n),   s_ref = K_true × q_ref</Formula>

With `n = 1` this is Marlin's linear spring: at steady flow `q` the compression is `s = K_true × q`, so `K` really is "mm of compression per mm/s", and it is also the time constant of the lag, in seconds. `n < 1` is a shear-thinning stand-in: the local slope `ds/dq` then changes with flow, so one linear `K` cannot be exact at every speed. Pressure advance adds `K × k_e × dv` to the feed, which is Klipper's `nominal + K × velocity` written per step.

**Worked example — a 100 mm/s line braking for a corner.** Linear melt, `K_true = 0.06 s`, `k_e = 0.0333` mm of filament per mm of path, braking from 100 to 5 mm/s at 3000 mm/s²:

```text
flow at 100 mm/s: q = 0.0333 × 100 = 3.33 mm/s; stored compression s = 0.06 × 3.33 = 0.200 mm
brake time = (100 − 5) / 3000 = 31.7 ms, shorter than the 60 ms lag: the stored pressure is still there at the corner
K = 0.06:   feed = 0.0333 × (v − 0.06 × 3000) = 0.0333 × (v − 180) -> −2.66 mm/s at 100, −5.83 mm/s at 5 (retracting)
K = 0.18:   feed = 0.0333 × (v − 540)                             -> −14.65 to −17.82 mm/s: it pulls too much back
```

Without advance the leftover compression keeps pushing plastic while the head crawls through the corner: a bulge. A matched advance retracts exactly what the melt has stored; three times too much starves the line before the corner, the "poor extrusion leading up to the corner" Klipper describes.

**Integration step and its stability criterion.** The toy steps `s` with explicit Euler. For `ds/dt = −λ s`, explicit Euler is stable only when `DT < 2 / λ`. Here the local `λ = dq/ds = q / (n × s)`; for a linear melt it is `1 / K_true`. The toy records the largest `λ` it meets on every run (25.84 1/s, so the limit is 77.41 ms) and asserts `DT × λ_max < 2`. It uses `DT = 0.1 ms` for path resolution (0.01 mm of travel per step at 100 mm/s, against 0.5 mm width bins), not for stability. The independent tests check that a linear melt decays at 0.98× the limit and grows at 1.02×, and that halving `DT` changes the corner RMS width error by under 5 %.

### Fair setup

`pressure_advance_corner_toy.py` is **synthetic** and stdlib only. **Model assumptions:** a 1-D compressible melt stand-in (one state, no nozzle geometry, no temperature dynamics); `q = 0` when `s ≤ 0`, so the nozzle cannot suck plastic back; no extruder torque, jerk or acceleration limit, so the toy does **not** model Marlin lowering the acceleration or Klipper's extruder skipping at high K; no smoothing; a trapezoidal planner stand-in with one corner speed; line width per 0.5 mm bin is deposited volume over nominal, times a filament-diameter error (sd 0.4 %, per bin). The tuned K is what a tower would give: the local slope `n × K_true` at 100 mm/s, times a reading error (sd 15 %), rounded to the tower step of 0.005 s.

```python
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Pressure / linear advance on a shared corner path: K=0 vs tuned K vs over-tuned K.

Synthetic, stdlib only.  Not Marlin's or Klipper's planner, not a bit-exact
stepper ISR, and not the snap_fit strain toy.  Real printer calibration:
本页未运行.  Marlin / Klipper statements are 以原文为准.

Melt stand-in (1-D, compressible):
    ds/dt = feed(t) - q(s)        s = filament compression in the hot end [mm]
    q(s)  = q_ref * (s / s_ref) ** (1 / n),  s_ref = K_true * q_ref,  q(s <= 0) = 0
n = 1 is the linear spring of Marlin's unit "mm of compression per mm/s";
n < 1 is a shear-thinning stand-in, so a single linear K cannot be exact.
Feed with advance K (Klipper's pa_position = nominal + K * nominal_velocity):
    feed step = k_e * v * DT + K * k_e * (v_next - v)
Arms: A_K0 (K = 0), B_tuned (K read off a tuning tower), C_over (3 x B's K).

Common random numbers: per (regime, seed) the melt K_true, the exponent n,
the tower reading error and the filament-diameter sequence are drawn once
and shared by all three arms.  Arms differ only in K.

Explicit Euler is stable for this scalar ODE when DT < 2 / lambda_max with
lambda = dq/ds = q / (n s); the toy records the largest lambda it meets on
every run and asserts DT * lambda_max < 2.
"""
import math
import random

SEEDS = (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)
DT = 1e-4                 # s
K_E = 0.0333              # mm filament per mm of path (0.4 x 0.2 mm bead / 2.405 mm^2 of 1.75 mm filament)
BIN = 0.5                 # mm of path per width bin
CAL_SPEED = 100.0         # mm/s: the tuning tower runs at high flow (Klipper's advice)
TOWER_STEP = 0.005        # s: K resolution of the tower
OVER = 3.0                # C_over uses 3 x the tuned K
NUM_ZERO = 1e-9           # |m| and sd both below this: floating-point residue, not a gap

# name: (cruise mm/s, accel mm/s^2, corner mm/s, corners, edge mm)
REGIMES = {
    "sharp_corner": (100.0, 3000.0, 5.0, 4, 30.0),
    "slow_corner": (25.0, 500.0, 5.0, 4, 30.0),
    "steady_straight": (60.0, 3000.0, 60.0, 1, 120.0),
}
ARMS = ("A_K0", "B_tuned", "C_over")


def shared_draws(regime, seed):
    """Common random numbers: one melt, one tower reading, one filament for all arms."""
    rng = random.Random(f"{regime}/{seed}")
    k_true = rng.uniform(0.04, 0.08)          # s, direct-drive PLA stand-in
    n = rng.uniform(0.75, 1.0)
    read_err = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.15)           # relative error reading the tower
    dia = [rng.gauss(0.0, 0.004) for _ in range(2000)]   # relative filament-diameter error per bin
    return k_true, n, read_err, dia


def tuned_k(k_true, n, read_err):
    # Local slope ds/dq at the tower's flow is n * K_true; the eye adds read_err, the tower quantizes.
    return max(0.0, round(n * k_true * (1.0 + read_err) / TOWER_STEP) * TOWER_STEP)


def speed_profile(cruise, accel, corner, corners, edge):
    """Planner stand-in: per edge accelerate from `corner` (or cruise at the start) to cruise, brake to `corner`."""
    vs = []
    for c in range(corners):
        v = cruise if c == 0 else corner
        end = corner if c < corners - 1 else cruise
        x = 0.0
        while x < edge:
            rem = edge - x
            v_next = min(cruise, v + accel * DT, math.sqrt(end * end + 2.0 * accel * max(rem - v * DT, 0.0)))
            vs.append(v)
            x += v * DT
            v = v_next
    vs.append(v)
    return vs


def flow(s, q_ref, s_ref, n):
    return 0.0 if s <= 0.0 else q_ref * (s / s_ref) ** (1.0 / n)


def run(regime, seed, arm):
    cruise, accel, corner, corners, edge = REGIMES[regime]
    k_true, n, read_err, dia = shared_draws(regime, seed)
    kb = tuned_k(k_true, n, read_err)
    k = {"A_K0": 0.0, "B_tuned": kb, "C_over": OVER * kb}[arm]
    q_ref = K_E * CAL_SPEED
    s_ref = k_true * q_ref
    vs = speed_profile(cruise, accel, corner, corners, edge)
    s = s_ref * ((K_E * vs[0]) / q_ref) ** n   # start in steady state at the first speed
    x, dep, lam_max = 0.0, {}, 0.0
    feed_min, feed_max = float("inf"), float("-inf")
    for i in range(len(vs) - 1):
        v, v_next = vs[i], vs[i + 1]
        feed = K_E * v * DT + k * K_E * (v_next - v)
        feed_min, feed_max = min(feed_min, feed / DT), max(feed_max, feed / DT)
        q = flow(s, q_ref, s_ref, n)
        if s > 0.0:
            lam_max = max(lam_max, q / (n * s))
        b = int(x / BIN)
        dep[b] = dep.get(b, 0.0) + q * DT
        s += feed - q * DT
        x += v * DT
    assert DT * lam_max < 2.0, (regime, seed, arm, lam_max)
    nb = int(x / BIN)
    rel = [dep.get(b, 0.0) * (1.0 + dia[b % len(dia)]) ** 2 / (K_E * BIN) - 1.0 for b in range(nb)]
    return {
        "bulge": 100.0 * max(0.0, max(rel)),
        "starve": 100.0 * max(0.0, -min(rel)),
        "rms": 100.0 * math.sqrt(sum(r * r for r in rel) / len(rel)),
        "lam": lam_max, "k": k, "emin": feed_min, "emax": feed_max,
    }


def mean_sd(xs):
    mu = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    return mu, math.sqrt(sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1))


def num(x):
    if x != 0.0 and abs(x) < 5e-4:
        return f"{x:.3e}"
    return f"{x:.4f}"


def sign_note(diffs, m):
    n, opp, zero = len(diffs), sum(1 for d in diffs if d * m < 0), sum(1 for d in diffs if d == 0)
    if opp == 0 and zero == 0:
        return f"same sign on all {n} seeds"
    if opp == 0:
        return f"no seed has the opposite sign, {zero} of {n} equal"
    note = f"{opp} of {n} seeds {'has' if opp == 1 else 'have'} the opposite sign"
    return note + (f", {zero} of {n} equal" if zero else "")


def fmt_pair(m, s):
    return f"{num(m)}+-{num(s)}"


def verdict(xa, xb):
    diffs = [a - b for a, b in zip(xa, xb)]
    if all(d == 0.0 for d in diffs):
        return "0.0000+-0.0000: tie: identical on every seed"
    m, s = mean_sd(diffs)
    if abs(m) < NUM_ZERO and s < NUM_ZERO:
        return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: numerically zero (floating-point residue), effectively a tie"
    if abs(m) <= s:
        label = "near-tie / small gap, within seed noise (|m| <= sd)"
    elif abs(m) < 0.2 * min(abs(mean_sd(xa)[0]), abs(mean_sd(xb)[0])):
        label = "small but consistent gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    else:
        label = "clear gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: {label}; {sign_note(diffs, m)}"


def main():
    print("Synthetic 1-D compressible-melt pressure-advance toy; arms differ only in K; shared melt / tower / filament draws; not a real printer")
    print(f"DT={DT} s K_E={K_E} mm/mm bin={BIN} mm; tower at {CAL_SPEED:.0f} mm/s, step {TOWER_STEP} s; C_over = {OVER:.0f} x tuned K; seeds={len(SEEDS)}")
    res = {r: {a: [run(r, s, a) for s in SEEDS] for a in ARMS} for r in REGIMES}
    lam = max(x["lam"] for r in res.values() for a in r.values() for x in a)
    print(f"Euler stability: largest lambda = dq/ds met on any run {lam:.2f} 1/s -> need DT < 2/lambda_max = {2.0 / lam * 1000:.2f} ms; DT={DT * 1000:.1f} ms ok")
    for seed in SEEDS:
        k_true, n, read_err, _ = shared_draws("sharp_corner", seed)
        print(f"  sharp_corner seed {seed}: K_true={k_true:.4f} s n={n:.3f} local slope n*K_true={n * k_true:.4f} s tuned K={tuned_k(k_true, n, read_err):.3f} s")
    print("regime arm bulge_% starve_% rms_width_% K_s  extruder_feed_min..max_mm/s  (mean+-sd over seeds)")
    for r, (cruise, accel, corner, corners, edge) in REGIMES.items():
        print(f"{r} (cruise {cruise:.0f} mm/s, accel {accel:.0f} mm/s^2, corner {corner:.0f} mm/s, {corners} edge(s) x {edge:.0f} mm)")
        for a in ARMS:
            xs = res[r][a]
            cols = " ".join(f"{num(mean_sd([x[m] for x in xs])[0])}+-{num(mean_sd([x[m] for x in xs])[1])}" for m in ("bulge", "starve", "rms"))
            kk = mean_sd([x["k"] for x in xs])[0]
            print(f"  {a} {cols} K={kk:.4f} feed {min(x['emin'] for x in xs):.2f}..{max(x['emax'] for x in xs):.2f}")
        for other in ("A_K0", "C_over"):
            for m in ("bulge", "starve", "rms"):
                xa, xb = [x[m] for x in res[r][other]], [x[m] for x in res[r]["B_tuned"]]
                print(f"  paired {other[0]}-B {m}: {verdict(xa, xb)}")
    print("No winner declared: advance only acts on speed changes; a tuned K shrinks corner bulge and an over-tuned K starves the line; the melt is a 1-D stand-in")
    print("Real printer / tuning tower: 本页未运行; Marlin / Klipper numbers 以原文为准")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

### Read the regimes

How the labels are used on this page, in this order: **tie: identical on every seed** only when the paired per-seed difference is exactly zero on every seed; **numerically zero (floating-point residue), effectively a tie** when `|paired mean|` and its sd are both below 1e-9 (no sign count is printed on such a line); **near-tie / small gap, within seed noise** when `|paired mean| ≤ sd`; otherwise a **consistent** gap (`|paired mean| > sd`), called **small** if `|m|` is under 20 % of the smaller arm mean and **clear** otherwise; the 20 % rule only chooses between those two words. Every consistent line carries a same-sign count over the 5 seeds, and any seed with the opposite sign is reported. Gaps are computed from unrounded values.

- **sharp_corner:** bulge A 191.2359±9.4373 %, B 25.3353±17.0094 %, C 26.3638±5.5818 %. **A loses with a clear gap:** paired A−B bulge 165.9007±18.7792 and RMS width 23.9144±1.8771, both same sign on all 5 seeds. On starve A−B is 19.2602±12.3244, clear, but 1 of 5 seeds has the opposite sign: on seed 4 the tower was read high (0.090 s against a local slope of 0.0781 s), so B pulled a little too much back. **C loses with a clear gap:** paired C−B starve 89.2661±10.3383 (C's line nearly breaks, 99.7746±0.4874 % deficit) and RMS 15.6032±3.0591, both same sign on all 5 seeds. C also asks the extruder for −27.56 to 30.29 mm/s, against −9.06 to 12.30 for B; a real extruder may not have that torque (not modelled).
- **slow_corner:** the same order with smaller numbers. A−B bulge 49.8740±8.5664, starve 16.3530±2.8234, RMS 6.8057±1.0347, all clear and same sign on all 5 seeds; C−B starve 49.7598±13.7362, clear, same sign on all 5 seeds; C−B bulge 4.5254±14.9714 is a **near-tie**. A slow corner still bulges without advance, because the 5 mm/s corner is slow compared with the melt's lag.
- **steady_straight:** at constant speed the advance term is `K × dv = 0` on every step, so all three arms feed the same filament: **tie: identical on every seed** on every metric, for A−B and C−B alike. The remaining 0.9958±0.0275 % RMS is the filament-diameter error, which no K touches. This tie is structural, not tuned: pressure advance only acts where the speed changes.

## Build orientation: flatwise versus upright

<Resource id="rajkumar-fdm-orientation" />

The design section above already says it in one line: a hook printed standing up snaps at a layer line, and the same hook printed lying down bends. This section puts that rule on a shared-geometry toy and on one paper. Santosh Rajkumar, *Effect of infill pattern and build orientation on mechanical properties of FDM printed parts: An experimental modal analysis approach* (arXiv 2202.05692; the abs page lists **one** author, Santosh Rajkumar, and the PDF byline reads Santosh Mohan Rajkumar; abs and PDF body read), prints 100 × 15 × 5 mm PLA beams in **flatwise, edgewise and upright** orientations with six infill patterns, five specimens each, and tests them in three-point bending. What the body reports, all **以论文为准**: cubic infill printed edgewise has the highest maximum flexural stress, 107.95 MPa; triangles printed upright the lowest, 62.32 MPa; across infills the **median** maximum flexural stress is highest flatwise, and upright "tend to have much lower" maximum flexural stress; flatwise also tends to the higher flexural modulus and upright to the lower; triangles–flatwise has the highest modulus, 3.36 GPa, and **zigzag–edgewise the lowest, 2.185 GPa** (以论文为准, per the paper); edgewise tends to the higher maximum flexural **strain**, which is a ductility reading, not a strength reading. The paper is a single-author graduate study with one printer and one material; none of its MPa or GPa values is reprinted below as a toy output.

The mechanism is the layer bond. When a beam printed upright is bent, the tensile stress runs **across** the layers, so the weaker inter-layer bond carries it; printed flatwise, the same stress runs **along** the deposited roads. This section is **not** the pressure-advance corner toy, **not** the snap-fit strain formula and **not** the wet-nylon note.

<Formula label="Three-point bending (the paper's beam theory)">σ = 3 P L / (2 w t²),   d = P L³ / (48 E I),   I = w t³ / 12</Formula>

**Worked example — a labelled hand calculation, also printed by the toy.** P = 220.0 N on L = 80.0 mm, w = 15.0 mm, t = 5.0 mm: σ = 3 × 220 × 80 / (2 × 15 × 5²) = 70.4000 MPa. Against the toy's flatwise strength of 90.0 MPa the utilisation is 0.7822; against the toy's upright strength of 55.0 MPa it is 1.2800, so the upright beam snaps. I = 15 × 5³ / 12 = 156.2500 mm⁴, and at P = 100.0 N with E = 3.2 GPa the mid-span deflection is 2.1333 mm.

**Boundary.** A part snaps if σ ≥ S and survives **if and only if** σ < S (**strict**): the toy prints snap = 1 at σ = S and snap = 0 when S is 1e-6 MPa higher.

**No integration step here.** The toy is closed-form statics: no time stepping, so no step-size criterion applies.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""FDM build-orientation anisotropy: flatwise vs upright vs an isotropic guess.

Synthetic three-point-bending beam, stdlib only.  Not a tensile lab, not an
Instron run, not a modal-hammer test, not the pressure-advance corner toy on
this page and not a moisture / wet-nylon section.
Real printer / Instron / modal hammer: 本页未运行.  Rajkumar (arXiv
2202.05692) statements and MPa / GPa values 以论文为准; none of them is
reprinted below as a toy output.

What the cited paper body says (and what this toy keeps): PLA beams printed
flatwise, edgewise and upright were tested in three-point bending.  Upright
tends to much lower maximum flexural stress (the load opens the layer
lines, so the inter-layer bond carries it); flatwise tends to higher median
maximum flexural stress and higher flexural modulus; one edgewise cell
(zigzag infill) has the lowest flexural modulus.  The paper's beam-theory
equations, with P the load, L the span, w the width and t the thickness:
  sigma = 3 P L / (2 w t^2)        d = P L^3 / (48 E I)        I = w t^3 / 12

Common random numbers: for each (regime, seed) one load jitter, one shared
print-quality factor on strength and one on modulus are drawn once and
shared by every arm (same spool, same printer, same day).  Arms:
  A_flatwise  : strength S_FLAT, modulus E_FLAT
  B_upright   : strength S_UP (inter-layer bond), modulus E_UP
  C_isotropic : one number for every orientation (S_FLAT, E_FLAT) used to
                predict the upright part - the naive "datasheet" guess
  D_edgewise  : strength S_EDGE, modulus E_EDGE (the "print it on its side" rule)
The arms differ only in the orientation-dependent strength and modulus;
geometry and load are shared, so the peak stress is the same for every arm.
A part snaps iff sigma >= S; it survives iff sigma < S (strict).
No integration step: closed-form statics, no time stepping.

Regimes and parameters were written before the first run:
  light   : load well below every strength - A vs B snap flag tie (both
            survive); keep
  mid     : load between the upright and flatwise strengths - B consistent
            snaps where A holds; C (isotropic) misses the snap
  modulus : zigzag-like parameter set, light load - the edgewise rule D can
            lose on stiffness (deflects more than A); keep
All strengths and moduli are declared toy parameters, ordered after the
paper's qualitative statements; they are not the paper's measured values.
"""
import math
import random
from decimal import ROUND_HALF_UP, Decimal, getcontext

getcontext().rounding = ROUND_HALF_UP

SEEDS = (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)
NUM_ZERO = 1e-9

# Declared parameters, written before the first run.  Toy values.
SPAN = 80.0       # mm, support span (toy)
WIDTH = 15.0      # mm, beam width (Rajkumar's 100 x 15 x 5 mm beam; 以论文为准)
THICK = 5.0       # mm, beam thickness
P_JITTER = 0.05   # +/- fraction on the load, shared
Q_JITTER = 0.08   # +/- fraction print quality on strength, shared
R_JITTER = 0.05   # +/- fraction print quality on modulus, shared
SETS = {
    # strengths in MPa, moduli in GPa (toy stand-ins, not paper values)
    "default": {"S": {"flat": 90.0, "edge": 85.0, "up": 55.0}, "E": {"flat": 3.2, "edge": 3.0, "up": 2.7}},
    "zigzag_like": {"S": {"flat": 80.0, "edge": 78.0, "up": 50.0}, "E": {"flat": 3.0, "edge": 2.2, "up": 2.5}},
}
REGIMES = {
    "light": {"p": 100.0, "set": "default"},
    "mid": {"p": 220.0, "set": "default"},
    "modulus": {"p": 100.0, "set": "zigzag_like"},
}
ARMS = ("A_flatwise", "B_upright", "C_isotropic", "D_edgewise")
ORIENT = {"A_flatwise": "flat", "B_upright": "up", "C_isotropic": "flat", "D_edgewise": "edge"}


def second_moment(w=WIDTH, t=THICK):
    return w * t ** 3 / 12.0


def peak_stress(p, span=SPAN, w=WIDTH, t=THICK):
    return 3.0 * p * span / (2.0 * w * t ** 2)


def deflection(p, e_gpa, span=SPAN, w=WIDTH, t=THICK):
    return p * span ** 3 / (48.0 * e_gpa * 1000.0 * second_moment(w, t))


def shared_draws(regime, seed):
    """Common random numbers: one load, one strength factor, one modulus factor."""
    rng = random.Random(f"{regime}/{seed}")
    dp = rng.uniform(-P_JITTER, P_JITTER)
    q = 1.0 + rng.uniform(-Q_JITTER, Q_JITTER)
    r = 1.0 + rng.uniform(-R_JITTER, R_JITTER)
    return REGIMES[regime]["p"] * (1.0 + dp), q, r


def run_arm(arm, regime, seed, span=SPAN, p_scale=1.0, s_up=None):
    p, q, r = shared_draws(regime, seed)
    p *= p_scale
    params = SETS[REGIMES[regime]["set"]]
    o = ORIENT[arm]
    s = params["S"][o]
    if o == "up" and s_up is not None:
        s = s_up
    strength, modulus = s * q, params["E"][o] * r
    sigma = peak_stress(p, span)
    return {"sigma": sigma, "util": sigma / strength, "snap": 1.0 if sigma >= strength else 0.0,
            "defl": deflection(p, modulus, span)}


def mean_sd(xs):
    if all(x == xs[0] for x in xs):
        return xs[0], 0.0  # identical values: no floating residue in mean or sd
    mu = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    return mu, math.sqrt(sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1))


def num(x):
    """Half-up rounding on the shortest decimal repr; tiny non-zero values in scientific form."""
    if x != 0.0 and abs(x) < 5e-4:
        return format(Decimal(repr(x)), ".3e")
    s = format(Decimal(repr(x)), ".4f")
    return "0.0000" if s == "-0.0000" else s


def sign_note(diffs, m):
    n, opp, zero = len(diffs), sum(1 for d in diffs if d * m < 0), sum(1 for d in diffs if d == 0)
    if opp == 0 and zero == 0:
        return f"same sign on all {n} seeds"
    if opp == 0:
        return f"no seed has the opposite sign, {zero} of {n} equal"
    note = f"{opp} of {n} seeds {'has' if opp == 1 else 'have'} the opposite sign"
    return note + (f", {zero} of {n} equal" if zero else "")


def fmt_pair(m, s):
    return f"{num(m)}+-{num(s)}"


def verdict(xa, xb):
    diffs = [a - b for a, b in zip(xa, xb)]
    if all(d == 0.0 for d in diffs):
        return "0.0000+-0.0000: tie: identical on every seed"
    m, s = mean_sd(diffs)
    if abs(m) < NUM_ZERO and s < NUM_ZERO:
        return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: numerically zero (floating-point residue), effectively a tie"
    if abs(m) <= s:
        label = "near-tie / small gap, within seed noise (|m| <= sd)"
    elif abs(m) < 0.2 * min(abs(mean_sd(xa)[0]), abs(mean_sd(xb)[0])):
        label = "small but consistent gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    else:
        label = "clear gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: {label}; {sign_note(diffs, m)}"


METRICS = ("sigma", "util", "snap", "defl")


def collect(regime, **kw):
    return {arm: [run_arm(arm, regime, s, **kw) for s in SEEDS] for arm in ARMS}


def col(rows, key):
    return [r[key] for r in rows]


def report(regime):
    cfg = REGIMES[regime]
    res = collect(regime)
    print(f"{regime} (P_nom={num(cfg['p'])} N, sigma_nom={num(peak_stress(cfg['p']))} MPa, parameter set {cfg['set']}):")
    for arm in ARMS:
        rows = res[arm]
        print(f"  {arm} " + " ".join(f"{key} {fmt_pair(*mean_sd(col(rows, key)))}" for key in METRICS)
              + f" snaps_on {int(sum(col(rows, 'snap')))}/5")
    for a, b, keys in (("B_upright", "A_flatwise", ("snap", "util")), ("B_upright", "C_isotropic", ("snap", "util")),
                       ("D_edgewise", "A_flatwise", ("defl", "util")), ("A_flatwise", "B_upright", ("sigma",))):
        for key in keys:
            print(f"  paired {a[0]}-{b[0]} {key}: {verdict(col(res[a], key), col(res[b], key))}")
    return res


def main():
    print("Synthetic FDM build-orientation toy; shared geometry, load and print-quality draws; not a lab measurement")
    print(f"seeds={len(SEEDS)} SPAN={num(SPAN)} mm WIDTH={num(WIDTH)} mm THICK={num(THICK)} mm P_JITTER=+-{num(P_JITTER)} "
          f"Q_JITTER=+-{num(Q_JITTER)} R_JITTER=+-{num(R_JITTER)} (declared toy parameters)")
    for name, ps in SETS.items():
        print(f"set {name}: S flat/edge/up = {num(ps['S']['flat'])}/{num(ps['S']['edge'])}/{num(ps['S']['up'])} MPa; "
              f"E flat/edge/up = {num(ps['E']['flat'])}/{num(ps['E']['edge'])}/{num(ps['E']['up'])} GPa (toy stand-ins, not paper values)")
    print("No integration step: closed-form statics, no time-step criterion")
    print("Regimes and parameters were written before the first run")
    print("rule: snaps iff sigma >= S; survives iff sigma < S (strict); sigma = 3PL/(2wt^2), d = PL^3/(48EI), I = wt^3/12")
    # Labelled hand calculation on one orientation, printed.
    i2 = second_moment()
    print(f"hand_calc flatwise P=220.0 N L=80.0 mm w=15.0 mm t=5.0 mm: sigma=3*220*80/(2*15*5^2)={num(peak_stress(220.0))} MPa; "
          f"util vs S_FLAT 90.0 MPa = {num(peak_stress(220.0) / 90.0)}; vs S_UP 55.0 MPa = {num(peak_stress(220.0) / 55.0)}")
    print(f"hand_calc I=15*5^3/12={num(i2)} mm^4; d at P=100.0 N, E=3.2 GPa: 100*80^3/(48*3200*{num(i2)})={num(deflection(100.0, 3.2))} mm")
    s_eq = peak_stress(220.0)
    print(f"boundary_rule: S=sigma={num(s_eq)} MPa -> snap={1 if s_eq >= s_eq else 0} (equality snaps); "
          f"S=sigma+1e-6 -> snap={1 if s_eq >= s_eq + 1e-6 else 0}")
    for regime in REGIMES:
        report(regime)
    base = collect("mid")
    print("alternative SPAN=60.0 mm on mid (same seeds; lower peak stress at the same load):")
    alt = collect("mid", span=60.0)
    print(f"  B_upright@alt snaps_on {int(sum(col(alt['B_upright'], 'snap')))}/5 (default {int(sum(col(base['B_upright'], 'snap')))}/5)")
    print(f"  paired B-A snap@alt: {verdict(col(alt['B_upright'], 'snap'), col(alt['A_flatwise'], 'snap'))}")
    print("alternative S_UP=65.0 MPa on mid (same seeds; a better inter-layer bond):")
    alt = collect("mid", s_up=65.0)
    print(f"  B_upright@alt snaps_on {int(sum(col(alt['B_upright'], 'snap')))}/5")
    print(f"  paired B-A snap@alt: {verdict(col(alt['B_upright'], 'snap'), col(alt['A_flatwise'], 'snap'))}")
    print("alternative load x0.80 on mid (same seeds; P_nom 176.0 N):")
    alt = collect("mid", p_scale=0.80)
    print(f"  B_upright@alt snaps_on {int(sum(col(alt['B_upright'], 'snap')))}/5")
    print(f"  paired B-A snap@alt: {verdict(col(alt['B_upright'], 'snap'), col(alt['A_flatwise'], 'snap'))}")
    print("No winner declared: flatwise is the safer default for bending here, upright snaps first at the layer lines, "
          "an isotropic guess hides that, and the side-on rule can cost stiffness")
    print("Real printer / Instron / modal hammer: 本页未运行; Rajkumar (arXiv 2202.05692) values 以论文为准")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

### Read the regimes

How the labels are used on this page, in this order: **tie: identical on every seed** only when the paired per-seed difference is exactly zero on every seed; **numerically zero (floating-point residue), effectively a tie** when `|paired mean|` and its sd are both below 1e-9; **near-tie / small gap, within seed noise** when `|paired mean| ≤ sd`; otherwise a **consistent** gap, called **small** if `|m|` is under 20 % of the smaller arm mean and **clear** otherwise. Every consistent line carries a same-sign count over the 5 seeds.

- **light.** No arm snaps on any seed: paired B−A snap is a **tie: identical on every seed**, as pre-declared. The **tie** is only on the go/no-go flag: the upright beam is still working harder (paired B−A util 0.2330±0.0133, clear, same sign on all 5 seeds), so a light-load pass says nothing about margin.

**Key points**

- First print: print the manufacturer's test model first and watch the first layer to the end. Rounded lines with gaps mean the nozzle is too high, lines squished flat with ridges mean it is too low, and an even surface with no gaps and no ridges is correct.
- Calibration order (OrcaSlicer): temperature → maximum volumetric speed → pressure advance → flow ratio → retraction, then tolerance; each test assumes the earlier ones are right.
- Flow ratio scales every extrusion (keep it within 0.95–1.05); pressure advance compensates for the pressure lag in the nozzle; retraction pulls the filament back before a travel move to stop strings.
- Heat-set inserts: size the hole from the insert datasheet and test it on a printed block; set the iron 10–20 °C above the printing temperature and press the last part flush with a tool.
- Hole = shaft + 2 × per-side clearance: a 5 mm shaft with 0.2 mm per side needs a 5.4 mm hole.
- Overhangs up to about 45° from vertical usually print without support.
- Overhang limit from bead geometry: tan θ ≤ w / (2 × h), which gives 45° for a 0.4 mm line on 0.2 mm layers; Tg, not the melting point, limits a part in service (PLA Tg 55–60 °C, PETG Tg 80 °C but heat deflection at 70 °C under 0.455 MPa).
- Snap strain ε = 1.5 × y × t / L²: with L = 10 mm, t = 1.2 mm and y = 0.8 mm it gives 1.44 %, inside the 1.5 % PLA limit.
- Break-even: $7.60 saved per revision, so a $219 printer needs about 29 revisions to pay for itself.
- Pressure advance: on a 100 mm/s corner a tuned K cuts the bulge by 165.9007±18.7792 points against K = 0 (a clear gap, same sign on all 5 seeds), 3 × that K starves the line instead (C−B starve 89.2661±10.3383, clear), and on a constant-speed straight all three **tie** (identical on every seed) — a synthetic melt, not a printer run.
- Build orientation: on a shared 15 × 5 mm beam a light load passes in every orientation (snap **tie**), a mid load snaps the upright print where flatwise holds (B−A snap 1.0000±0.0000, clear, same sign on all 5 seeds) and an isotropic guess misses it, and a zigzag-like edgewise print loses on stiffness (D−A defl 0.8295±0.0478, clear, kept) — toy parameters, not Rajkumar's measured values.

**Common mistakes**

- Copying a total-difference gauge number into a per-side clearance parameter, which makes every gap twice as big as measured.
- Drawing the hole as shaft + clearance only once: 5.2 mm instead of 5.4 mm leaves just 0.1 mm per side.
- Drawing a short thick snap arm: at L = 5 mm the strain is 9.00 % and the part cracks, while making the arm thicker raises both strain and push-on force.
- Choosing PLA for a part pressed against a 70 °C motor, when PLA softens around 55–60 °C.
