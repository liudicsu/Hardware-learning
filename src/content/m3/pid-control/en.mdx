A joint is told to go to 60°. It goes to 55.5° and stops, holding the arm against gravity but never reaching the mark. A balancer is told to stay in one place. It balances perfectly and then rolls slowly across the floor until it hits something. Both failures have the same root cause: a controller that reacts to <Term id="error">error</Term> can be stable, smooth, and still wrong.

## What P, I and D each do

Every feedback controller in this course does the same four things, forever: measure the thing you care about, compare it with the target, compute the error, and act on the error. Then it does it again, at a fixed rate, whether or not the last correction worked. The controller never decides that it has arrived; it only keeps reducing the error it can see.

The textbook PID law is one line:

```text
output = Kp × error + Ki × (sum of error × dt) + Kd × (rate of change)
```

Three terms, three jobs. The <Term id="proportional-term">proportional term</Term> P pushes in proportion to how far off you are: double the error, double the push. The <Term id="integral-term">integral term</Term> I adds up error over time, so a small error that never goes away eventually becomes a large command; it is the term that keeps pushing until a <Term id="steady-state-error">steady error</Term> is gone, for example the gravity load on an arm joint. The <Term id="derivative-term">derivative term</Term> D reacts to how fast the measurement is changing and brakes the approach: when the joint is moving toward the target quickly, D subtracts from the command so it does not sail past.

In the mass–spring–damper model, P is the spring (force proportional to displacement), D is the damper (force proportional to velocity), and I is a constant bias torque proportional to the accumulated error. A spring alone lets the system oscillate, and it can settle short of the target. A damper alone produces no force at rest. The integral term keeps supplying that bias torque, and the remaining steady error disappears once the accumulated torque matches the load.

Why does P alone leave an error when there is a constant load? Because the controller can only make torque by having an error. If the joint needs 0.227 N·m to hold the arm against gravity, then at rest the motor must produce 0.227 N·m, and a P controller produces Kp × error. Set those equal and solve:

```text
Kp = 0.05 N·m per degree
error = 0.227 / 0.05 = 4.5°
arm stops at 60 − 4.5 = 55.5°
```

The arm is not broken and the controller is not unstable. It has found the only angle where its push balances gravity. Increase Kp and the error shrinks, but it does not become zero; only an integral term can supply torque at zero error.

Two practical details change a textbook PID into one that works on a robot. First, take the derivative of the measurement, not of the error. If D differentiates the error and the target jumps from 0° to 60°, the error jumps too, and the derivative sees a huge rate for one sample. The controller answers with a violent derivative kick to the motor, even though nothing physical has moved. Differentiating the measurement removes that kick: a target step changes the error, but not the measured angle, so D stays quiet. The code in this lesson uses the measurement difference divided by `dt` and subtracts `kd × rate`. Second, run the loop at a fixed rate, using the timing discipline from Month 2 Lesson 2. A PID assumes that `dt` is the same every pass; if the loop sometimes takes 5 ms and sometimes 15 ms, the integral and derivative terms are calculated with the wrong time and the gains mean something different from one moment to the next.

## One joint, four controllers

The best way to see what the terms do is to run four controllers on the same joint. The model below is one arm joint, like an SO-101 shoulder with a short arm. It starts horizontal at 0° and is asked to lift to 60°. Gravity pulls down with 0.4 N·m × cos(angle), the motor can give at most 1 N·m, there is a little viscous friction, and the controller runs at 200 Hz. The gains are in the model's own units, not PWM values to copy into firmware.

<CodeSource path="tools/sim/models/pid_sim.py" />

```python
"""pid_sim.py: P, PI, PID and integral windup on one arm joint lifting against gravity.

Toy model of a single joint (think of an SO-101 shoulder with a short arm): the motor
torque is limited, gravity pulls the arm down by 0.4 N*m * cos(angle), and there is a
little friction. The controller runs at 200 Hz and is asked to move the joint from
0 degrees (horizontal) to 60 degrees at t = 0.
"""
import math

J = 0.004          # kg*m^2, arm inertia seen at the joint
B = 0.05           # N*m per rad/s, viscous friction
GRAV = 0.4         # N*m, gravity torque with the arm horizontal
MAX_TORQUE = 1.0   # N*m the motor can give
DT = 0.005         # 200 Hz control loop


def run(kp, ki=0.0, kd=0.0, anti_windup=True, target=60.0, seconds=3.0):
    q, w, integral, t = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0       # angle (rad), speed (rad/s)
    peak, settle = 0.0, None
    prev = q
    while t < seconds:
        error = target - math.degrees(q)
        rate = (math.degrees(q) - math.degrees(prev)) / DT   # derivative of the measurement
        prev = q
        unclipped = kp * error + ki * integral - kd * rate
        torque = max(-MAX_TORQUE, min(MAX_TORQUE, unclipped))
        if not anti_windup or torque == unclipped:            # only integrate while not saturated
            integral += error * DT
        for _ in range(5):                                    # physics at 1 ms
            alpha = (torque - B * w - GRAV * math.cos(q)) / J
            w += alpha * 0.001
            q += w * 0.001
        t += DT
        deg = math.degrees(q)
        peak = max(peak, deg)
        if abs(deg - target) > 1.0:
            settle = None
        elif settle is None:
            settle = t
    over = max(0.0, peak - target)
    s = f"{settle:.2f} s" if settle is not None else "never"
    return f"final {math.degrees(q):5.1f} deg, overshoot {over:4.1f} deg, within 1 deg from {s}"


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("P    kp 0.05                  ", run(0.05))
    print("P    kp 0.2                   ", run(0.2))
    print("PD   kp 0.05, kd 0.002        ", run(0.05, kd=0.002))
    print("PI   kp 0.05, ki 0.1          ", run(0.05, ki=0.1))
    print("PID  kp 0.05, ki 0.1, kd 0.002", run(0.05, ki=0.1, kd=0.002))
    print("  ...same, no anti-windup     ", run(0.05, ki=0.1, kd=0.002, anti_windup=False))
    print("PID  kp 0.05, ki 0.4, kd 0.002", run(0.05, ki=0.4, kd=0.002))
    print("  ...same, no anti-windup     ", run(0.05, ki=0.4, kd=0.002, anti_windup=False))
```

Read the output one line at a time. P with Kp = 0.05 stops at 55.5°, exactly the 4.5° shortfall calculated above, and <Term id="overshoot">overshoots</Term> 10.8° on the way: the arm has inertia, so it carries past the target before the proportional term pulls it back. P with Kp = 0.2 gets closer, 59.0°, because at rest its error is about 1°, where 0.2 × 1.0° = 0.2 N·m nearly matches gravity at 59°: 0.4 × cos 59° = 0.4 × 0.515 = 0.206 N·m. The two are almost equal, so the arm stops there. It never gets inside the 1° band because an error of about 1° is exactly what Kp = 0.2 needs to make 0.2 N·m; the balance point lands just outside that line. Raise Kp further and the final error shrinks, but the overshoot grows: the same proportional term that pushes harder also stores more energy in the approach.

PD adds Kd = 0.002 and removes the overshoot completely (0.0°), but the final angle is still 55.5°. That is the important lesson about D: it acts only while something is moving. Once the arm is still, the rate is zero, D contributes zero, and the steady error is exactly where P left it. D improves the journey; it does not fix the destination.

PI adds Ki = 0.1 and reaches 60.0°, with 11.5° of overshoot and within 1° from 0.92 s on, its <Term id="settling-time">settling time</Term> for a 1° band. The integral eventually supplies the gravity torque at zero error, so the destination is right; the cost is that the integral keeps pushing while the arm is arriving, so it overshoots. PID combines all three: Kp 0.05, Ki 0.1, Kd 0.002 reaches 60.0° with 0.1° overshoot and is within 1° from 0.24 s. For this joint it is clearly the best of the set: the integral fixes the destination, the derivative controls the arrival, and P does the bulk of the work.

### Where the model's numbers come from

Each constant in the model stands for something you can measure or look up on a real joint.

- **Gravity torque, 0.4 N·m.** Gravity's torque about the joint is the weight of the moving part times the horizontal distance from the axis to its centre of mass, τ = m × g × r. It is largest with the link horizontal and falls with cos(angle) as the link rises, which is the cos term in the model. On a real joint you can measure it: with the servo unpowered, hold the link horizontal by resting its tip on a kitchen scale at a distance L from the axis, convert the reading to a force, F = reading in kg × 9.81 m/s², and multiply, τ = F × L. An unpowered gearbox still has friction and can partly hold the link by itself, so tap the link lightly before each reading, take several readings, and use their average. A reading of 0.204 kg at L = 0.2 m is 2.0 N at 0.2 m, which is 0.40 N·m.
- **Inertia, 0.004 kg·m².** For a compact load the moment of inertia from Lesson 3 is close to J = m × r². The two constants describe one load: 0.42 kg with its centre of mass 0.098 m from the axis gives J = 0.42 × 0.098² = 0.0040 kg·m² and τ = 0.42 × 9.81 × 0.098 = 0.40 N·m. A real link is spread out rather than concentrated at one point, so m × r² is a lower bound; a CAD tool's mass-properties report gives the full value once the material density is set. Through the gearbox the motor's own rotor adds n² × J_rotor (Lesson 3), and that has to be added to the link's value.
- **Motor limit, 1 N·m.** The STS3215 servo on the SO-101 has a stall torque of 19.5 kg·cm at 7.4 V and 16.5 kg·cm at 6 V (Lesson 5; Feetech's specification, item 5-4), which is 1.91 N·m and 1.62 N·m: the most it produces at zero speed while it draws its largest current. Its rated load, the torque it is meant to carry continuously, is 5 kg·cm at 7.4 V and 4 kg·cm at 6 V (item 5-8), about 0.49 N·m and 0.39 N·m, and the standard build supplies 5 V. At 5 V, budget an upper bound of ≤0.4 N·m for a continuous load: Feetech gives no 5 V rating, and scaling the 6 V rating linearly with voltage gives about 0.33 N·m (0.39 × 5 / 6), which is an unverified estimate. The model's gravity torque of 0.4 N·m is already close to this upper bound. The 1 N·m limit in the model is therefore an optimistic short-term peak between rated and stall, a torque the servo can deliver for a brief correction but cannot hold.
- **Friction, 0.05 N·m per rad/s.** Viscous friction is the one constant you cannot look up. In this model it is an assumed value that gives the joint a little damping; for a real joint it is fitted by recording a step response and adjusting B until the model's response matches.
- **Loop rate, 200 Hz.** The rate follows from how fast the closed loop moves. With proportional gain alone the joint behaves like a mass on a spring of stiffness Kp, and its natural frequency, the rate at which it would oscillate without friction, is ωn = √(Kp / J). Kp = 0.05 N·m per degree is 0.05 × 180 / π = 2.86 N·m per radian, so ωn = √(2.86 / 0.004) = 26.8 rad/s, or 4.26 Hz. A common rule of thumb for digital control is a sample rate of 20 to 60 times ωn ([Real-Time Computing, closed-loop control](https://rtcbook.org/closed-loop-control/summary/)), here 535 to 1606 rad/s, which is 85 to 256 Hz. 200 Hz is 1257 rad/s, 47 times ωn, inside that range.

## Integral windup

The motor limit is where the integral term gets into trouble. At the start the error is 60°, and P alone asks for 0.05 × 60 = 3 N·m. The motor can give 1 N·m, so the output is clipped at 1 N·m: the controller is saturated. If the integral keeps adding error while the output is clipped, it accumulates a command far larger than the torque the motor ever delivered. In a tenth of a second at 200 Hz the integral gains about 60 × 0.1 = 6 degree-seconds; with Ki = 0.1 that is 0.1 × 6 = 0.6 N·m of command that the motor never delivered, and with Ki = 0.4 it is 0.4 × 6 = 2.4 N·m. When the arm finally arrives, that stored command is still there, so the controller drives past the target and the integral has to unwind before the arm can settle.

The last four lines of the output show the size of the problem. With Ki = 0.1 and anti-windup, the PID overshoots 0.1°; without anti-windup it overshoots 8.8°. With Ki = 0.4, anti-windup gives 8.9° and no anti-windup gives 39.0°. The higher the integral gain, the faster it accumulates and the worse the overshoot.

5. Set Ki = 0.10 and Kd = 0.0000 and read overshoot = 11.5° and Within 1° from = 0.92 s. Conclusion: with Ki = 0 the steady error is the gravity torque divided by Kp: 0.4 N·m × cos 55.5° = 0.227 N·m, 0.227 / 0.05 = 4.5°, so 60° − 4.5° = 55.5°; anti-windup stops the integral accumulating while the torque is saturated at 1 N·m.

The fix in the model is one condition: only integrate while the output is not saturated. In code, that is the line `if not anti_windup or torque == unclipped:`. When the requested torque is larger than the limit, `torque` and `unclipped` differ, so the integral is frozen; it starts accumulating only after the arm is close enough that P alone no longer saturates the motor. Other fixes work on the same principle. Clamp the integral to a range, as the balancer's outer loop does. Or reset the integral when the target jumps, so old error from a previous move does not leak into the new one. Any of them is better than letting the integral run while the motor is flat out.

## Tuning step by step

Tuning is not guesswork; it is a short procedure with one change at a time. The order matters because each gain depends on the ones before it.

1. **Check the sign with a tiny Kp.** Give the joint a small target step and watch which way it moves. If it moves away from the target, flip the sign of the controller output (or swap the motor wires) before doing anything else. A positive-feedback loop drives the output to its limit, whatever the gain.
2. **Raise Kp until it reaches quickly and starts to oscillate, then back off to about half.** The oscillation point tells you where the loop has more gain than damping; half is a practical starting point, not a law.
3. **Add Kd to calm the overshoot.** D amplifies sensor noise, because differentiating a noisy signal multiplies the fast wiggle by the frequency. Filter the rate, or use a rate sensor directly: the balancer uses the gyro for exactly this reason, so its D term does not differentiate a noisy angle.
4. **Add a little Ki to remove the remaining error, with anti-windup from the start.** Take small steps; the integral is the slowest term and the easiest to wind up.
5. **Change one gain at a time and write every try in a tuning log.** Record the gains, the overshoot, the settling time, and what you saw and heard. Month 2 asked for the same log; it is the only way to know which change helped.

The loop rate must be well above how fast the system moves. A 200 Hz loop gives a correction every 5 ms, which is plenty for a balancer or a small joint. A slow loop adds delay: the command is applied to a state that has already moved on, so the loop loses phase margin and destabilises more easily. If you cannot raise the rate, lower the gains; a slow loop with high gains is a machine that oscillates.

Backlash from Lesson 3 limits how high you can push Kp and Ki when the encoder is after the gears. Inside the gap the motor has no grip on the arm; a high-gain loop crosses the gap, overshoots, reverses and hunts in a small limit cycle. If the joint buzzes or hunts at standstill, backlash is one of the first things to suspect, along with derivative noise. Reducing the gain, adding preload, or moving the encoder are the real fixes; more gain makes it worse.

## Cascade loops

A <Term id="cascade-control">cascade</Term> is two feedback loops nested, one inside the other. The inner loop is fast and controls something quick, such as pitch. The outer loop is slower and controls something slower, such as wheel speed, by changing the inner loop's target. The outer loop does not drive the motors directly; it only moves the <Term id="setpoint">setpoint</Term> that the inner loop is trying to hold.

<Diagram id="pid-cascade" />

The rule is that the outer loop must run clearly slower than the inner one. Here the inner loop is 200 Hz and the outer loop is 25 Hz, eight times slower. The outer loop's output is also clamped to ±3° of lean. Both rules serve the same purpose: a mistake in the outer loop must not be able to knock the robot over. The inner loop still has authority over balance; the outer loop only suggests a small lean.

Both numbers can be derived. The outer loop's rate is set by the speed measurement. The encoder gives one count per π × 65 mm / 90 = 2.27 mm of travel, and the speed is the number of counts in a time window divided by the window, so one count is the smallest speed step it can show: 2.27 mm / 5 ms = 0.45 m/s if the outer loop ran every 5 ms like the inner loop, and 2.27 mm / 40 ms = 0.057 m/s every 8th tick. A 5 ms window reads 0 or ±0.45 m/s for any slower speed, a step almost half the 1 m/s top speed, and no gain can control speed from that. The 40 ms window still reads in steps of 0.057 m/s, which is why high outer gains turn it into a jumpy setpoint below. A longer window gives finer steps but reports the speed later; an encoder with more counts per turn, or counting every edge of both channels, gives finer steps at the same rate.

The ±3° clamp follows from the motors' acceleration limit. To keep the body at a constant lean θ, the wheels have to accelerate at a = g × tan θ; at 3° that is 9.81 × tan 3° = 0.51 m/s², under a tenth of the 6 m/s² the motors can deliver (MAX_ACCEL), so most of the motors' authority stays with the inner loop for balance. The same formula gives the hard limit: at 6 m/s², tan θ = 6 / 9.81 and θ = 31.5°, so beyond about 31° even full acceleration cannot bring the body back. The model counts the robot as fallen at 40°, the same cut-off as balance_sim.py and CUTOFF_DEG in the firmware below. The clamp also caps the outer P term: with kp_s = 12 degrees per m/s, the setpoint reaches 3° at a speed error of 3 / 12 = 0.25 m/s.

Why does a balancer roll away with the inner loop alone? Because the IMU is never mounted perfectly. If the sensor reads 0° when the robot is actually leaning 1° forward, then holding pitch = 0 means holding a 1° forward lean. Gravity pulls the robot forward, the inner loop drives the wheels to stay under it, and the robot accelerates. It keeps accelerating until the wheels reach top speed, and then it falls. The model adds exactly that: a 1° mounting error, a 1 m/s top speed, and a coarse encoder with 90 counts per wheel turn, counting rising edges of A only, as in the firmware.

<CodeSource path="tools/sim/models/cascade_sim.py" />

```python
"""cascade_sim.py: finish the Month 2 balancer with an outer speed loop (a cascade).

Same toy inverted pendulum as balance_sim.py (centre of mass 0.10 m above the axle, the
controller sets wheel acceleration, complementary filter with a 0.5 s time constant),
plus the things that make a real balancer roll away:
  * the IMU is mounted 1 degree off, so "pitch = 0" is not quite the balance point
  * the wheels have a top speed, and a coarse encoder (90 counts per turn of a 65 mm wheel)
Inner loop: PD on pitch at 200 Hz. Outer loop: PI on wheel speed at 25 Hz, which moves
the inner loop's SETPOINT. 60 s run; at t = 30 s someone pushes the robot.
"""
import math
import random

G, L = 9.81, 0.10            # gravity, height of the centre of mass (m)
MAX_ACCEL = 6.0              # m/s^2 the motors can deliver
MOTOR_TAU = 0.02             # wheels reach a new acceleration in ~20 ms
MAX_SPEED = 1.0              # m/s, wheel top speed
MOUNT_ERROR = 1.0            # deg, IMU reads this much more than the true tilt
WHEEL_TURN = math.pi * 0.065 # m per wheel turn (65 mm wheel)
COUNTS_PER_TURN = 90         # encoder counts per wheel turn (A edges only, as in the sketch)
KP, KD = 0.8, 0.04           # inner PD, same gains as balance_sim.py


def run(outer=None, kp_s=0.0, ki_s=0.0, push=40.0, go=None, seconds=60.0, seed=3, trace=None):
    """outer=None: inner loop only; outer="P" or "PI" adds the speed loop.
    kp_s is in deg of setpoint per m/s of speed error, ki_s in deg per metre.
    go=(t, speed): from time t on, ask for this wheel speed instead of 0."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    dt = 0.005
    theta, omega, accel = math.radians(3.0), 0.0, 0.0
    x, v = 0.0, 0.0
    est, setpoint, integral = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    last_counts, tick, t, next_ctrl = 0, 0, 0.0, 0.0
    far, pushed, sq, n = 0.0, False, 0.0, 0
    x_before, push_far, settled = 0.0, 0.0, None
    log = []
    while t < seconds:
        if t >= next_ctrl:                                   # --- 200 Hz controller tick ---
            next_ctrl += dt
            gyro = math.degrees(omega) + rng.gauss(0, 0.3)
            acc = math.degrees(theta) + MOUNT_ERROR + rng.gauss(0, 2.5)
            a = 0.5 / (0.5 + dt)
            est = a * (est + gyro * dt) + (1 - a) * acc
            if outer and tick % 8 == 0:                      # --- 25 Hz outer loop ---
                counts = math.floor(x / WHEEL_TURN * COUNTS_PER_TURN)
                speed = (counts - last_counts) / COUNTS_PER_TURN * WHEEL_TURN / (8 * dt)
                last_counts = counts
                target = go[1] if go and t >= go[0] else 0.0
                error = target - speed
                if outer == "PI":
                    integral += error * 8 * dt
                    limit = 3.0 / ki_s                       # anti-windup: the integral alone
                    integral = max(-limit, min(limit, integral))  # may ask for at most 3 deg
                setpoint = max(-3.0, min(3.0, kp_s * error + ki_s * integral))
            tick += 1
            command = KP * (est - setpoint) + KD * gyro
            command = max(-MAX_ACCEL, min(MAX_ACCEL, command))
            if trace and trace[0] <= t < trace[1] and tick % 8 == 1:
                log.append(f"t={t:5.2f} s  setpoint {setpoint:+5.2f} deg  tilt {math.degrees(theta):+5.2f} deg  "
                           f"wheels {v:+.2f} m/s")
        # --- physics at 1 ms ---
        accel += (command - accel) * 0.001 / MOTOR_TAU
        a_real = accel
        if (v >= MAX_SPEED and a_real > 0) or (v <= -MAX_SPEED and a_real < 0):
            a_real = 0.0                                     # motors are flat out
        alpha = (G * math.sin(theta) - a_real * math.cos(theta)) / L
        omega += alpha * 0.001
        theta += omega * 0.001
        v += a_real * 0.001
        x += v * 0.001
        if not pushed and t >= 30.0:
            omega += math.radians(push)
            pushed, x_before = True, x
        if pushed:
            push_far = max(push_far, abs(x - x_before))
            if settled is None and t >= 35.0:
                settled = abs(x - x_before)                  # 5 s after the push
        if 40.0 < t < 60.0:
            sq += math.degrees(theta) ** 2
            n += 1
        t += 0.001
        if not go:
            far = max(far, abs(x))
        if abs(math.degrees(theta)) > 40:
            return f"fell over after {t:.1f} s, {x:+.2f} m from the start", log
    return (f"upright, farthest {far / WHEEL_TURN:.2f} wheel turns from the start; push moved it "
            f"{push_far / WHEEL_TURN:.2f} turns, {settled / WHEEL_TURN:.2f} turns off 5 s later; "
            f"wobble {math.sqrt(sq / n):.1f} deg rms"), log


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("inner loop only:         ", run()[0])
    print("outer P   (12):          ", run("P", kp_s=12)[0])
    print("outer PI  (12, 12):      ", run("PI", kp_s=12, ki_s=12)[0])
    print("outer PI  (60, 60):      ", run("PI", kp_s=60, ki_s=60)[0])
    print("outer PI (12, 12), asked to drive at +0.2 m/s from t = 40 s:")
    print("\n".join(run("PI", kp_s=12, ki_s=12, go=(40.0, 0.2), seconds=41.0, trace=(39.96, 40.6))[1]))
```

### Why it moves the wrong way first

The trace at the end asks for +0.2 m/s at t = 40 s. The setpoint jumps to +3°, which means lean forward. But look at the wheels: they first go backwards, −0.04, −0.06, −0.03 m/s, and only then does the robot drive forward, reaching +0.21 m/s at 40.56 s. This is not a bug. To make the body lean forward, the inner loop must accelerate the wheels backwards, so the body rotates forward over the contact patch. Once the body is leaning, the inner loop drives the wheels forward to stay under it.

Stopping works the same way in reverse: to brake, the robot first speeds up a little to get under a backward lean, and then slows down. Engineers call this non-minimum-phase behaviour. For you it means one practical rule: do not try to fix the first wrong-way movement. It is the physics of an <Term id="inverted-pendulum">inverted pendulum</Term>, and a controller that suppresses it will fall over.

## Finish the balancer

This lesson completes the optional [Month 2 Lesson 6 challenge](/en/month/month-2/first-robots): the self-balancer in "Your first two robots: line follower & self-balancer". You already have the inner PD loop; the new part is the encoders (the same gear motors with quadrature encoders from Month 2 Lesson 4) and the outer PI loop. Do not start the outer loop until the inner loop balances on its own. A cascade cannot fix a broken inner loop.

The firmware is the Month 2 balancer plus encoders and an outer PI loop. The wiring additions are:

| Signal | Uno pin | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Left encoder A | D2 | interrupt pin |
| Left encoder B | D10 | direction |
| Right encoder A | D3 | interrupt pin |
| Right encoder B | D11 | direction |

D2 and D3 are the Uno's two external interrupt pins, which is why both A channels go there.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-3/pid-control/balancer_cascade/balancer_cascade.ino" />

```cpp
// Self-balancer with a cascade: the Month 2 balancer (inner PD on pitch, 200 Hz) plus an
// outer PI loop on wheel speed (25 Hz) that moves the inner loop's SETPOINT.
// Uno + TB6612FNG + two gearmotors with encoders + MPU-6050, wired as in Month 2 Lesson 6,
// plus: left encoder A -> D2, B -> D10; right encoder A -> D3, B -> D11.
// Get the inner loop balancing first (Month 2), then copy your KP/KD here.
#include <Wire.h>

const int PWMA = 5, AIN1 = 7, AIN2 = 8;     // left motor
const int PWMB = 6, BIN1 = 4, BIN2 = 12;    // right motor
const int STBY = 9;
const int ENC_LA = 2, ENC_LB = 10;          // A channels on the two interrupt pins
const int ENC_RA = 3, ENC_RB = 11;

const uint8_t MPU = 0x68;
const float ACCEL_LSB_PER_G = 16384.0;
const float GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS = 131.0;

const unsigned long PERIOD_US = 5000;       // inner loop: 200 Hz
const float DT = PERIOD_US / 1e6;
const int OUTER_EVERY = 8;                  // outer loop: every 8th tick = 25 Hz
const float OUTER_DT = DT * OUTER_EVERY;
const float ALPHA = 0.5 / (0.5 + DT);       // complementary filter, 0.5 s time constant

// Encoder: counting rising edges of A only = 1/4 of the full quadrature resolution.
// Example: 12 CPR motor encoder (3 rising edges of A per motor turn) x 30:1 gearbox, 65 mm wheel.
const float METERS_PER_COUNT = 3.14159 * 0.065 / (3 * 30);

float KP = 25.0, KD = 0.8;                  // inner PD: YOUR tuned Month 2 values
float KP_S = 3.0;                           // outer P: deg of setpoint per m/s of speed error
float KI_S = 3.0;                           // outer I: deg of setpoint per metre
const float SETPOINT_MAX = 3.0;             // the outer loop may lean the robot at most 3 deg
const float CUTOFF_DEG = 40.0;

volatile long leftCount = 0, rightCount = 0;
float pitch = 0, gyroBias = 0, setpoint = 0, integral = 0, speed = 0, targetSpeed = 0;
long lastPos = 0;
bool running = false;
unsigned int tick = 0;
unsigned long nextTick = 0, worstLoopUs = 0, lastReport = 0;

// If a wheel counts backwards when it rolls forward, swap that motor's A and B wires.
void leftEdge()  { leftCount  += digitalRead(ENC_LB) ? -1 : 1; }
void rightEdge() { rightCount += digitalRead(ENC_RB) ? -1 : 1; }

void setMotor(int pwmPin, int in1, int in2, int speed) {
  speed = constrain(speed, -255, 255);
  digitalWrite(in1, speed > 0);
  digitalWrite(in2, speed < 0);
  analogWrite(pwmPin, abs(speed));
}

void readImu(float &accelPitch, float &rate) {
  Wire.beginTransmission(MPU);
  Wire.write(0x3B);
  Wire.endTransmission(false);
  Wire.requestFrom(MPU, (uint8_t)14);
  int16_t v[7];
  for (int i = 0; i < 7; i++) v[i] = (Wire.read() << 8) | Wire.read();
  accelPitch = atan2(v[0] / ACCEL_LSB_PER_G, v[2] / ACCEL_LSB_PER_G) * 180.0 / PI;
  rate = v[5] / GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS - gyroBias;
}

void outerLoop() {                          // 25 Hz: wheel speed -> setpoint
  noInterrupts();
  long pos = (leftCount + rightCount) / 2;
  interrupts();
  speed = (pos - lastPos) * METERS_PER_COUNT / OUTER_DT;   // m/s
  lastPos = pos;
  float error = targetSpeed - speed;
  integral += error * OUTER_DT;
  float iMax = SETPOINT_MAX / KI_S;         // anti-windup: the I term alone stays within 3 deg
  integral = constrain(integral, -iMax, iMax);
  setpoint = constrain(KP_S * error + KI_S * integral, -SETPOINT_MAX, SETPOINT_MAX);
}

void setup() {
  int outs[] = {PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, PWMB, BIN1, BIN2, STBY};
  for (int p : outs) pinMode(p, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(STBY, HIGH);
  int ins[] = {ENC_LA, ENC_LB, ENC_RA, ENC_RB};
  for (int p : ins) pinMode(p, INPUT_PULLUP);
  attachInterrupt(digitalPinToInterrupt(ENC_LA), leftEdge, RISING);
  attachInterrupt(digitalPinToInterrupt(ENC_RA), rightEdge, RISING);
  Serial.begin(115200);
  Wire.begin();
  Wire.setClock(400000);
  Wire.beginTransmission(MPU);
  Wire.write(0x6B);
  Wire.write(0);
  Wire.endTransmission();

  float sum = 0, a, r;                      // gyro bias: hold still for one second
  for (int i = 0; i < 200; i++) { readImu(a, r); sum += r; delay(5); }
  gyroBias = sum / 200;
  readImu(a, r);
  pitch = a;
  Serial.print(F("gyro bias (deg/s): "));
  Serial.println(gyroBias, 2);
  nextTick = micros();
}

void loop() {
  if ((long)(micros() - nextTick) < 0) return;
  unsigned long start = micros();
  nextTick += PERIOD_US;

  float accelPitch, rate;
  readImu(accelPitch, rate);
  pitch = ALPHA * (pitch + rate * DT) + (1 - ALPHA) * accelPitch;

  if (fabs(pitch) > CUTOFF_DEG) running = false;
  if (!running && fabs(pitch) < 5.0) {      // stood back up: start both loops from zero
    running = true;
    integral = 0;
    setpoint = 0;
    noInterrupts();
    lastPos = (leftCount + rightCount) / 2;
    interrupts();
  }

  if (running) {
    if (++tick % OUTER_EVERY == 0) outerLoop();
    int out = (int)(KP * (pitch - setpoint) + KD * rate);
    setMotor(PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, out);
    setMotor(PWMB, BIN1, BIN2, out);
  } else {
    setMotor(PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, 0);
    setMotor(PWMB, BIN1, BIN2, 0);
  }

  unsigned long used = micros() - start;
  if (used > worstLoopUs) worstLoopUs = used;
  if (millis() - lastReport >= 1000) {
    lastReport = millis();
    Serial.print(F("pitch "));
    Serial.print(pitch, 1);
    Serial.print(F("  setpoint "));
    Serial.print(setpoint, 2);
    Serial.print(F("  speed "));
    Serial.print(speed, 3);
    Serial.print(F("  worst loop us "));
    Serial.println(worstLoopUs);
    worstLoopUs = 0;
  }
}
```

Compared with Month 2, the new pieces are:

- The encoder interrupts count rising edges of A and read B for direction. If a wheel counts backwards when it rolls forward, swap that motor's A and B wires.
- `METERS_PER_COUNT` is π × 0.065 / (3 × 30) = 0.00227 m for a 12 CPR encoder (3 rising A edges per motor turn) on a 30:1 gearbox with a 65 mm wheel. Change it for your motors.
- `outerLoop()` runs every 8th tick, at 25 Hz. It reads the counts with interrupts briefly off so the two longs are not read half-updated, computes speed in m/s, then applies a PI controller. The integral is clamped to 3 / KI_S, so the I term alone can ask for at most 3°, and the setpoint is clamped to ±3°.
- When the robot is stood back up, the integral, setpoint and position reference reset to zero, so an old error does not throw it over.
- The outer gains 3 and 3 are a cautious start. The model liked 12 and 12; your robot is not the model.

Then the run: the simavr simulator with the simulated MPU-6050 (scripted: flat, tilted to 30° between 3 and 4 s, held, back to 0° between 7 and 8 s; gyro bias +1.5 °/s) and encoder pulses injected on D2 and D3 as if the robot rolled backwards at 0.1 m/s from 1.5 s to 5.5 s. No motors or robot physics are simulated, and the I2C bus time is not modelled. It is an 8 s run, and `tools/sim/reproduce.sh` generates it.

The pitch column is the scripted tilt, not the robot falling over; it stays under the 40° cutoff, so both loops keep running. The B channels sit high on their pull-ups, so every A edge counts −1: the injected pulses look like backwards motion. ### Bring-up on the real robot

1. Get the inner loop balancing on its own, then copy your Month 2 KP and KD into the sketch.
2. Set KP_S and KI_S to 0. Roll the robot by hand and watch the speed print. Forward must be positive. One full wheel turn is π × 0.065 = 0.204 m, so if the number does not match the distance you rolled, check `METERS_PER_COUNT` and the encoder wiring.
3. Add KP_S in small steps, then KI_S. With the robot held in your hand, it should lean against a push: the wheels try to drive back under the robot. If it leans the wrong way, the sign of KP_S is wrong.
4. Watch the setpoint in the serial print. If it sits at +3.00 or −3.00 all the time, the outer loop is saturated: the gains are too high or the speed sign is wrong. A saturated outer loop is not controlling anything; it is just asking for maximum lean.
5. Log every try: inner gains, outer gains, what the setpoint did, how far it moved, and what it sounded like.

The editor has not run this cascade on a real robot. Treat the gains as a starting point, not a result. Your motors, wheels, encoder counts and IMU mounting will all shift the numbers.

<Summary>

**Key points**

- P alone leaves error = load / Kp: 0.227 N·m at Kp = 0.05 N·m/degree gives 4.5°, so a 60° target stops at 55.5°.
- PID with Kp 0.05, Ki 0.1 and Kd 0.002 reaches 60.0° with 0.1° overshoot and is within 1° from 0.24 s.
- Windup: with Ki = 0.4 the overshoot is 8.9° with anti-windup and 39.0° without it.
- Cascade: inner loop 200 Hz, outer loop 25 Hz, setpoint clamped to ±3°; the inner loop alone falls over after 6.9 s, while the outer PI with kp_s = 12 and ki_s = 12 stays within 0.51 wheel turns over 60 s.
- 90 counts per wheel turn on a 65 mm wheel is π × 65 / 90 = 2.27 mm per count.
- Model constants come from the joint: gravity torque m × g × r (measure F × L with a scale), J ≥ m × r² plus n² × J_rotor, a torque limit between the servo's rated load and its stall torque (an optimistic short-term peak; at 5 V, budget ≤0.4 N·m for a continuous load: Feetech gives no 5 V rating, and linear scaling by voltage gives ≈0.33 N·m, an unverified estimate), and a loop rate of 20 to 60 times ωn = √(Kp / J) (200 Hz is 47 times here).
- Cascade: a 40 ms speed window resolves 0.057 m/s per count (5 ms would give 0.45 m/s), and a 3° lean needs only 0.51 m/s² of the 6 m/s² available.

**Common mistakes**

- Expecting P or D to remove the steady error: only I can produce torque at zero error.
- Integrating while the motor is saturated; without anti-windup, Ki = 0.4 overshoots 39.0°.
- Differentiating the error instead of the measurement, so a target step produces a derivative kick.
- Starting the outer loop before the inner loop balances on its own, or leaving the setpoint pinned at +3.00 or −3.00, which means the outer loop is saturated.
