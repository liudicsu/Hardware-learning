## How hard to squeeze

A two-finger gripper holds an object with **friction**, not by clamping harder. Each of the two fingers presses on the object with a force F, and because there are two contact faces, the friction that resists the object sliding out is **2 × μ × F**, where μ (mu) is the <Term id="friction-coefficient">coefficient of friction</Term> between the finger material and the object. If whatever pulls on the object is larger than that, the object slips — however tight the grip felt to your hand.

Three things pull on a held object. Its own weight is m × g. When the arm accelerates the object at a, the object's inertia produces an opposing force m × a that the grip has to carry. And a <Term id="safety-factor">safety factor</Term> S sits in front of both, because you never want to work right at the edge of slipping. Requiring the friction to cover all of it gives the minimum squeeze per finger:

```text
2 × μ × F ≥ S × m × (g + a)

solve for F:

F = S × m × (g + a) / (2 × μ)
```

The consequence lives in the denominator: μ matters exactly as much as force. A grippy material cuts the squeeze you need: with the guesses below, TPU needs well under half of what PLA needs, and that is exactly what you want when the object is an egg. "Just squeeze harder" is not a strategy; it is how you crack a shell, crush a can, or leave a servo buzzing and hot.

The short program below runs this arithmetic for three objects and two finger materials. It is ordinary Python doing ordinary arithmetic — not a simulation, and it has never been near a robot. The μ values inside it are rough guesses, and this lesson will ask you to measure your own and put them in.

Three of the program's inputs are **example assumptions**, not measured or published values, and the course has not measured any of them on an SO-101: μ = 0.3 for PLA and μ = 0.8 for TPU on the objects below; an arm acceleration of a = 3.0 m/s², chosen to stand for a brisk move; and a safety factor of S = 2, which means the fingers are asked to hold twice the load they are expected to carry. You replace μ with a measurement later in this lesson. You can estimate a for your own arm the same way: time a move that starts from rest and covers a distance d in a time t; at constant acceleration d = ½ × a × t², so a = 2 × d / t². Raise S for objects that must not be dropped.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-3/gripper-fingers/grip_force.py" />

```python
"""grip_force.py: how hard must a two-finger gripper squeeze so the object does not slip?

With two flat fingers squeezing with force F each, friction can hold up to 2 * mu * F.
To hold weight m * g while the arm also accelerates the object (a), with a safety factor S:
    F = S * m * (g + a) / (2 * mu)
mu is the friction coefficient between the finger and the object. Hard PLA on smooth
objects is low; soft TPU is much higher. The mu values below are rough guesses: measure
your own (tilt a board until the object slides: mu = tan(angle)).
"""
G = 9.81
SAFETY = 2.0
ARM_ACCEL = 3.0          # m/s^2: a brisk move of the arm

OBJECTS = [              # name, mass (kg)
    ("egg", 0.060),
    ("full 330 ml can", 0.350),
    ("AA battery", 0.023),
]
FINGERS = [("PLA", 0.3), ("TPU", 0.8)]


def squeeze(mass, mu):
    return SAFETY * mass * (G + ARM_ACCEL) / (2 * mu)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"safety factor {SAFETY}, arm acceleration {ARM_ACCEL} m/s^2")
    for name, mass in OBJECTS:
        row = ", ".join(f"{f} (mu {mu}): {squeeze(mass, mu):4.1f} N" for f, mu in FINGERS)
        print(f"{name:16s} {mass * 1000:4.0f} g  ->  {row}")
```

Real output:

```text
$ python3 grip_force.py
safety factor 2.0, arm acceleration 3.0 m/s^2
egg                60 g  ->  PLA (mu 0.3):  2.6 N, TPU (mu 0.8):  1.0 N
full 330 ml can   350 g  ->  PLA (mu 0.3): 14.9 N, TPU (mu 0.8):  5.6 N
AA battery         23 g  ->  PLA (mu 0.3):  1.0 N, TPU (mu 0.8):  0.4 N
```

Take the egg with PLA fingers. The mass is 0.060 kg, g is 9.81 m/s², the arm accelerates at 3.0 m/s², the safety factor is 2, and PLA on a smooth shell is taken as μ = 0.3 (all three are the example assumptions above):

```text
F = 2 × 0.060 × (9.81 + 3.0) / (2 × 0.3)
  = 2 × 0.060 × 12.81 / 0.6
  = 1.537 / 0.6
  = 2.56 ≈ 2.6 N
```

Swap to TPU fingers and only μ changes, to about 0.8:

```text
F = 2 × 0.060 × 12.81 / (2 × 0.8)
  = 1.537 / 1.6
  = 0.96 ≈ 1.0 N
```

Force alone is not the whole story with fragile things. The same total force spread over a soft finger that wraps around the object is gentler than the same force concentrated on two hard points, because pressure is force divided by area. That is the motivation for the rest of the lesson: a finger that conforms spreads the load, and a load spread over a larger area needs less force to hold on.

## Measure the friction

The formula is only as good as the μ you feed it, and most tables you find online describe clean lab surfaces that are nothing like your printed finger on a real object. Measure it yourself with a tilted-board test.

Put the object on a flat piece of the finger material — a small printed test plate is ideal, but a scrap of your filament works too. Tilt the board slowly and steadily until the object just begins to slide, and read the angle at that instant. The friction coefficient is the tangent of that angle:

```text
μ = tan(angle)
```

Why? At the moment sliding starts, gravity pulls the object down the slope with a force m × g × sin θ, and friction resists it with μ × m × g × cos θ. The two are equal right at that instant:

```text
m × g × sin θ = μ × m × g × cos θ
divide both sides by m × g × cos θ:
μ = sin θ / cos θ = tan θ
```

The mass cancels out completely, which is a gift: the same object on the same material gives the same angle however heavy it is.

Two worked readings:

```text
object starts to slide at 35°:  μ = tan 35° = 0.70
object starts to slide at 17°:  μ = tan 17° = 0.31
```

A few habits make the number trustworthy. Repeat the test three times and write down the lowest angle: the lowest angle gives the lowest friction coefficient, and the lowest coefficient is the one that lets the object slip. Test on the real objects you plan to grasp — an eggshell, a painted can and a battery wrapper each friction differently, and a smooth can is much more slippery than a cardboard box. Clean both surfaces first, because grease and dust change the answer. You do not need a protractor: a phone's level or angle app reads the board's tilt accurately enough for this.

Then go back to `grip_force.py`, replace the guessed mu values with your measured ones, and rerun it. Now the numbers describe your gripper and your objects rather than a textbook's.

## The fin-ray idea

A stiff finger, the kind you would draw as a straight bar, touches a round object at one or two points. If two flat, parallel jaws close squarely on a round object, the two normal forces lie on one line through its centre and cancel each other; they do not push the object out. The trouble starts when the contacts sit off the object's diameter, or the jaws are angled to each other: then each normal force has a component along the line between the jaws' opening and the object's centre, and if friction is too small, more squeeze pushes the object out between the jaws. Even when the object stays put, two small contacts concentrate the whole force on two tiny areas, which is how a shell cracks. That is why a rigid gripper is bad at eggs and good at boxes.

A <Term id="fin-ray">fin-ray finger</Term> solves this by deforming on purpose. Picture a triangle standing on its base: two thin walls, called flanks, run up and meet at the tip, and a set of thin ribs bridges the gap between them. Press the front flank against an object, and the finger does not simply bend away. The ribs pass the push across to the back flank, the triangle bends toward the push, and the triangular section changes shape so that the two flanks wrap around the object instead of folding away from it. Contact spreads from two points to a long arc, so the pressure on the object is lower and the hold is more secure.

The principle is named after the way fish fins bend: a fin pushed from the side curls toward the push instead of folding away. The biologist Leif Kniese described the effect in 1997 from studies of fish tail-fin mechanics, and simplified the fin into a triangular frame with stacked crossbeams; EvoLogics GmbH (Berlin) later patented it ([Manoonpong P. et al., "Fin Ray Crossbeam Angles for Efficient Foot Design for Energy-Efficient Robot Locomotion", Adv. Intell. Syst. 4(1), 2100133, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1002/aisy.202100133); [Crooks et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2016.00070)). "Fin Ray Effect®" is a trademark of EvoLogics GmbH, not of Festo: Festo builds products on the effect, such as its FinGripper fingers, and its own documents carry the notice "Fin Ray Effect® is a trademark of EvoLogics GmbH" ([Festo BionicTripod 2.0 documentation](https://media.festo.com/media/113674_documentation.pdf), which also describes the FinGripper). The course has not checked the patent status of the geometry in any country, so it makes no claim that the geometry is free to use; this lesson prints a finger for your own bench.

Three parameters decide how a fin-ray finger behaves. The mechanics behind each one is ordinary beam bending. The numbers below use this lesson's default finger (`length` 60 mm, `base_w` 22 mm, `height` 18 mm, `flank` 1.2 mm, `ribs` 6) and textbook beam formulas. Treat them as a **first-order model** for comparing two designs, not as a prediction of the real finger's stiffness: the model ignores the joints where ribs meet flanks and TPU's non-linear stretch.

```text
one flank bending on its own (a strip 18 mm wide, 1.2 mm thick):
  I = b × t³ / 12 = 18 × 1.2³ / 12 = 2.592 mm⁴
the two flanks working together through the ribs (each flank A = 18 × 1.2 = 21.6 mm²):
  I ≈ 2 × A × (d / 2)²
  at the base, d = 22 mm:   2 × 21.6 × 11² = 5227 mm⁴
  halfway up,  d = 11 mm:   2 × 21.6 × 5.5² = 1307 mm⁴
flank span between ribs:    60 / (6 + 1) = 8.57 mm
```

I is the second moment of area, the cross-section's resistance to bending; a beam's bending stiffness is E × I, where E is the material's modulus. The second line is why the ribs matter. Without them each flank bends on its own, and the two together have I = 2 × 2.592 = 5.184 mm⁴. With them the two flanks act as one beam, one side stretching while the other shortens, and at the base I rises to 5227 mm⁴, about 1000 times as much. The finger is stiffest at the base, where the flanks are far apart, and softest toward the tip, where they meet.

- **Flank thickness.** Thinning the flanks from 1.2 mm to 0.8 mm multiplies the stiffness of a flank bending on its own by (0.8 / 1.2)³ = 0.30, because I ∝ t³. The two-flank beam only drops to 0.8 / 1.2 = 0.67, because there I ∝ A ∝ t. So a thinner flank mostly lets the front flank dent locally between the ribs and wrap the object, while the finger as a whole stays reasonably stiff.
- **Rib count and thickness.** The ribs are what couple the two flanks. Fewer ribs lengthen the flank span between them, and a span loaded in its middle gets softer as 1/L³: with 4 ribs the span is 60 / 5 = 12 mm, so the local stiffness is (8.57 / 12)³ = 0.36 of the 6-rib finger's. Thin ribs also buckle sooner under the push they carry (Euler: the buckling load grows with I ∝ t³ of the rib).
- **Rib tilt.** Tilting the crossbeams gives the finger a preferred bending direction and changes the force it needs to bend ([Crooks et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2016.00070)). A later finite-element (FE) simulation looked at the angle ([W. Crooks, Soft Robotic Manipulators Inspired by the Fin Ray Effect, PhD thesis, Tufts University, May 2017, §2.3.1](https://dl.tufts.edu/downloads/p5548309k?filename=3b591m85r.pdf)): a Nastran nonlinear static analysis of a silicone finger pushed with 1 N against half-cylinders 30, 50 and 70 mm in diameter, with the crossbeam angle swept from −15° to +15° in 2.5° steps and the maximum fingertip displacement as the measure. The angle of largest displacement, from a polynomial fit, changed with the cylinder size (+2.3° at 30 mm, −6.4° at 50 mm, −5.8° at 70 mm), so it lay between −6.4° and +2.3°. It is a simulation, not a physical test. Those studies define the sign of the angle with their own drawings, which do not map one-to-one onto this file, so the course does not claim which sign of `rib_tilt` wraps further on your object: print one finger at `rib_tilt = 10` and one at `rib_tilt = -10` and compare them.

Check the model on your own prints rather than trusting it. Clamp the finger's mounting block in a vice, hang a known mass on a thread from a point on the front flank, and measure how far that point moves with a ruler or calipers. A 100 g mass pulls with 0.100 × 9.81 = 0.98 N, and the finger's stiffness at that point is k = F / δ. Do it for two prints that differ in one parameter; the model predicts which one is stiffer and roughly by how much, and the measurement gives the real number.

The finger below is written in OpenSCAD so every one of those is a one-line change. Like the bracket in Lesson 1 ("CAD: pick a tool, then design with parameters"), it is a parametric file: measure the jaw it bolts to, and change the mount parameters.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-3/gripper-fingers/finray_finger.scad" />

```scad
// finray_finger.scad: a parametric fin-ray style finger to print in TPU.
// Two thin flanks meet at the tip; thin ribs join them. Press the front flank and the
// ribs make the finger curl AROUND the object instead of pushing it away.
//   Render:  openscad -o finray_finger.stl finray_finger.scad
// The mounting base is generic: measure the jaw you bolt it to and change mount_*.

length     = 60;     // base to tip, mm
base_w     = 22;     // finger depth at the base (front flank to back flank), mm
height     = 18;     // extrusion = finger width across the object, mm
flank      = 1.2;    // flank thickness: thinner = softer
ribs       = 6;      // number of internal ribs
rib_t      = 0.9;    // rib thickness
rib_tilt   = 10;     // deg: > 0 leans the ribs toward the base; try +10 and -10

base_t     = 5;      // solid mounting block, mm
mount_d    = 3.2;    // M3 holes
mount_pitch= 10;     // distance between the two holes: MEASURE YOUR JAW

echo("finger (mm)", length + base_t, "x", base_w, "x", height);
assert(flank >= 0.8, "flanks thinner than two 0.4 mm lines will not print cleanly");
assert(mount_pitch + mount_d < height, "mount holes do not fit across the finger width");

// front flank runs from (0,0) to the tip (length, 0); back flank from (0, base_w) to the tip
function back_y(x) = base_w * (1 - x / length);

module outline() {
  polygon([[0, 0], [length, 0], [0, base_w]]);
}

module hollow() {                        // the triangle minus its insides = two flanks
  difference() {
    outline();
    offset(delta = -flank) outline();
  }
}

module rib(i) {                          // a rib from the front flank to the back flank
  x = length * i / (ribs + 1);
  dx = back_y(x) * tan(rib_tilt);        // lean toward the base
  hull() {
    translate([x, 0]) square([rib_t, 0.01]);
    translate([max(0, x - dx), back_y(max(0, x - dx)) - 0.01]) square([rib_t, 0.01]);
  }
}

linear_extrude(height) {
  hollow();
  intersection() {
    outline();
    union() for (i = [1 : ribs]) rib(i);
  }
}
difference() {                           // mounting block behind the base of the finger
  translate([-base_t, 0, 0]) cube([base_t, base_w, height]);
  for (s = [-1, 1])
    translate([-base_t - 1, base_w / 2, height / 2 + s * mount_pitch / 2])
      rotate([0, 90, 0]) cylinder(d = mount_d, h = base_t + 2, $fn = 24);
}
```

## Printing TPU

TPU is a flexible filament. This lesson uses 95A (<Term id="shore-hardness">Shore hardness</Term> 95A): it is the grade the SO-ARM100 project names for its compliant gripper fingers ([SO-ARM100 README](https://github.com/TheRobotStudio/SO-ARM100#6-compliant-gripper)), and Polymaker's PolyFlex TPU95 is one 95A filament with a published data sheet. Shore A hardness is measured with a durometer ([ASTM D2240-15(2021)](https://www.astm.org/d2240-15r21.html)): a spring-loaded indenter is pressed onto the material; the standard's type A indenter extends 2.50 mm, and the reading falls by one point for every 0.025 mm it sinks in (§5.1.1.5 and §5.1.1.8), so 100 means no penetration and 0 means the full 2.50 mm. At 95A the indenter sinks about 5 × 0.025 = 0.125 mm; a lower number is a softer material. Prusa's guide to flexible filaments puts it this way: "the softer the filament is, the harder it is to print" ([Prusa Knowledge Base, Flexible materials](https://help.prusa3d.com/article/flexible-materials_2057)), so start with 95A rather than a softer grade. Flexible filament behaves differently from PLA. Each setting below names its source and the failure it prevents. Where the two sources give different numbers, follow the maker of the filament you bought; the Prusa figures are written for Prusa printers.

- **Print slowly.** Prusa gives 20 mm/s as a typical speed for flexible filament and 30–40 mm/s as the maximum it recommends, because at higher speeds you "risk clogging the nozzle or tangling filament into the gears". Polymaker gives 30–50 mm/s for PolyFlex TPU95 in its data sheet ([TDS V5.5](https://polymaker.com/wp-content/uploads/TDS_Polymaker_PolyFlex-TPU95_V5.5_2025_12_29_EN.pdf)) and 20–40 mm/s on its product page ([product page](https://shop.polymaker.com/products/polyflex-tpu95)).
- **Stay inside the filament maker's temperature range.** Prusa's general range for flexible filament is 230–245 °C at the nozzle and 60–75 °C on the bed; Polymaker gives 210–230 °C and 25–60 °C for PolyFlex TPU95. The ranges differ by brand and hardness, which is why the maker's own figure wins.
- **Use a direct-drive extruder.** Polymaker: "Direct extruder with a gear ratio mechanical advantage highly recommended", and "any large gap between your extruder and hotend will cause printing issues" ([product page](https://shop.polymaker.com/products/polyflex-tpu95)). Prusa adds: loosen the idler so the gears push the filament only gently, or "the filament might get entangled in the gears". The reason is buckling. The extruder pushes the filament like a column, and Euler's formula for a slender column gives the load at which it bows sideways as P = π² × E × I / L², where L is the unsupported length. A soft filament has a small E, so its P is already low; the formula then says that doubling the unsupported length divides P by 4, and a length 10 times longer divides it by 100. The lengths are illustrative, not measured on any printer: the point is that a short, guided path from gears to nozzle keeps the soft filament from folding.
- **Use little or no retraction.** Prusa: "With lower retractions, there is a lower risk of clogging the nozzle or filament tangling", and it is "alright to turn the retractions off completely". If you do retract, Polymaker gives 3 mm on a direct-drive extruder and 6 mm on an indirect (Bowden) one ([product page](https://shop.polymaker.com/products/polyflex-tpu95)).
- **Keep the filament dry.** Prusa: "Flexible filaments tend to absorb moisture and can be hard to print when wet." Polymaker's data sheet gives a drying setting of 70 °C for 8 h ([TDS V5.5](https://polymaker.com/wp-content/uploads/TDS_Polymaker_PolyFlex-TPU95_V5.5_2025_12_29_EN.pdf)), while its product page gives 65 °C for 8 h, only if the material has absorbed moisture ([product page](https://shop.polymaker.com/products/polyflex-tpu95)); the two differ by 5 °C. The data sheet also gives an equilibrium water absorption of 0.82 % at 70 % RH and 23 °C for PolyFlex TPU95.
- **Part-cooling fan: follow the maker.** The sources disagree: Polymaker's product page says "Fan: ON" for PolyFlex TPU95 ([product page](https://shop.polymaker.com/products/polyflex-tpu95)) and its data sheet also lists the cooling fan ON, while Prusa's rules for flexible filaments say to keep print cooling off (rule 5 of "Important rules for successful printing with flexible filaments"). Start with the filament maker's setting and change it only if a test print shows the problem.
- **Lay the finger so its triangular profile is on the bed.** Then the flanks and ribs are walls built up layer by layer, and the finger bends within each layer rather than across the layer lines. This is Lesson 2's orientation rule: layer lines are the weak direction, so never put the bending load across them.
- **<Term id="infill">Infill</Term> hardly matters.** The flanks and ribs are the structure; keep infill low and let the walls do the work.
- **Make flanks and ribs at least two extrusion widths.** At a 0.4 mm nozzle that is 2 × 0.4 = 0.8 mm and up, so they print as clean walls instead of a thin, ragged web.

The build sheet matters too, because TPU bonds strongly to many surfaces. Prusa: "If you print on a smooth or satin sheet, apply a separation layer on it (glue stick or Kapton tape should work)"; its powder-coated textured sheet "does not need any separation layer". Polymaker lists PC and textured PEI as the build surfaces for PolyFlex TPU95. Removing a part that has bonded to a bare smooth sheet can tear the sheet's surface, so put the separation layer down before the print, not after.

## Fit it to the SO-101

Do not guess the mounting geometry. Change the `mount_*` parameters in `finray_finger.scad` to match, or, if you want the finger to be part of the jaw itself, redesign the jaw around your finger shape.

The same repository also ships an optional compliant gripper designed for TPU 95A by Zach Tabor and Caitlin Freeman, from a June 2025 hackathon. Its outside shape matches the stock gripper, so it is a drop-in swap, and it is worth studying before you design your own: look at how its ribs are laid out and how it mounts to the arm.

<Resource id="so101-compliant-gripper" />

Before you mount anything, run two checks. First, does the gripper still close fully without the two fingers hitting each other or the jaw? A finger a few millimetres too long collides in the middle of the travel. Second, does the open width still clear your largest object? Measure the gap when the gripper is fully open and compare it with the widest thing you plan to grasp.

The gripper is servo ID 6, and it drives its output through a position loop: you send a goal, and the servo keeps pushing until its encoder says it has arrived. When the fingers close on an object, the servo keeps pushing toward that goal and settles with a <Term id="steady-state-error">steady error</Term> — the same steady-state behaviour from Lesson 4 (PID & control). There is a useful side effect: because a soft TPU finger deflects, it absorbs some of that force and treats the object gently. LeRobot already limits this servo: it writes Max_Torque_Limit 500, Protection_Current 250 and Overload_Torque 25 to the gripper ([so_follower.py, lines 169–171, LeRobot v0.6.1](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/v0.6.1/src/lerobot/robots/so_follower/so_follower.py#L169-L171)). In Feetech's [online STS memory table](https://ftservo.github.io/ftservo-wiki/en/reference/parameter/memory-sts/) that is 50 % of the maximum torque, a protection current of 250 × 6.5 mA = 1.625 A, and an overload timer that starts once the load exceeds 25 % of the maximum torque; the 2019 table (V3.6, firmware 2.43) reposted by a third party ([STS3215 Memory Table.xlsx in matthieuvigne/STS_servos](https://github.com/matthieuvigne/STS_servos/blob/4d2278432a2b12027d04d0449e1e6f97df812d68/extras/STS3215%20Memory%20Table.xlsx)) uses the same units, and the SO-101 lesson has the details. The torque cap takes effect from the servo's next power-up: both tables say address 16 is copied to address 48, the limit actually in effect, only at power-up. That only softens a stall, so do not leave the servo stalled against a hard stop for long: it draws current and heats up. In teleoperation, open the leader's gripper as soon as you have put the object down, otherwise the follower's gripper keeps driving against its position-loop target with nothing to hold.

## Practice: three objects

Your task for this lesson is the course's practice task, in your own hands: design your own gripper fingers, print them in TPU, fit them to the arm, and test them on three objects of different shapes — for example something round and fragile like an egg, something heavy and smooth like a full can, and something small and thin like a battery or a pen. These fingers are the part that proves you can design as well as assemble. Bolting a kit together needs only the instructions; a working custom gripper is your own design.

A test protocol, so the result actually means something:

1. Do 10 grasps per object, for each finger set — the stock fingers and yours.
2. Start the arm from the same position every time, so the only thing that changes is the grasp.
3. Score a success only if the grasp picks the object up, lifts it 10 cm, holds it for 5 s, and puts it down without dropping or damaging it.
4. Log every try, not just the successes.

Then compare the two sets. A worked example:

```text
egg:  stock fingers 6 / 10 = 60 %
      my fingers    9 / 10 = 90 %
```

A difference of three successes in ten tries is a hint, not proof. If the two sets come out close, repeat the test with more tries before you conclude anything. Record your runs in a table like this:

| Object | Mass (g) | μ measured | Stock fingers | My fingers | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| egg | – | – | – / 10 | – / 10 | |
| full can | – | – | – / 10 | – / 10 | |
| battery | – | – | – / 10 | – / 10 | |

The last step is the easiest to skip: change **one** parameter — the rib count or the flank thickness — print again, and rerun the test. If the change helps, you have learned something about your design; if it does not, you have learned that too. That single-parameter loop is the design loop from Lesson 2, and it is the whole method.

<Summary>

**Key points**

- Two fingers hold by friction: 2 × μ × F ≥ S × m × (g + a), so each finger needs F = S × m × (g + a) / (2 × μ).
- With the example assumptions S = 2, a = 3.0 m/s² and the μ values below, a 60 g egg needs 2.6 N per finger with PLA (μ ≈ 0.3) and 1.0 N with TPU (μ ≈ 0.8); a 350 g can needs 14.9 N and 5.6 N.
- Measure μ with a tilted board: μ = tan θ at the angle where sliding starts (17° gives 0.31, 35° gives 0.70). The mass cancels; repeat three times and use the lowest angle.
- A fin-ray finger curls toward the push because the ribs pass the load to the back flank, so contact spreads from two points to an arc. Flank thickness, rib count and thickness, and rib tilt set how stiff it is and how far it curls.
- Print TPU 95A slowly, preferably with a direct-drive extruder, with little or no retraction, lying on its triangular side, and with flanks and ribs at least 2 × 0.4 = 0.8 mm at a 0.4 mm nozzle.
- Take the mounting geometry from the SO-101 STEP files, not the STL, and check full closing and the open width before you mount the fingers.
- Test 10 grasps per object for each finger set; a success lifts the object 10 cm, holds it 5 s and puts it down undamaged.

**Common mistakes**

- Squeezing harder instead of raising μ: the 350 g can needs 14.9 N per PLA finger but 5.6 N per TPU finger.
- Using μ from an online table instead of measuring it on your own finger material and objects.
- Laying the finger so the bending load crosses the layer lines.
- Printing with `mount_pitch = 10` unchanged: it is a placeholder, not a measurement of your jaw.
- Leaving the gripper servo stalled against an object or a hard stop, where it keeps drawing current and heats up. LeRobot's torque and current limits on the gripper soften this, but they do not make a long stall safe.

- Can you explain why the mass cancels in μ = tan θ?

- Can you name the one parameter you changed between your two prints and what it did to the success rate?
