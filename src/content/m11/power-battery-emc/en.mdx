## Why a desktop arm still needs a power plan

When six bus servos stall together, peak current is not "one servo × 6" in isolation — wire resistance pulls the voltage at the load down while the logic board still wants a steady 5 V / 3.3 V. Month 2 already showed "battery sags → open-loop speed changes"; here we turn that into a checklist you can compute.

Three hard EMC rules (summarised from Novanta / Roboteq / ARK wiring guides; not measured here):

1. **Keep motor phase leads away from encoder / I2C.** Fast current edges are noise sources; separate runs, cross at ~90° when they must meet.
2. **Single-point common ground.** Star the logic and motor grounds near the supply entry so large currents do not return through signal loops.
3. **Shields default to source-end ground.** Ground the signal-cable shield at the drive / controller end; minimise loop area.

## Battery packs: read only, do not open

Series raises voltage; parallel raises capacity; C-rate says how much continuous / pulse current the cell can deliver. The BMS cuts on over-discharge, over-current and short — **this lesson does not teach BMS parameter changes or pack teardown**. Bench habits:

- Li-ion uses its own charger; do not "just top it up" on a bench supply.
- Stay present while charging; stop on swelling, smell or heat.
- E-stop cuts the motor bus, not only logic (see [8.5 safety & reliability](/en/month/month-8/safety-reliability)).

## Power-budget toy

`power_budget_toy.py` uses a fixed seed and illustrative currents: 6 servos, 0.35 A idle, 1.8 A peak, 12 V nominal, 0.8 m × 50 mΩ/m wire, 10 A fuse. It prints totals, peak drop, voltage at the load, fuse margin, and whether the 11 V floor still holds.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-11/power-battery-emc/power_budget_toy.py" />

```python
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""power_budget_toy.py: IR drop and fuse margin for a desktop-arm bus (stdlib).

Illustrative SO-101-like bus-servo currents — NOT measured on this site.
Fixed seed for reproducibility; change n_servos / wire length to explore.
"""
SEED = 5
# SO-101-like bus servos: illustrative currents, not measured
V_nom = 12.0
I_idle = 0.35  # A per servo idle
I_peak = 1.8   # A stall-ish peak
n_servos = 6
wire_mohm_per_m = 50.0  # 22 AWG ballpark illustrative
wire_len_m = 0.8
fuse_A = 10.0
V_floor = 11.0

R = wire_mohm_per_m * 1e-3 * wire_len_m
I_total_peak = n_servos * I_peak
I_total_idle = n_servos * I_idle
V_drop = I_total_peak * R
V_at_load = V_nom - V_drop
margin = fuse_A / I_total_peak

print(f"seed={SEED}")
print(f"V_nom={V_nom:.1f}")
print(f"n_servos={n_servos}")
print(f"I_idle_total={I_total_idle:.2f}")
print(f"I_peak_total={I_total_peak:.2f}")
print(f"wire_R_ohm={R:.4f}")
print(f"V_drop_peak={V_drop:.3f}")
print(f"V_at_load={V_at_load:.3f}")
print(f"fuse_A={fuse_A:.1f}")
print(f"fuse_margin={margin:.3f}")
print(f"pass_11v_floor={str(V_at_load >= V_floor).lower()}")
```

How to read it:

- Peak total **10.80** A already sits above a 10 A fuse rating — fuse_margin = **0.926** (fuse / peak &lt; 1) warns that a slow-blow fuse can ride a short spike, but sustained stall is not fine; size from your servo datasheet.
- 0.0400 Ω of wire drops **0.432** V at peak, leaving **11.568** V at the load, still above the 11 V floor (`pass_11v_floor=true`). R here is a **one-way conductor** resistance (R = ρ·L, length is supply-to-load one way; a symmetric return would double it — this toy uses the one-way example). Longer or thinner wire hits the floor first.
- These currents are **example assumptions**; this site has not measured whole SO-101 peak current.

Probing peak current with a clamp or scoping a ground loop is not run on this page. No power analyser purchase.

## Against the SO-101

Bus servos (e.g. STS3215) share motor power and a TTL bus. Logic 5 V often comes from an on-board LDO or a separate rail: when the motor bus sags, the logic rail needs its own decoupling — one fat wire does not keep both sides steady. Change `n_servos`, `I_peak` and `wire_len_m` to your assumptions, re-run, and put the numbers on your power checklist.
