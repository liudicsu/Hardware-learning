## Which Gazebo

Few names in robotics cause more confusion than this one, so here is the whole story in one paragraph. Gazebo Classic, the simulator whose versions were numbered 1 to 11, reached end of life in January 2025. Its successor was a rewrite rather than an upgrade, and for a while it was called Ignition Gazebo. In April 2022 the project renamed it back to plain Gazebo, and every `ign` command became `gz`. So today "Gazebo" means the new simulator, "Gazebo Classic" means the old one, and "Ignition" is a historical name you will still meet in forum posts. The rename did not change the physics, but it changed the commands, the plugin names and the documentation, which is exactly enough to break every tutorial written before 2022.

That history gives you a practical filter. An outdated tutorial usually betrays itself in the first screen:

- it types `roslaunch` (the ROS 1 command) or `ros2 launch gazebo_ros ...`;
- it runs `ign gazebo` instead of `gz sim`;
- its URDF loads plugins named `libgazebo_ros_*.so`;
- it talks about `.world` files and `gazebo_ros_pkgs` as if they were the current stack.

None of those are crimes; they were correct once. But following them on Jazzy wastes an afternoon on missing packages and renamed plugins. Close the tab and find a Harmonic-era tutorial instead.

Current Gazebo releases are named alphabetically: Fortress, Garden, Harmonic, Ionic, Jetty. Garden is already end of life, so a guide that recommends it is out of date even though the name sounds modern. Install the release paired with your ROS 2 distribution, or you will fight build errors that have nothing to do with your robot.

| ROS 2 distro | Gazebo release | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Humble | Fortress | LTS pair |
| Jazzy | Harmonic | LTS pair, what this course uses |
| Kilted | Ionic | — |
| Lyrical | Jetty | LTS pair, the newest |

The source for this is the Gazebo documentation's compatibility table, which also marks other combinations as "possible, but use with caution". In practice, "use with caution" means you are the first person to try that pair and the build errors are yours to debug. The ROS-Gazebo bridge is compiled against a particular Gazebo version, so a mismatched pair can compile and then fail at runtime, which is the worst kind of failure.

On Ubuntu 24.04 with Jazzy, one command installs the right Gazebo and the ROS bridge together:

```bash
sudo apt install ros-jazzy-ros-gz
```

The package name is a small history lesson of its own: `gazebo_ros_pkgs` connected ROS to Gazebo Classic, and `ros_gz` connects ROS 2 to the new Gazebo. `gazebo_ros_pkgs` has no Jazzy release at all, so a tutorial that tells you to install it was written for the previous era. After installing, source `/opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash` (the `gz` command this brings lives under `/opt/ros/jazzy/opt/gz_tools_vendor`, so it is not on the PATH before that), run `gz sim --versions` and check that it prints 8.x, not some older version left on the system.

## Choosing a simulator

ROS 2 can talk to more than one simulator, and the practical answer to "which one should I learn" is "the one that matches what you are learning right now". The course's decision framework has three rules. Gazebo first, because it is the only simulator wired natively into ROS 2; MuJoCo when contact dynamics and learning are the point; Isaac Sim only when you have the hardware and a concrete reason.

| Simulator | Cost and hardware | Strength | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gazebo | free, runs on the laptop you already have | the only simulator natively wired into ROS 2 | learn it first |
| MuJoCo | free, open source, CPU-first, no GPU | contact dynamics used as a research standard for locomotion and manipulation learning ([Todorov, Erez & Tassa, IROS 2012](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2012.6386109)) | reinforcement learning, legged robots |
| NVIDIA Isaac Sim / Isaac Lab | free download, but the minimum is an RTX 4080 with 16 GB VRAM and 32 GB RAM ([NVIDIA system requirements](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/6.0.0/installation/requirements.html), checked October 2026); data-centre cards without RT cores (A100, H100) are not supported | photoreal rendering and synthetic data | only with the RTX hardware and a specific reason |

Gazebo's advantage is not that it has the prettiest physics. It is that the whole ROS 2 ecosystem assumes it: the bridge, the launch files, the sensor message types and half the tutorials you will read all expect it. For a first robot, that integration is worth more than any benchmark. It also runs on ordinary integrated graphics, which is why this lesson can be produced on a machine with no GPU at all.

MuJoCo wins where contacts dominate: a foot hitting the ground, a gripper closing on an object, a hand manipulating a tool. It is CPU-first, so you do not need a graphics card, and it is the default in robot-learning papers. If your interest is reinforcement learning or legged robots, you will end up there anyway. It is not, however, the natural place to learn how ROS 2 topics and TF fit together.

Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab are the photoreal option, and their hardware requirements are published: the download is free, but the minimum is an RTX 4080 with 16 GB of VRAM and 32 GB of RAM (NVIDIA's system-requirements page, checked October 2026). Data-centre cards such as the A100 and H100 are not supported at all, because they lack RT cores; paying more for a compute card does not help. Synthetic-data generation, a warehouse full of photoreal pallets, or a camera-based policy trained in simulation are good reasons. "It looks impressive in a video" is not.

Two more names come up often, and the current status of each matters more than the original verdict. PyBullet: the course says skip it, and its older justification was that there had been no release since 2022. That specific claim is now slightly out of date. PyPI shows version 3.2.6 in November 2023 and 3.2.7 in January 2025, so the project is not dead. The advice still stands, though: occasional maintenance releases are not the same as active development, and PyBullet is not where new robotics work is happening. Learn it only if an existing codebase forces you to.

Genesis is the opposite case. The course says watch it but do not build a portfolio on it yet, because version 1.0 was fresh and it had no ROS 2 integration. It has moved fast since then: version 1.4.0 appeared on PyPI in September 2026, and 1.4.4 followed in October. Fast movement cuts both ways; features arrive quickly, and so do API changes. Check its current ROS 2 support before you rely on it, and keep the project small until the integration is proven.

## What the simulator is stepping

A physics simulator is not a video player. It keeps a state — every link's pose and velocity, every contact force, every joint effort — and advances that state by a fixed time step. In this world's SDF, `<max_step_size>0.001</max_step_size>` means each physics tick is one millisecond of simulated time. A thousand ticks make one simulated second. The solver integrates forces and resolves contacts at that rate whether or not anyone is watching a window.

The <Term id="real-time-factor">real-time factor</Term> (RTF) is how fast simulated time runs relative to wall-clock time. An RTF of 1.0 means one second in the world takes about one second on your clock; 0.5 means the simulation is half as fast as reality; values above 1.0 mean it is running ahead. Gazebo shows RTF in the GUI; headless, read it with `gz topic -e -t /world/obstacles/stats -n 1` (the `real_time_factor` field). If it stays well below 1, make the sensors cheaper first — lower the camera's resolution or its `update_rate` — rather than shrinking `max_step_size`: a smaller step means more physics ticks per simulated second, so RTF falls further. When the laptop is busy, RTF drops first and sensor rates follow: a lidar set to 10 Hz may publish at 7 Hz of wall time while still being "10 Hz" in sim time. That is normal under load, not a broken sensor.

Contact is what makes the world feel solid. Wheels push on the ground, a caster slides, a box does not sink. Bad inertias, overlapping collision shapes, or friction that is orders of magnitude off turn contact into explosions, jitter or tunnelling — the robot looks possessed, not broken in a way `ros2 topic list` can explain. Animation-only tools (many CAD viewers, pure RViz motion without a physics server) move meshes on a timeline; they do not solve contacts or accumulate odometry. Gazebo does. That distinction is why the hand checks later in this lesson matter: if the physics is wrong, the pretty picture still lies.

Unless another engine is selected (`gz sim --physics-engine`, highest priority; or the Physics plugin's `<engine><filename>`), Gazebo Harmonic solves contact with DART (`gz-physics-dartsim-plugin`); the `type` attribute on `<physics>` does not choose it, which is why this world can say `type="ignored"`. Friction lives in `<surface><friction><ode><mu>` and `<mu2>` on each collision — the URDF `<mu1>`/`<mu2>` tags in `sensors.gazebo.xacro` below become exactly these — and that is the knob for a robot that veers, spins or skids. Older Gazebo Classic tutorials tell you to raise `<surface><contact><ode><kp>` / `<kd>` (contact stiffness and damping) when a body sinks or jitters, but the default DART plugin does not read those two; for sinking or jitter, fix the masses and inertias first, then make sure no collision shapes overlap when the robot spawns.

Month 7 adds a MuJoCo practice lesson (`mujoco-so101`). Isaac and Webots still do **not** get separate practice lessons here. MuJoCo, Isaac and Webots are named in the table above so you know when to reach for them; what you practise in this lesson is world files, sensors, bridges and hand arithmetic — that way of thinking transfers, and the product-specific click paths for Isaac or Webots do not need a second month.

## A world of your own

URDF describes one robot for ROS: links, joints, inertias, frames. <Term id="sdf">SDF</Term> (Simulation Description Format) is Gazebo's native format, and it describes a whole world: the sun, the ground, static obstacles and the robots standing in it. The two formats overlap but answer different questions. When you spawn a URDF, Gazebo converts it to SDF internally, which is why a URDF can carry Gazebo-specific extras inside `<gazebo>` tags without breaking RViz.

An SDF file is nested like this:

| Level | What it is | This lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `world` | one simulated scene | `obstacles` |
| `model` | a named body (robot, wall, box) | `walls`, `red_box`, `m4bot` after spawn |
| `link` | a rigid piece inside a model | each obstacle's single `link` |
| `collision` | the shape physics touches | boxes and cylinders used for contact |
| `visual` | the shape the GUI draws | often the same geometry, sometimes simpler |

<Term id="collision-geometry">Collision geometry</Term> and visual geometry are allowed to differ. A wheel can show a detailed mesh while colliding as a cylinder; a thin decorative fin can be visual-only so the robot never snags on it physically — but a `gpu_lidar` renders visuals, so that fin still appears in the scan. Mixing them up is a classic bug: you see a gap in the GUI and the robot still bumps, because the collision box is larger than the painted mesh. `<static>true</static>` marks a model that never moves and is skipped by the dynamic solver — walls and test obstacles should be static so they cannot tip over and so they cost almost nothing to simulate.

This world does not pull models from Fuel, Gazebo's online library. Offline files load the same way on a plane, in CI, and on a machine whose DNS cannot reach the model server. Fuel is fine for demos; it is a fragile dependency for a course world whose numbers you will check by hand.

Here is the world this lesson uses. It is deliberately small and completely self-contained.

The `<physics>` block and the four system plugins at the top are what make a world work at all. `<max_step_size>0.001</max_step_size>` is a one-millisecond physics step (1 000 Hz of simulated integration). `<real_time_factor>1.0</real_time_factor>` asks Gazebo to keep simulated time aligned with the wall clock when the machine can manage it. Leave `max_step_size` huge and contacts become unstable. Both elements have SDF defaults — 0.001 s and 1.0 — so leaving them out gives the same values; writing them down just makes the world file say what it does. Gazebo reports the RTF it actually achieves either way.

| System plugin | Role | If it is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Physics` | integrates forces, contacts, joints each `max_step_size` | nothing moves; the world is a still picture |
| `UserCommands` | lets spawners and CLI tools add or remove entities | `create` cannot insert the robot into a running world |
| `SceneBroadcaster` | publishes the scene for GUIs and some introspection | a later GUI client has nothing to attach to |
| `Sensors` (ogre2 here) | renders gpu_lidar / camera into Gazebo topics | sensors exist in the SDF but no messages are published — `ros2 topic hz /scan` prints nothing; looks like a dead bridge |

Headless runs still need `Physics` and `Sensors`. They often keep `SceneBroadcaster` so you can attach a GUI later. Skipping `Sensors` is the mistake that costs beginners the most time: `ros2 topic list` shows `/scan` (the bridge created it), the bridge is "fine", and yet no message ever arrives — `ros2 topic hz /scan` prints nothing — because the world never rendered a beam.

Why write all four? If a world file has no `<plugin>` at all, gz-sim falls back to its default `server.config`, which loads `Physics`, `UserCommands` and `SceneBroadcaster` — but not `Sensors`. As soon as the world lists any plugin, that default file is not loaded at all, so a world that names only `Sensors` loses physics too. Copying half a world file from a tutorial is the easiest way to hit either trap.

`<static>true</static>` means the obstacle never moves and costs no physics computation. A wall, a box and a pillar in a test world do not need to fall over, so marking them static saves time and removes a source of surprise. Poses are written as x y z roll pitch yaw, in metres and radians, and a box's pose is its centre, not a corner. The red box is 0.4 m wide with its centre at x = 2.0, so its front face is at 2.0 − 0.4/2 = 2.0 − 0.2 = 1.80 m. The walls are 0.1 m thick and their centres sit at ±3.0 m, so their inner faces are at 3.0 − 0.05 = 2.95 m. Those two numbers, 1.80 and 2.95, will come back in the arithmetic later; write them down now.

Everything is defined in the file rather than downloaded from Gazebo's online model library, Fuel. That is a deliberate choice: the world loads offline, loads the same way every time, and cannot fail because a model server is slow. On the editor's machine Fuel downloads were not even reachable, which would have made a "download the sun and the ground" tutorial impossible to follow. Copying a few dozen lines into a file is cheaper than depending on the network.

The second is the description Gazebo actually loads. It includes the Lesson 3 robot and the sensor file, then adds Gazebo's own plugins for the wheels and the joints:

```xml

     plugins for driving the wheels and reporting and holding the joints. -->
<robot name="m4bot" xmlns:xacro="http://www.ros.org/wiki/xacro">

  <xacro:include filename="$(find m4_description)/urdf/robot.urdf.xacro"/>
  <xacro:include filename="$(find m4_gazebo)/urdf/sensors.gazebo.xacro"/>

         Lesson 5 replaces this plugin with ros2_control. -->

      <left_joint>left_wheel_joint</left_joint>
      <right_joint>right_wheel_joint</right_joint>
      <wheel_separation>${wheel_separation}</wheel_separation>
      <wheel_radius>${wheel_radius}</wheel_radius>
      <topic>cmd_vel</topic>
      <odom_topic>odom</odom_topic>
      <tf_topic>tf</tf_topic>
      <frame_id>odom</frame_id>
      <child_frame_id>base_footprint</child_frame_id>
      <odom_publish_frequency>30</odom_publish_frequency>
    </plugin>
    <!-- report every joint's position, so robot_state_publisher can publish the TF -->
    <plugin filename="gz-sim-joint-state-publisher-system" name="gz::sim::systems::JointStatePublisher">
      <topic>joint_states</topic>
    </plugin>
    <!-- hold the pan-tilt head where it is told (otherwise gravity tips the camera over) -->
    <plugin filename="gz-sim-joint-position-controller-system" name="gz::sim::systems::JointPositionController">
```

Six details in these two files are worth understanding, because they are the same six that break other people's robots:

1. `$(find m4_description)` locates the other package when xacro runs (here, every time the launch file starts). The description package and the Gazebo package stay separate, so the RViz-only workflow from Lesson 3 does not need Gazebo installed, and this package does not duplicate the robot's geometry.
2. `<gazebo reference="link">` adds simulator-only properties to a named link. Here it is friction, `mu1` and `mu2`: the wheels grip with 1.0, the caster ball slides with 0.001. Without that difference the caster drags, the robot veers to one side, and you spend an hour blaming the diff-drive plugin for a friction problem.
3. The lidar is a `gpu_lidar` with 360 beams from −180° to 179°. The maximum angle is π minus one degree (3.12414 rad), so the first and last beam do not point the same way and you get exactly 360 distinct directions, 1° apart. It runs at 10 Hz, sees from 0.20 m to 8 m, and adds Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 1 cm.
4. `<gz_frame_id>` sets the `frame_id` written into every message, and it must be a link name from the URDF. Here it is `lidar_link` for the scan and `camera_optical_link` for images. This is Lesson 3's frame discipline in practice: a sensor message whose frame does not exist in TF is unusable in RViz, no matter how good the data is.
5. The camera sits on `camera_link`, whose x axis points forward, which is the direction a Gazebo camera looks. It stamps its images with `camera_optical_link`, the frame image tools expect, where z points forward and x points right. The sensor is on one link; the picture is labelled with another. That is not a bug, it is the convention.
6. Three kinds of Gazebo plugin do the work. DiffDrive listens on `cmd_vel` and publishes odometry plus the `odom → base_footprint` transform; Lesson 5 replaces it with `ros2_control`. JointStatePublisher reports every joint position, so `robot_state_publisher` publishes the TF just as the sliders did in Lesson 3. JointPositionController holds pan and tilt where they are told; without it, gravity tips the camera head over the moment physics starts, and your first camera image is the floor.

The minimum range of 0.20 m deserves its own sentence. The mast stands 0.18 m behind the lidar (`lidar_joint` at x = 0.12 m, `mast_joint` at x = −0.06 m: 0.12 − (−0.06) = 0.18 m), so beams pointing backwards would hit the robot itself and appear as a fake obstacle in every scan. A real lidar sees its own mounting hardware too, which is why drivers offer a minimum range or an angle filter. Setting the minimum to 0.20 m drops those beams cleanly.

### Sensors: simulation versus the datasheet

A simulated lidar is ideal geometry plus optional noise, not a clone of a particular hardware datasheet. Rays travel in straight lines through empty space and return a range — for a `gpu_lidar`, they hit the **visual** geometry the renderer draws, not the collision shapes. A collision-only object is therefore invisible to it, and a visual-only decoration shows up in the scan unless you hide it with `<visibility_flags>`. In this world every obstacle's visual and collision are the same box or cylinder, so the numbers below are unaffected. There is no dust, no sun-bloom, no multi-path from a glass door unless you model those effects on purpose. Real units also carry a range bias, quantised range steps, dropped points on black or glossy surfaces, and a close-range blind zone; Gazebo's lidar models none of those by default — `<min>` simply clips the range. The Gaussian `stddev` of 0.01 m here is a teaching knob so that a single scan disagrees with the hand calculation by about a centimetre; averaging five scans in `scan_probe.py` shrinks that scatter but does not remove it.

That is why this lesson insists on the hand check. If the red-box face is at 1.80 m and the lidar is at 0.12 m, the straight-ahead beam must be near 1.68 m. Agreement within the noise tells you the frames, the bridge and the world file are the same story. Disagreement means you debug before you trust SLAM or a controller. On a real robot you will still compare a tape measure to a scan for the same reason — the datasheet's max range and accuracy bands are not a substitute for a one-time geometric sanity check in *your* mounting.

## Who drives the wheels

Three layers show up in this month, and only one of them is "the motors":

1. **This lesson — Gazebo <Term id="diff-drive">DiffDrive</Term>.** The `gz-sim-diff-drive-system` plugin subscribes to `/cmd_vel`, spins the wheel joints inside the physics engine, and publishes odometry plus `odom → base_footprint`. It is the fastest way to get a moving robot in simulation, and it is also Gazebo-specific: there is no such plugin on a real motor board.
2. **Lesson 5 — `ros2_control`.** The DiffDrive plugin goes away. A hardware interface (Gazebo plugin or real electronics) exposes state and command interfaces; `diff_drive_controller` turns body twists into wheel speeds. The same controller YAML is meant to run in sim, on mock hardware and on the machine.
3. **A real robot.** Encoders, current limits, backlash and friction replace perfect joint integration. The topic names and the controller graph can stay; the perfect odometry will not.

Sensors do not care which layer commands the wheels. That is why `sensors.gazebo.xacro` is shared with Lesson 5: the lidar and camera keep the same topics and frames while the drive stack is swapped. If you skip straight from DiffDrive teleop to Nav2 without `ros2_control`, you learn navigation on an API that disappears the day the robot ships — Lesson 5 exists to close that gap.

## Launch and bridge

Gazebo and ROS 2 are separate systems with separate transport. Gazebo publishes on its own topics with its own message types; ROS 2 nodes cannot see them until something copies selected topics across and converts the types. That something is <Term id="ros-gz-bridge">ros_gz_bridge</Term>. The bridge is deliberately explicit: you list each topic, its type on both sides and the direction, and nothing else crosses. The launch file starts everything in the right order.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-4/gazebo-sim/m4_gazebo/launch/sim.launch.py" />

```python
"""m4bot in the obstacles world: Gazebo, the robot, the ROS-Gazebo bridge (+ RViz).

ros2 launch m4_gazebo sim.launch.py                   # Gazebo window and RViz
ros2 launch m4_gazebo sim.launch.py headless:=true    # no windows (servers, CI)"""
import os

from ament_index_python.packages import get_package_share_directory
from launch import LaunchDescription
from launch.actions import DeclareLaunchArgument, IncludeLaunchDescription
from launch.conditions import IfCondition, UnlessCondition
from launch.launch_description_sources import PythonLaunchDescriptionSource
from launch.substitutions import Command, LaunchConfiguration
from launch_ros.actions import Node
from launch_ros.parameter_descriptions import ParameterValue


def generate_launch_description():
    share = get_package_share_directory('m4_gazebo')
    world = os.path.join(share, 'worlds', 'obstacles.sdf')
    gz_launch = os.path.join(get_package_share_directory('ros_gz_sim'), 'launch', 'gz_sim.launch.py')
    robot_description = ParameterValue(
        Command(['xacro ', os.path.join(share, 'urdf', 'm4bot_gz.urdf.xacro')]), value_type=str)
    headless = LaunchConfiguration('headless')

    def gazebo(gz_args, condition):
        return IncludeLaunchDescription(PythonLaunchDescriptionSource(gz_launch),
                                        launch_arguments={'gz_args': gz_args}.items(),
                                        condition=condition)

    return LaunchDescription([
        DeclareLaunchArgument('headless', default_value='false',
                              description='true = server only, no Gazebo window and no RViz'),
        # -r: start running at once; -s: server only; --headless-rendering: sensors without a display
        gazebo(f'-r {world}', UnlessCondition(headless)),
        gazebo(f'-s -r --headless-rendering {world}', IfCondition(headless)),
        # the same robot_state_publisher as Lesson 3, but on the simulator's clock
        Node(package='robot_state_publisher', executable='robot_state_publisher',
             parameters=[{'robot_description': robot_description, 'use_sim_time': True}]),
        # put the robot into the running world, from the robot_description topic
        Node(package='ros_gz_sim', executable='create',
             arguments=['-topic', 'robot_description', '-name', 'm4bot', '-z', '0.01']),
        # Gazebo topics <-> ROS 2 topics, as listed in bridge.yaml
        Node(package='ros_gz_bridge', executable='parameter_bridge',
             parameters=[{'config_file': os.path.join(share, 'config', 'bridge.yaml'),
                          'use_sim_time': True}]),
        Node(package='rviz2', executable='rviz2',
             arguments=['-d', os.path.join(share, 'rviz', 'sim.rviz')],
             parameters=[{'use_sim_time': True}], condition=UnlessCondition(headless)),
    ])
```

The launch file includes `ros_gz_sim`'s own `gz_sim.launch.py` instead of starting `gz sim` by hand, because the include already knows where the Gazebo binaries and plugins live. `-r` starts the simulation running immediately rather than paused. The headless variant adds `-s` for server only and `--headless-rendering` so sensors render without a display; that combination is what the editor used, and it is what any CI machine should use.

### Headless checklist

On a server, in CI, or on a laptop with no free display:

1. Launch with `headless:=true` (flags `-s -r --headless-rendering` on the Gazebo server).
2. Confirm the process is alive: `ros2 topic list` shows `/clock`, `/scan`, `/odom` after the bridge starts.
3. Do **not** require a GUI to trust the run. Use `ros2 topic hz /scan`, `python3 scan_probe.py`, and `python3 snap_camera.py` — the same scripts as a desktop session. `ros2 topic hz` measures the wall-clock rate by default, so expect about 10 Hz only when the RTF is near 1, and less when the RTF drops below 1; add `--use-sim-time` to measure in sim time instead.
4. If `/scan` exists but `hz` stays silent, check the Sensors system plugin and the bridge entry before restarting the desktop compositor.
5. Set `export GZ_IP=127.0.0.1` when Gazebo transport cannot find peers on a locked-down network (the editor needed this).

`robot_state_publisher` is the same node as in Lesson 3, with one addition: <Term id="use-sim-time">use_sim_time</Term> set true. Every node that talks to a simulator must use the simulator's clock from `/clock`. Otherwise timestamps from Gazebo, which are seconds since the simulation started, and timestamps from the wall clock disagree, and TF reports "extrapolation into the future" because a node is asking for a transform at a time the simulation has not reached. That is point 5 of Lesson 3's debug list, now with a concrete cause.

The `create` node spawns the robot from the `robot_description` topic, not from a file path. It reads the same description that `robot_state_publisher` received, so the model in Gazebo and the TF in ROS 2 cannot drift apart. The small `-z 0.01` lifts the robot one centimetre above the floor, which avoids a first-step collision with the ground plane.

## Reading the sensors

First the lidar. The probe subscribes to `/scan`, averages five scans to knock down the noise, and prints a short summary: how many beams, their angular range, how many have a hit, what is straight ahead and where the nearest object is.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-4/gazebo-sim/scan_probe.py" />

```python
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Read five LaserScans from /scan and summarise them: what the lidar sees, in numbers.

usage: python3 scan_probe.py        (with the simulation running)
Each range is averaged over the five scans, which takes out most of the sensor noise."""
import math

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from rclpy.qos import qos_profile_sensor_data
from sensor_msgs.msg import LaserScan


def summarise(scans):
    scan = scans[0]
    n = len(scan.ranges)
    ranges = [sum(s.ranges[i] for s in scans) / len(scans) for i in range(n)]
    angle = lambda i: math.degrees(scan.angle_min + i * scan.angle_increment)
    valid = [(r, i) for i, r in enumerate(ranges) if scan.range_min <= r <= scan.range_max]
    ahead = min(range(n), key=lambda i: abs(angle(i)))         # the beam closest to 0 degrees
    print(f'frame_id: {scan.header.frame_id}')
    print(f'beams: {n}, from {angle(0):.1f} to {angle(n - 1):.1f} deg, '
          f'step {math.degrees(scan.angle_increment):.2f} deg')
    print(f'range limits: {scan.range_min:.2f} to {scan.range_max:.2f} m')
    print(f'beams with a hit: {len(valid)}, without (inf): {n - len(valid)}')
    print(f'straight ahead ({angle(ahead):.1f} deg): {ranges[ahead]:.2f} m')
    r, i = min(valid)
    # a round object returns almost the same range over several beams: report their middle
    near = [j for d, j in valid if d <= r + 0.02 and abs(angle(j) - angle(i)) < 20]
    print(f'nearest hit: {r:.2f} m, centred at {sum(angle(j) for j in near) / len(near):.0f} deg')


def main():
    rclpy.init()
    node = Node('scan_probe')
    got = []
    node.create_subscription(LaserScan, 'scan', got.append, qos_profile_sensor_data)
    while rclpy.ok() and len(got) < 5:
        rclpy.spin_once(node, timeout_sec=0.5)
    summarise(got[:5])
    node.destroy_node()
    rclpy.shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

Note the QoS profile: `qos_profile_sensor_data` is best effort, which is Lesson 2's QoS section applied. Subscribe to sensor streams this way; it also matches a reliable publisher, so you do not have to know how the other side was configured. Getting this wrong produces the maddening situation where `ros2 topic list` shows the topic and your subscriber receives nothing.

A simulator you can check by hand is a simulator you can trust. Once the numbers agree, you can use the simulator to debug things you cannot compute by hand, such as a controller.

## Physics step size and contact stability

*What the simulator is stepping* said that a `max_step_size` of 0.001 s is one millisecond per physics tick, and the table below warns not to shrink it just to rescue the real-time factor. This section looks at the other side: what goes wrong when the step is **too large** for the contact, and how to check a step against a stability criterion before trusting it. It is **not** the inertia check from Lesson 3, and **not** the MuJoCo `solref` grip sweep in Month 7.

**Sources actually opened.**

- **SDFormat 1.11 `physics.sdf` (spec source on GitHub; the sdformat.org spec page itself is a JavaScript shell, so it is not cited):** `max_step_size` (default 0.001) is the "maximum time step size at which every system in simulation can interact with the states of the world"; `real_time_factor` (default 1.0) is the target ratio of simulation time to real time. 以原文为准.
- **Gazebo Sim 8 API — Physics engines:** Gazebo uses the DART engine by default; Bullet and Bullet Featherstone are preliminary alternatives, chosen with the Physics plugin's `<engine><filename>` or `gz sim --physics-engine`. 以原文为准.
- **Wikipedia — Euler method** (stability region `|1 + hk| ≤ 1` for `y′ = ky`; unstable for stiff problems when the step is too large) and **Wikipedia — Semi-implicit Euler method** (update `v` first, then `x` with the new `v`; first order, symplectic, nearly conserves energy). 以原文为准.

### A contact as a stiff spring-damper

A penalty contact behaves like a very stiff spring `k` in parallel with a damper `c` while the body penetrates the ground (a Kelvin–Voigt element). Its natural frequency is `ω = √(k/m)` and its damping ratio `ζ = c / (2√(k·m))`. A stiff contact has a large `ω`, so it is the **fastest dynamics in the world**, and the step has to resolve it. For the semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler update `v ← v + Δt·a`, `x ← x + Δt·v` used below, the step matrix of `m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = 0` is stable when `(ωΔt)² + 4ζ(ωΔt) < 4`, that is, when `ωΔt` stays below

<Formula label="Semi-implicit Euler step limit">(ωΔt)_max = 2(√(ζ² + 1) − ζ)</Formula>

With no damping this is the classic `ωΔt < 2`. Explicit (forward) Euler is far stricter: its eigenvalues `λ = −ζω ± iω√(1 − ζ²)` need `|1 + λΔt| < 1`, which works out to `ωΔt < 2ζ`; an undamped spring is never stable under explicit Euler. Stable is **not** the same as accurate: a step just inside the limit still samples a contact only a couple of times.

### Fair setup

`gazebo_step_contact_stability_toy.py` is a **synthetic** stdlib stand-in, **not** DART and not bit-exact to Gazebo. **Model assumptions:** a 1 kg box moving only vertically; one contact point; a linear Kelvin–Voigt contact with `k = 2.5 × 10⁵ N/m` and `ζ = 0.2` (so `ω = 500 rad/s`, `c = 200 N·s/m`), active only while the box penetrates and allowed to pull slightly near separation (no force clamp); no friction; gravity 9.81 m/s²; no contact event location (contact switches only at step boundaries). DART resolves contact with constraints rather than this spring, and, as noted above, ignores `<contact><ode><kp>`/`<kd>`, so `k` and `c` here are **not** Gazebo knobs. What transfers is the rule: the step must resolve the fastest dynamics.

The criterion gives `Δt < 3.279 ms` for semi-implicit Euler and `Δt < 0.800 ms` for explicit Euler on this contact.

| Arm | Step `Δt` | Steps per simulated second |
|---|---|---|
| A_coarse_3ms | 3 ms (stable, `ωΔt = 1.5`) | 333 |
| B_default_1ms | 1 ms (the world's `max_step_size`) | 1000 |
| C_fine_0.1ms | 0.1 ms | 10000 |

A reference run at 10 µs (not an arm) stands in for truth. **Common random numbers:** for each seed, one drop height (0.10 ± 0.02 m) and one disturbance force `f(t)` (σ = 0.5 N, zero-order hold on a 0.1 ms grid) are drawn once; every arm and the reference apply that same `f(t)`, averaged over their own step, so every arm receives the same impulse over any whole step. Regimes: `impact_drop` (fall onto the ground, 0.6 s) and `gentle_rest` (start at static equilibrium, 0.2 s). Metrics: maximum penetration and its error against the reference, bounce count (lift-offs fast enough to rise ≥ 1 mm), and the first-bounce coefficient of restitution `COR = √(h_apex / h_drop)` (so `COR²` is the fraction of energy kept). Five seeds, mean±sd. "Steps per simulated second" is the compute proxy; it is **blind** to per-step solver iterations, sensor rendering and everything else that sets a real RTF.

```python
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Synthetic physics step-size (max_step_size) vs contact stability toy. Stdlib only.

Stand-in, NOT Gazebo/DART: a 1 kg box falls onto the ground through a linear
Kelvin-Voigt contact (spring k in parallel with damper c, active only while the
box penetrates the ground). Every arm uses the SAME semi-implicit (symplectic)
Euler integrator; only the step dt changes, like <max_step_size>.

  v[n+1] = v[n] + dt * a(x[n], v[n], t[n])
  x[n+1] = x[n] + dt * v[n+1]

Stability criterion of this integrator on the contact spring-damper
(omega = sqrt(k/m), zeta = c / (2 sqrt(k m))):
  (omega*dt)^2 + 4*zeta*(omega*dt) < 4   <=>   omega*dt < 2*(sqrt(zeta^2+1) - zeta)
Explicit (forward) Euler needs |1 + lambda*dt| < 1, i.e. omega*dt < 2*zeta.

Arms (shared plant, shared drop heights and shared disturbance force f(t)):
  A_coarse_3ms   dt = 3 ms   (stable, but only about two steps per contact)
  B_default_1ms  dt = 1 ms   (the lesson world's max_step_size)
  C_fine_0.1ms   dt = 0.1 ms (ten times the steps of B)
Reference: the same integrator at dt = 10 us (not an arm).

Common random numbers: per seed, one drop height and one disturbance sequence
f(t) (zero-order hold on a 0.1 ms grid) are drawn once; every arm and the
reference apply the SAME f(t), averaged over their own step so that every arm
receives the same impulse (integral of f) over any whole step.

Regimes (5 seeds): impact_drop (coarse loses to fine on every seed; A vs B is
erratic) and gentle_rest (no impact: every error is small next to the static
sag, but the gaps are consistent, not a near-tie). "Consistent" means only
|paired mean| > paired sd over seeds; the sign count says how many seeds
disagree with the paired mean. No presumed winner. Real Gazebo / DART: 本页未运行.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random

M = 1.0  # kg
G = 9.81  # m/s^2
K = 2.5e5  # N/m contact stiffness (synthetic)
ZETA = 0.2  # contact damping ratio (synthetic)
C = 2.0 * ZETA * math.sqrt(K * M)  # N s/m
OMEGA = math.sqrt(K / M)  # rad/s
NOISE_DT = 1e-4  # disturbance grid, s
NOISE_SIGMA = 0.5  # N, disturbance force sd
LIFTOFF_V = math.sqrt(2.0 * G * 1e-3)  # count a bounce if it would rise >= 1 mm
SEEDS = (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)
ARMS = (("A_coarse_3ms", 3e-3), ("B_default_1ms", 1e-3), ("C_fine_0.1ms", 1e-4))
REF_DT = 1e-5
REGIMES = (("impact_drop", 0.6), ("gentle_rest", 0.2))  # (name, simulated seconds)


def si_euler_limit(zeta: float = ZETA) -> float:
    """Largest stable omega*dt for semi-implicit Euler on m x'' + c x' + k x = 0."""
    return 2.0 * (math.sqrt(zeta * zeta + 1.0) - zeta)


def explicit_euler_limit(zeta: float = ZETA) -> float:
    """Largest stable omega*dt for explicit Euler (|1 + lambda dt| < 1)."""
    return 2.0 * zeta


def spectral_radius_si(dt: float) -> float:
    """|largest eigenvalue| of the semi-implicit Euler step on the contact spring-damper."""
    a = dt * C / M
    b = dt * dt * K / M
    tr = 2.0 - a - b
    det = 1.0 - a
    disc = tr * tr - 4.0 * det
    if disc >= 0.0:
        r = math.sqrt(disc)
        return max(abs((tr + r) / 2.0), abs((tr - r) / 2.0))
    return math.sqrt(det)  # complex pair: |lambda|^2 = det


def spectral_radius_explicit(dt: float) -> float:
    """|1 + lambda dt| for explicit Euler on the same spring-damper."""
    wd = OMEGA * math.sqrt(1.0 - ZETA * ZETA)
    re = 1.0 - ZETA * OMEGA * dt
    return math.hypot(re, wd * dt)


def shared_draws(seed: int, regime: str, t_end: float) -> tuple[float, list[float]]:
    """Common random numbers: identical for every arm and the reference."""
    rng = random.Random(9_000 + 31 * seed + (0 if regime == "impact_drop" else 7))
    h0 = 0.10 + rng.uniform(-0.02, 0.02) if regime == "impact_drop" else 0.0
    n = int(round(t_end / NOISE_DT)) + 2
    force = [rng.gauss(0.0, NOISE_SIGMA) for _ in range(n)]
    return h0, force


def impulse_prefix(force: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    """P[i] = integral of the zero-order-hold f(t) from 0 to i*NOISE_DT."""
    p = [0.0]
    for f in force:
        p.append(p[-1] + f * NOISE_DT)
    return p


def impulse_until(force: list[float], prefix: list[float], t: float) -> float:
    """Integral of f from 0 to t (exact for the zero-order-hold grid)."""
    i = min(int(t / NOISE_DT + 1e-9), len(force) - 1)
    return prefix[i] + force[i] * (t - i * NOISE_DT)


def simulate(dt: float, regime: str, h0: float, force: list[float], t_end: float) -> dict:
    """Integrate one run. x is the box bottom height (negative = penetration)."""
    if regime == "impact_drop":
        x, v = h0, 0.0
    else:
        x, v = -M * G / K, 0.0  # resting exactly at static equilibrium
    steps = int(round(t_end / dt))
    max_pen = max(0.0, -x)
    bounces = 0
    in_contact = x < 0.0
    first_apex = None  # highest point after the first liftoff, before the next touch
    tracking_apex = False
    prefix = impulse_prefix(force)
    for n in range(steps):
        t = n * dt
        # step-averaged disturbance: same impulse per whole step for every dt
        f_ext = (impulse_until(force, prefix, t + dt) - impulse_until(force, prefix, t)) / dt
        f_contact = (-K * x - C * v) if x < 0.0 else 0.0
        a = (f_contact + f_ext) / M - G
        v = v + dt * a
        x = x + dt * v
        if x < 0.0 and not in_contact:
            in_contact = True
            tracking_apex = False
        elif x >= 0.0 and in_contact:
            in_contact = False
            if v >= LIFTOFF_V:
                bounces += 1
                if bounces == 1:
                    tracking_apex = True
                    first_apex = x
        if tracking_apex:
            first_apex = max(first_apex, x)
        max_pen = max(max_pen, -x)
    # Coefficient of restitution from heights: e = sqrt(h_apex / h_drop) (energy ratio e^2)
    first_cor = math.sqrt(first_apex / h0) if (first_apex is not None and h0 > 0.0) else None
    return {"max_pen": max_pen, "bounces": bounces, "cor": first_cor, "steps": steps}


def mean_sd(xs: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
    mu = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    var = sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1)
    return mu, math.sqrt(var)


def sign_note(diffs: list[float], m: float) -> str:
    """How many seeds disagree in sign with the paired mean (zeros listed apart)."""
    n, opp, zero = len(diffs), sum(1 for d in diffs if d * m < 0), sum(1 for d in diffs if d == 0)
    if opp == 0 and zero == 0:
        return f"same sign on all {n} seeds"
    note = f"{opp} of {n} seeds {'has' if opp == 1 else 'have'} the opposite sign"
    return note + (f", {zero} of {n} equal" if zero else "")


def paired(xa: list[float], xb: list[float], digits: int) -> tuple[float, float, str]:
    """Paired per-seed difference xa - xb: mean, sd and its label.
    tie: identical on every seed; within seed noise: |mean| <= sd;
    consistent gap: |mean| > sd (the only meaning of "consistent" here)."""
    diffs = [a - b for a, b in zip(xa, xb)]
    if all(d == 0.0 for d in diffs):
        return 0.0, 0.0, f"{0.0:.{digits}f}+-{0.0:.{digits}f}: tie: identical on every seed"
    m, s = mean_sd(diffs)
    if abs(m) <= s:
        label = f"within seed noise (|m| <= sd), not a consistent gap; {sign_note(diffs, m)}"
    else:
        label = f"consistent gap (|m| > sd), not a tie; {sign_note(diffs, m)}"
    return m, s, f"{m:.{digits}f}+-{s:.{digits}f}: {label}"


def run_regime(regime: str, t_end: float) -> dict:
    """Per arm: lists over seeds of |max_pen - ref| (um), bounces, |COR - ref|, max_pen."""
    out = {name: {"pen_err_um": [], "bounces": [], "cor_err": [], "pen_um": []} for name, _ in ARMS}
    refs = []
    for seed in SEEDS:
        h0, force = shared_draws(seed, regime, t_end)
        ref = simulate(REF_DT, regime, h0, force, t_end)
        refs.append(ref)
        for name, dt in ARMS:
            r = simulate(dt, regime, h0, force, t_end)
            out[name]["pen_err_um"].append(abs(r["max_pen"] - ref["max_pen"]) * 1e6)
            out[name]["pen_um"].append(r["max_pen"] * 1e6)
            out[name]["bounces"].append(float(r["bounces"]))
            if ref["cor"] is not None and r["cor"] is not None:
                out[name]["cor_err"].append(abs(r["cor"] - ref["cor"]))
    out["_ref"] = refs
    return out


def main() -> None:
    print("Synthetic Kelvin-Voigt contact stand-in (not DART); semi-implicit Euler; shared drop heights and f(t)")
    lim = si_euler_limit()
    print(
        f"m={M}kg k={K:.0f}N/m zeta={ZETA} omega={OMEGA:.1f}rad/s seeds={len(SEEDS)} "
        f"noise_sd={NOISE_SIGMA}N ref_dt=10us"
    )
    print(
        f"criterion semi-implicit Euler: omega*dt < 2*(sqrt(zeta^2+1)-zeta) = {lim:.4f} "
        f"-> dt < {lim / OMEGA * 1e3:.3f} ms; explicit Euler: omega*dt < 2*zeta = "
        f"{explicit_euler_limit():.4f} -> dt < {explicit_euler_limit() / OMEGA * 1e3:.3f} ms"
    )
    print("dt_ms omega_dt rho_semi_implicit rho_explicit")
    for dt in (1e-4, 1e-3, 3e-3, 3.2e-3, 3.4e-3, 4e-3):
        rs, re_ = spectral_radius_si(dt), spectral_radius_explicit(dt)
        print(
            f"{dt * 1e3:.1f} {OMEGA * dt:.3f} {rs:.4f}{' UNSTABLE' if rs >= 1 else ''} "
            f"{re_:.4f}{' UNSTABLE' if re_ >= 1 else ''}"
        )
    print("regime arm steps_per_sim_s max_pen_um |pen-ref|_um bounces |COR-ref| (mean+-sd over seeds)")
    summary = {}
    pairs = {}
    for regime, t_end in REGIMES:
        res = run_regime(regime, t_end)
        refs = res["_ref"]
        rp = mean_sd([r["max_pen"] * 1e6 for r in refs])
        rb = mean_sd([float(r["bounces"]) for r in refs])
        line = f"{regime} reference_10us {1 / REF_DT:.0f} {rp[0]:.2f}+-{rp[1]:.2f} - {rb[0]:.2f}+-{rb[1]:.2f}"
        cors = [r["cor"] for r in refs if r["cor"] is not None]
        if cors:
            rc = mean_sd(cors)
            line += f" COR={rc[0]:.4f}+-{rc[1]:.4f}"
        print(line)
        for name, dt in ARMS:
            d = res[name]
            pm, ps = mean_sd(d["pen_um"])
            em, es = mean_sd(d["pen_err_um"])
            bm, bs = mean_sd(d["bounces"])
            cor = "-"
            if len(d["cor_err"]) == len(SEEDS):
                cm, cs = mean_sd(d["cor_err"])
                cor = f"{cm:.4f}+-{cs:.4f}"
            summary[(regime, name)] = (em, es, pm, ps)
            print(
                f"{regime} {name} {1 / dt:.0f} {pm:.2f}+-{ps:.2f} {em:.2f}+-{es:.2f} "
                f"{bm:.2f}+-{bs:.2f} {cor}"
            )
        a, b, c = (res[n] for n, _ in ARMS)
        digits = 2 if regime == "impact_drop" else 3
        rows = [("A-B", "|pen-ref|_um", a, b, "pen_err_um", digits),
                ("A-C", "|pen-ref|_um", a, c, "pen_err_um", digits),
                ("B-C", "|pen-ref|_um", b, c, "pen_err_um", digits),
                ("A-B", "bounces", a, b, "bounces", 2)]
        if regime == "impact_drop":
            rows += [("A-B", "|COR-ref|", a, b, "cor_err", 4), ("B-C", "|COR-ref|", b, c, "cor_err", 4)]
        for tag, metric, x, y, key, dg in rows:
            pairs[(regime, tag, key)] = paired(x[key], y[key], dg)
            print(f"{regime} paired {tag} {metric} {pairs[(regime, tag, key)][2]}")
    ia, ib, ic = (summary[("impact_drop", n)] for n, _ in ARMS)
    print(
        f"impact_drop: |pen-ref| A {ia[0]:.2f}+-{ia[1]:.2f} um, B {ib[0]:.2f}+-{ib[1]:.2f} um, "
        f"C {ic[0]:.2f}+-{ic[1]:.2f} um (A is stable, rho<1, yet about two steps per contact): A and B lose to C "
        f"on every seed; A vs B is erratic, not a consistent gap (paired A-B {pairs[('impact_drop', 'A-B', 'pen_err_um')][0]:.2f}"
        f"+-{pairs[('impact_drop', 'A-B', 'pen_err_um')][1]:.2f} um)"
    )
    ga, gb, gc = (summary[("gentle_rest", n)] for n, _ in ARMS)
    print(
        f"gentle_rest: |pen-ref| A {ga[0]:.3f}+-{ga[1]:.3f} um, B {gb[0]:.3f}+-{gb[1]:.3f} um, "
        f"C {gc[0]:.3f}+-{gc[1]:.3f} um: all small relative to the {M * G / K * 1e6:.2f} um static sag, "
        f"but the gaps are consistent, not a near-tie (paired A-B {pairs[('gentle_rest', 'A-B', 'pen_err_um')][0]:.3f}"
        f"+-{pairs[('gentle_rest', 'A-B', 'pen_err_um')][1]:.3f} um, B-C {pairs[('gentle_rest', 'B-C', 'pen_err_um')][0]:.3f}"
        f"+-{pairs[('gentle_rest', 'B-C', 'pen_err_um')][1]:.3f} um; bounce counts are identical: 0)"
    )
    print("No winner declared: smaller steps cost compute (steps per simulated second); a step past the criterion diverges however cheap it is")
    print("Real Gazebo / DART contact solver: 本页未运行; SDFormat/Gazebo docs 以原文为准")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

### Read the regimes

The criterion table is the check: `ρ` (spectral radius of one step) stays below 1 for every semi-implicit step up to 3.2 ms and jumps to 1.3293 at 3.4 ms; explicit Euler is already unstable at 1 ms (`ρ = 1.0247`). The tests in `tools/test_b12_thevenin_gazebo_toys.py` also integrate a free spring-damper at 0.98× and 1.02× the limit and see it decay and blow up.

The `paired` lines subtract two arms seed by seed on the same draws. On this page a **consistent** gap means only that the paired mean is larger than its seed-to-seed sd (`|m| > sd`); `|m| ≤ sd` is within seed noise. The sign count says how many of the 5 seeds disagree with the mean, which is a separate, stronger check.

- **impact_drop:** the reference keeps COR 0.5181±0.0008 (the no-gravity formula `exp(−ζπ/√(1−ζ²))` gives 0.5266) and three bounces. A, although stable (`ρ = 0.6325`), sees each roughly 6.4 ms contact in about two steps: its penetration error is 624.23±554.20 µm, its COR error 0.2690±0.3767 and its bounce count 3.00±1.73 — the mean matches the reference only by luck, the spread is huge. B's penetration error is 159.87±37.34 µm, C's 6.04±6.15 µm. **Coarse loses to fine:** paired A−C is 618.19±549.08 µm and B−C 153.83±40.77 µm, both consistent gaps with the same sign on all 5 seeds. **A vs B is erratic, not a consistent gap:** paired A−B is 464.36±572.63 µm (|m| ≤ sd; A is worse on 4 of 5 seeds, 1 of 5 seeds has the opposite sign), and the COR error A−B 0.2623±0.3771 is also within seed noise even though A is worse on all 5 seeds — one seed (A's COR error 0.93) dominates the spread. On COR, B (0.0067±0.0011) and C (0.0050±0.0008) differ by a **small but consistent gap**, not a near-tie: paired B−C 0.0018±0.0017, |m| just above sd, same sign on all 5 seeds. Ten times more steps buy only that much COR here because contact still starts and ends only on a step boundary.
- **gentle_rest:** penetration error A 1.539±0.277 µm, B 0.093±0.079 µm, C 0.004±0.003 µm on a 39.24 µm static sag, and every arm has 0 bounces (identical counts: that part is a tie). The penetration gaps are **all small relative to the ~39 µm static sag, but the gaps are consistent**, not a near-tie: paired A−B 1.446±0.261 µm and B−C 0.088±0.077 µm, both |m| > sd with the same sign on all 5 seeds (5/5). The static equilibrium `m·g/k` is the same for every step; the remaining gap is how a coarser step filters the shared disturbance, so it shrinks with the step but does not vanish.

The page **does not declare a universal winner**. A smaller step costs compute — C runs 10× the steps of B, which is why the table below says not to shrink `max_step_size` to fix RTF — while a step past the criterion diverges however cheap it is. For a real world: find the stiffest contact or joint, keep `ωΔt` well inside the limit of the engine's integrator, then confirm by halving the step and checking that penetration and bounce stop changing. ## When things go wrong

Most first-week failures are the same short list. Reach for this table before rewriting the robot.

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TF "extrapolation into the future", model flickers in RViz | mixed clocks | every node has `use_sim_time: true`; `/clock` is bridged |
| `/scan` missing in `ros2 topic list` | bridge or launch | `bridge.yaml` entry; launch did start `ros_gz_bridge` |
| `/scan` exists but no messages arrive (`ros2 topic hz` prints nothing) | Sensors plugin or headless render | world has `gz-sim-sensors-system`; try `--headless-rendering` |
| Laser points in the wrong place | `frame_id` ≠ URDF link | `gz_frame_id` is `lidar_link` (or your real link name) |
| Robot veers or spins in place | friction / caster | wheel `mu` high, caster `mu` tiny; wheel/caster collisions neither sink into nor float above the floor |
| Robot explodes, jitters or sinks at start | bad inertias | Lesson 3 `inertia_check.py`; mass on every physical link; no overlapping collisions at spawn (DART ignores `<contact>` `kp`/`kd`) |
| RTF well below 1, sensors slow in wall time | rendering / step cost | `gz topic -e -t /world/obstacles/stats -n 1` → `real_time_factor`; lower camera resolution or `update_rate`; do not shrink `max_step_size` |
| RViz empty (no model, no points) | Fixed Frame | set Fixed Frame to `odom` or `base_footprint`, not `map` |
| Tutorial commands fail on Jazzy | Gazebo Classic / Ignition-era docs | `ign gazebo`, `libgazebo_ros_*.so`, `gazebo_ros_pkgs` → close the tab |
| World waits forever / missing meshes | Fuel download | keep models inline like this lesson; do not depend on the model server |

## What transfers to a real robot

**Transfers.** The URDF/xacro tree and TF frame names; the idea of an explicit bridge (or driver) from sensor hardware into ROS topics; launch files that compose description + drivers + viz; the habit of checking a range with a tape measure the way you checked 1.80 − 0.12 = 1.68 m; DiffDrive-as-concept becoming `ros2_control` + a hardware interface in Lesson 5.

**Does not transfer as-is.** Perfect wheel odometry (no slip, no bias); the exact friction numbers that made the sim stable; GPU lidar noise that is only a Gaussian knob; any reliance on `/clock` and `use_sim_time: true`. On the real machine no simulator publishes `/clock`; wall time is the clock, and SLAM (Lesson 6) exists because odometry drifts while the lidar still sees the room.

Simulation is a test bench you can reset. It is not a certificate that the physical robot will track 0.95 m when you ask for four seconds at 0.2 m/s. Keep the topics and frames; recalibrate the numbers that touch the ground.

Now the practice task, with your own robot. Put the xacro robot from Lesson 3 into your own SDF world with obstacles, add a lidar and a camera, and confirm both appear on ROS 2 topics and in RViz. Work through this checklist:

1. The world loads offline, with no Fuel download and no missing model error.
2. The robot spawns and sits still. If it jumps, twitches or sinks, check the inertias with Lesson 3's `inertia_check.py` before changing anything else.
3. `ros2 topic list` shows `/scan` and `/camera/image_raw`.
4. The `frame_id` values are link names that exist in your URDF, not invented names.
5. The scan's straight-ahead range matches a hand calculation from your world file.
6. RViz shows the laser points and the camera image, and the robot model lines up with them.

The folder `examples/month-4/gazebo-sim` contains the package and both scripts. The editor's runs can be repeated with `tools/sim/ros/run.sh`.
