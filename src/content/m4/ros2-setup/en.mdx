Month 4 is where a robot stops being a single program and becomes a system. By the end of it you will have built a robot in ROS 2, simulated it, and taught it to map a room and drive to a goal on its own.

Two facts shape everything that follows. The first is that ROS 2 is how industry builds robots, and it is the skill that appears most often in robotics job listings; learning it is not academic preparation, it is the thing employers ask for. The second is that this whole month needs no hardware at all. A normal laptop is enough — no NVIDIA GPU, no robot arm, no lidar. The belief that robotics requires expensive equipment is the main reason people never start; for the next six lessons, the expensive equipment is the computer you already own.

Months 1–3 were your way. You wrote one program, it ran one loop on a microcontroller, and the board did exactly what that loop said. ROS 2 has a different shape. It runs on a computer — your laptop now, a Raspberry Pi or a small x86 machine on a real robot later — and its job is to connect many programs that each do one thing. One program reads the lidar, another turns readings into a map, a third decides where to drive, a fourth talks to the motors. They exchange messages over named channels instead of calling each other directly, which is why you can replace one of them without rewriting the rest.

The month builds in order. Lesson 2, "Nodes, topics, services and actions", gives you the vocabulary. Lesson 3, "URDF and TF: describing a robot", gives the robot a body. Lesson 4, "Simulation with Gazebo", puts that body in a world. Lesson 5, "ros2_control: drive through a controller", makes the wheels turn. Lesson 6, "SLAM and Nav2: map and navigate", closes the loop with a map and a destination. This lesson is the floor: choose a version, install it, and get one node of your own running.

## Pick a distro

ROS 2 ships a new release every May. Each release is called a distribution, or distro, and the names run alphabetically through turtles: Humble, Iron, Jazzy, Kilted, Lyrical. The alphabetical order is not decoration — it tells you which release is newer, and the first letter is often the fastest way to place a tutorial in time.

The release cadence has a pattern worth memorising. Even years are LTS (long-term support) releases and get five years of support. Odd years are ordinary releases supported for about 18 months. That single rule explains almost every version question you will have for the next few years.

| Distro | Released | End of life | Ubuntu | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lyrical Luth | May 2026 | May 2031 | 26.04 | The newest LTS; what you would pick for a brand-new production project |
| Kilted Kaiju | May 2025 | December 2026 | 24.04 | Non-LTS, support ends in about two months: do not start here |
| Jazzy Jalisco | May 2024 | May 2029 | 24.04 | LTS: start here |
| Humble Hawksbill | May 2022 | May 2027 | 22.04 | Legacy; you will meet it on older robots and in older courses |

Read the table with a calendar, not with a vague sense of "newer is better". Kilted was released in May 2025 and dies in December 2026, which is 19 months — the "about 18 months" figure in practice. Jazzy was released in May 2024 and gets until May 2029, exactly five years. The end-of-life date is not the day the code stops running; it is the day fixes, security updates and new binary packages stop; from then on you are on your own when something breaks, and new tutorials and packages move to newer distros.

Here is the calculation you will repeat for any distro. Say it is October 2026 and you want to know how much support Lyrical has left:

```text
October 2026 → October 2030     4 years
October 2030 → May 2031         7 months
7 / 12                          ≈ 0.58 year
4 + 0.58                        ≈ 4.6 years
```

The same sum for Jazzy gives the answer to the quiz below.

So why start on Jazzy rather than the newest LTS? Because almost every course, book and video series still targets Jazzy. It has more than two and a half years of support left, which is plenty of runway to learn on. And when a tutorial and your install are the same distro, you fight the topic instead of version differences: the package names match, the launch syntax matches, and error messages you find online were written about the code you are running. Move to Lyrical when the tutorial stack you rely on catches up; the concepts transfer almost unchanged, so that move will be a week of adjustment, not a restart.

One more piece of vocabulary from the releases page: each distro is built against one Ubuntu LTS as its Tier 1 platform. Jazzy and Kilted target Ubuntu 24.04; Humble targets 22.04; Lyrical targets 26.04. Other operating systems are supported at lower tiers, which means fewer prebuilt binaries and fewer tested combinations. That is why the install instructions care so much about the exact Ubuntu version, and why the alternatives in this lesson exist at all.

Practical habit: write your distro at the top of your notes, and read it in the first command of every tutorial before spending time on the rest.

## Spot ROS 1

ROS 1 is finished. Its last release, Noetic, reached end of life on 31 May 2025, and there is no successor: the project moved to ROS 2, which is a different set of libraries with different commands, not a version bump. A huge amount of highly ranked tutorial content online is still ROS 1, so the useful skill is to recognise it in seconds and close the tab.

| If you see… | It is ROS 1 because… | The ROS 2 way |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `catkin_make` or `catkin build` | the ROS 1 build tool | `colcon build` |
| `roscore` | ROS 1 needs a central master | nothing: ROS 2 nodes find each other automatically (DDS discovery), no master |
| `rosrun pkg node` | ROS 1 runner | `ros2 run pkg node` |
| `import rospy` | ROS 1 Python library | `import rclpy` |
| `roslaunch` with a `.launch` XML file | ROS 1 launch | `ros2 launch` with a Python `.launch.py` file (ROS 2 can also read XML and YAML launch files, but the documentation and this site use Python) |

The pattern behind the table is that ROS 1 was built around a central master process. Every node registered with it, and `roscore` had to be running before anything else worked. ROS 2 removed the master: nodes discover each other over DDS, so starting order stops mattering and there is no single process to restart when the graph misbehaves. The Python library changed name from `rospy` to `rclpy`, and the build system changed from catkin to ament with colcon on top.

Paths give it away too. If the tutorial says `/opt/ros/noetic`, `melodic` or `kinetic`, those are ROS 1 installations; ROS 2 lives in `/opt/ros/jazzy`, `/opt/ros/humble` and so on. You will also see `source /opt/ros/noetic/setup.bash` in old setup instructions — the shape is familiar, but everything it loads is ROS 1.

Practical tip: before reading any tutorial, look at the publication date and at the first command. A page from 2019 about "ROS" is almost certainly ROS 1 unless it explicitly says otherwise. Be careful with old Gazebo commands as well, because the Gazebo integration was renamed and restructured between generations; Lesson 4 covers which commands belong to which. Do not throw away old material entirely — the concepts of topics, services and coordinate frames carry over — but do not copy its commands into a Jazzy terminal and then wonder why nothing exists.

## Install it

There are four sane routes. They all end in the same place — a terminal where `ros2` works and the talker and listener can find each other — so pick the one that matches your operating system and your patience, not the one that sounds most professional.

### Ubuntu 24.04

This is the reference setup: Ubuntu 24.04, native or dual-boot, with the official apt packages. Most books, university courses and video series assume exactly this, so problems you hit are problems someone else has already solved.

The steps below are from the official Jazzy install page, checked in October 2026.

```bash
sudo apt install software-properties-common
sudo add-apt-repository universe
sudo apt update && sudo apt install curl -y
export ROS_APT_SOURCE_VERSION=$(curl -s https://api.github.com/repos/ros-infrastructure/ros-apt-source/releases/latest | grep -F "tag_name" | awk -F'"' '{print $4}')
curl -L -o /tmp/ros2-apt-source.deb "https://github.com/ros-infrastructure/ros-apt-source/releases/download/${ROS_APT_SOURCE_VERSION}/ros2-apt-source_${ROS_APT_SOURCE_VERSION}.$(. /etc/os-release && echo ${UBUNTU_CODENAME:-${VERSION_CODENAME}})_all.deb"
sudo dpkg -i /tmp/ros2-apt-source.deb
sudo apt update && sudo apt install ros-dev-tools
sudo apt install ros-jazzy-desktop
```

The first lines enable Ubuntu's universe repository and install the package that adds the ROS 2 apt repository and its signing key. The `ROS_APT_SOURCE_VERSION` line asks GitHub for the newest release tag of the apt-source package, and the `curl` line downloads the `.deb` built for your Ubuntu codename — on 24.04 that is `noble`. Installing it tells apt where the ROS 2 packages live; without it, `apt install ros-jazzy-desktop` would simply find nothing.

`ros-dev-tools` brings colcon and the other build tools you need from Lesson 2 onward. `ros-jazzy-desktop` is ROS 2 plus RViz, the demos and the tutorials. There is also `ros-jazzy-ros-base`, the bare-bones version without GUI tools, which is what you would put on a robot with no monitor. For learning, take the desktop package.

These commands change over time — repository URLs, package names and supported Ubuntu versions all move. If a step fails, compare it with the official page rather than with a blog post from 2024. After installation, every terminal needs the environment loaded, which is the first thing in the next section.

## First nodes

### Source, then test

ROS 2 finds its programs through environment variables: where the executables are, where the Python modules are, where the package index lives. A freshly opened terminal knows none of that, so every new terminal needs the environment loaded:

```bash
source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash
```

Many people add that line to `~/.bashrc` so it happens automatically. That is convenient, but be aware that it then applies to every terminal you open, including ones where you meant to test another distro. If you later install a second ROS 2 version, an old line in `~/.bashrc` becomes a source of confusing errors; keep it in mind when things behave strangely.

The official installation check uses two demo programs: a C++ talker and a Python listener. Run them in two terminals.

```bash
# terminal 1
ros2 run demo_nodes_cpp talker
# terminal 2
ros2 run demo_nodes_py listener
```

```text

[INFO] [1792442649.473299676] [listener]: I heard: [Hello World: 2]

Topics deliver what is published while you are listening; there is no history waiting for you unless a publisher is configured to keep one. Lesson 2 goes into that in detail.

### Look at the graph

A running ROS 2 system is a graph: nodes are programs, topics are named streams of messages, and the edges are publishers and subscribers. The `ros2` command can inspect this graph from any terminal, including one that is not running any node.

```bash
ros2 node list                  # every node currently running
ros2 topic list                 # every topic currently alive
ros2 topic echo /hello --once   # print one message, then exit
ros2 topic hz /hello            # measure the publish rate (Ctrl+C to stop)
```

The output below comes from this lesson's own node, which you build in the next section — not from the demo talker.

```text
$ ros2 node list
/hello_node
$ ros2 topic list
/hello
/parameter_events
/rosout
$ ros2 topic echo /hello --once
data: 'hello #7'
---
$ ros2 topic hz /hello
average rate: 2.000
```

One node is running: ours. `/hello` is the topic we created; `/parameter_events` and `/rosout` exist in every ROS 2 system, carrying parameter changes and log messages respectively, which is why they appear even though you never wrote them. `echo --once` printed one message and exited, and the `---` separates messages when more than one is printed. `hz` measured two messages per second, which matches the 0.5 s timer in the code. It keeps printing until you press Ctrl+C; only its first line is shown here.

Two details worth noticing. The node name begins with a slash because it is in the root namespace; topic names work the same way. And `hz` is a measurement, not a setting: it counts arrivals over a window, so the first line can be slightly off if you start it mid-stream. A steady 2.000 on a 0.5 s timer is exactly what correct looks like.

### The node

<CodeSource path="examples/month-4/ros2-setup/m4_hello/m4_hello/hello_node.py" />

```python
"""A first ROS 2 node: publish a counter on /hello twice a second."""
import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from std_msgs.msg import String


class HelloNode(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('hello_node')            # the name shown by `ros2 node list`
        self.pub = self.create_publisher(String, 'hello', 10)   # topic, queue depth
        self.count = 0
        self.timer = self.create_timer(0.5, self.tick)          # call tick() every 0.5 s

    def tick(self):
        msg = String()
        msg.data = f'hello #{self.count}'
        self.pub.publish(msg)
        self.get_logger().info(f'published "{msg.data}"')
        self.count += 1


def main():
    rclpy.init()
    node = HelloNode()
    try:
        rclpy.spin(node)                          # run callbacks until Ctrl+C
    except KeyboardInterrupt:
        pass
    finally:
        node.destroy_node()
        rclpy.try_shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

A node is a class that inherits from `Node`. The string passed to `super().__init__` is the name you see in `ros2 node list`; change it and the graph changes.

`create_publisher(String, 'hello', 10)` takes the message type, the topic name and the queue depth. The queue depth is how many messages may wait if a subscriber is slower than the publisher; ten is the usual starting value and has nothing to do with frequency. The topic name has no leading slash, which makes it relative: in the default namespace it resolves to `/hello`.

`create_timer(0.5, self.tick)` asks ROS to call `tick` every 0.5 seconds. Inside `tick` you build a `String` message, fill `.data`, publish it, log a line and increment the counter.

`rclpy.spin(node)` runs callbacks until Ctrl+C. This is the ROS 2 equivalent of Month 2's non-blocking `millis()` loop, except that the timer and the message delivery are handled for you: you never write the loop, you write functions that the executor calls. The `finally` block cleans up the node and shuts the library down even when you interrupt it.

The entry point that turns this file into the command `hello_node` lives in `setup.py`:

<CodeSource path="examples/month-4/ros2-setup/m4_hello/setup.py" />

```python
from setuptools import find_packages, setup

package_name = 'm4_hello'

setup(
    name=package_name,
    version='0.0.0',
    packages=find_packages(exclude=['test']),
    data_files=[
        ('share/ament_index/resource_index/packages',
            ['resource/' + package_name]),
        ('share/' + package_name, ['package.xml']),
    ],
    install_requires=['setuptools'],
    zip_safe=True,
    maintainer='Your Name',
    maintainer_email='you@example.com',
    description='Month 4 lesson 1: a first ROS 2 node',
    license='TODO: License declaration',
    entry_points={
        'console_scripts': [
            'hello_node = m4_hello.hello_node:main'
        ],
    },
)
```

The important line is `'hello_node = m4_hello.hello_node:main'` in `entry_points`. The left side is the command name; the right side is the module path and the function to call, separated by a colon. That is why `ros2 run m4_hello hello_node` works: ROS finds the package, then the console script, then the `main` function.

Now the two words that explain most workspace confusion. `/opt/ros/jazzy` is the underlay: the installed ROS 2, which you sourced earlier. Your workspace's `install/setup.bash` is the overlay: it adds your packages on top of the underlay without modifying it. Source the underlay first, then the overlay, because the overlay's script extends what is already loaded. In a new terminal you need both, in that order.

`colcon build --symlink-install` links Python files instead of copying them, so edits take effect without rebuilding. Use it once you are editing often. New files and changes to `setup.py` still need a rebuild, because those change what gets installed, not just the contents of an installed file.

## When it breaks

Nearly every setup failure in the first week is one of four mistakes.

- **Not sourced.** A new terminal does not know your workspace. Real output of `ros2 run m4_hello hello_node` in a terminal where only ROS itself is sourced:

  ```text
  Package 'm4_hello' not found
  ```

  Fix: `source ~/ros2_ws/install/setup.bash` (after the ROS underlay). The package is not missing; the terminal simply has not been told where to look.

- **Building in the wrong folder.** Run `colcon build` from the workspace root, never inside `src/`. If you build inside `src/`, you get `build/` and `install/` folders inside your source tree and a confusing second workspace that competes with the first. If that happens, delete the stray folders and rebuild from the root.

- **Mixing distros.** Sourcing Humble and Jazzy in the same terminal — often from an old `~/.bashrc` line — gives errors that look like broken packages, missing symbols or Python import failures. Check with `printenv ROS_DISTRO` and make sure it says `jazzy`. Then look at what your shell startup files source.

- **Nodes that cannot see each other, or see too much.** ROS 2 discovers every node on the same network with the same `ROS_DOMAIN_ID`, which defaults to 0. On a shared network (university, office), you will see strangers' nodes and they will see yours. Set your own domain, for example `export ROS_DOMAIN_ID=42` (any number from 0 to 101 is safe), or stay on your computer with `export ROS_AUTOMATIC_DISCOVERY_RANGE=LOCALHOST`. Two terminals with different domain IDs will never hear each other, so when a topic "publishes but nothing receives", check this first. Lesson 2 covers the other cause, QoS.

One node publishing a counter is the start. Lesson 2 turns that into a whole graph, with services, actions, parameters, launch files and bags.
