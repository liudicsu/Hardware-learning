## Links, joints and frames

A robot description is a tree. That one sentence is the whole idea of this lesson, and it is worth unpacking slowly before a line of XML appears.

The nodes of the tree are **links**. A link is a rigid part: a piece of the robot whose shape never changes, such as the chassis plate, a wheel or a camera body. Every link owns its own coordinate frame — its own little origin with an x, a y and a z axis — and everything you later say about that part is written in that frame.

The branches are **joints**. A joint connects exactly one parent link to exactly one child link, and it does two jobs at once. First it says where the child sits relative to the parent: an `origin` with a translation `xyz` in metres and an orientation `rpy` (roll, pitch, yaw) in radians. Second it says how the child is allowed to move: the joint type. Because a link can be the child of only one joint, links and joints always form a tree, with exactly one path between any two parts.

**<Term id="urdf">URDF</Term>** (Unified Robot Description Format) is the XML file in which you write that tree down. It is a description and nothing more: there is no code and no behaviour in it, just a static statement of what the parts are and how they are joined. **TF** is the system that, while the robot runs, publishes where every frame sits relative to its parent, each answer stamped with a time, so that any node can ask "where is the camera relative to the base right now?" and get an answer it can trust. The `frame_id` that every message carried in Lesson 2 — the one that pointed at nothing — is simply the name of a frame in this system. From here on it can point at something real.

Two conventions fix these choices so that every package agrees on them. **ROS REP 103** fixes the units and the axis directions: metres, kilograms and radians (not centimetres, not grams, not degrees), with x forward, y left and z up, in a right-handed set. **ROS REP 105** fixes the frames for a mobile platform: `base_link` is attached to the robot body, and `base_footprint` is the point on the floor directly below it. The split matters because a robot pitches and rolls as it drives over a bump; navigation wants a frame that stays flat on the ground, and `base_footprint` is that frame.

A joint is defined by its type. As a beginner you will meet only four:

| Type | Moves | Example in m4bot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| fixed | not at all | lidar, mast, camera |
| continuous | rotates without limits | the two wheels |
| revolute | rotates between limits | pan and tilt |
| prismatic | slides along an axis | (none here; a lift or linear stage) |

Distinguish `continuous` from `revolute` carefully: both rotate about a single axis, but a continuous joint spins forever (a driven wheel), while a revolute joint stops at a lower and an upper limit (a servo arm). A prismatic joint is the sliding version, like a lift or a linear stage; m4bot has none, but you will meet them the first time you build a mechanism that goes up and down.

**m4bot** is arranged like this. The chassis is a box 0.30 m long, 0.24 m wide and 0.08 m tall. Two wheels of radius 5 cm sit on either side of the axle, and a caster ball at the front supplies the third point of contact. A lidar puck sits on the front of the deck, and a 20 cm mast at the back carries a pan-tilt camera head. Drawn by the `tf2_tools` tool `view_frames`, its tree looks like this:

![The m4bot TF tree drawn by tf2_tools view_frames: base_footprint at the top, then base_link with five children (left_wheel_link, mast_link, right_wheel_link, caster_link, lidar_link), and the chain mast_link, pan_link, tilt_link, camera_link, camera_optical_link](/m4/tf_tree.png)

Two kinds of edge appear in that drawing. The edges published at about 10 Hz are the **moving joints** — the wheels, the pan and the tilt — because their positions change and something has to republish them continuously. ## The m4bot in xacro

Plain URDF makes you repeat yourself. Two identical wheels want two nearly identical blocks of XML; every visual, collision and inertial repeats the same origin three times; and every number is hard-coded where it is used, so changing the chassis length means hunting down four separate places. **xacro** is a small macro language that writes the URDF for you and removes all of that repetition. Four features carry almost everything you will ever need: `xacro:property` for named dimensions, `${...}` to do arithmetic on them, `xacro:macro` for a part you want more than once, and `xacro:include` to split one big robot across several files.

Here is the whole of m4bot, top to bottom.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-4/urdf-tf/m4_description/urdf/robot.urdf.xacro" />

```xml

     All lengths in metres, masses in kilograms, angles in radians. -->
<robot name="m4bot" xmlns:xacro="http://www.ros.org/wiki/xacro">

  <xacro:include filename="inertial_macros.xacro"/>

  <!-- ===== dimensions: change these, everything else follows ===== -->
  <xacro:property name="chassis_x" value="0.30"/>
  <xacro:property name="chassis_y" value="0.24"/>
  <xacro:property name="chassis_z" value="0.08"/>
  <xacro:property name="chassis_mass" value="2.0"/>
  <xacro:property name="chassis_offset_x" value="0.05"/>   <!-- chassis centre ahead of the axle -->
  <xacro:property name="wheel_radius" value="0.05"/>
  <xacro:property name="wheel_width" value="0.03"/>
  <xacro:property name="wheel_mass" value="0.2"/>

  <xacro:property name="mast_height" value="0.20"/>
  <xacro:property name="chassis_top" value="${chassis_z - 0.01}"/>  <!-- top face, above base_link -->

  <link name="base_footprint"/>
  <joint name="base_joint" type="fixed">
    <parent link="base_footprint"/>
    <child link="base_link"/>
    <origin xyz="0 0 ${wheel_radius}"/>
  </joint>

  <link name="base_link">
    <visual>
      <origin xyz="${chassis_offset_x} 0 ${chassis_z/2 - 0.01}"/>
      <geometry><box size="${chassis_x} ${chassis_y} ${chassis_z}"/></geometry>
      <material name="orange"><color rgba="1.0 0.45 0.1 1"/></material>
    </visual>
    <collision>
      <origin xyz="${chassis_offset_x} 0 ${chassis_z/2 - 0.01}"/>
      <geometry><box size="${chassis_x} ${chassis_y} ${chassis_z}"/></geometry>
    </collision>
    <xacro:inertial_box mass="${chassis_mass}" x="${chassis_x}" y="${chassis_y}" z="${chassis_z}">
      <origin xyz="${chassis_offset_x} 0 ${chassis_z/2 - 0.01}"/>
    </xacro:inertial_box>
  </link>

  <!-- one macro, two wheels: reflect = +1 for left (+y), -1 for right -->
  <xacro:macro name="wheel" params="side reflect">
    <link name="${side}_wheel_link">
      <visual>
        <origin rpy="${-pi/2} 0 0"/>
        <geometry><cylinder radius="${wheel_radius}" length="${wheel_width}"/></geometry>
        <material name="black"><color rgba="0.1 0.1 0.1 1"/></material>
      </visual>

        <geometry><cylinder radius="${wheel_radius}" length="${wheel_width}"/></geometry>
      </collision>

      </xacro:inertial_cylinder>
    </link>
    <joint name="${side}_wheel_joint" type="continuous">
      <parent link="base_link"/>
      <child link="${side}_wheel_link"/>
      <origin xyz="0 ${reflect * wheel_separation/2} 0"/>
      <axis xyz="0 1 0"/>   <!-- positive rotation drives the robot forward on both sides -->
    </joint>
  </xacro:macro>
  <xacro:wheel side="left" reflect="1"/>
  <xacro:wheel side="right" reflect="-1"/>

  <!-- caster: a sphere touching the floor; visual and collision are the same ball -->
  <link name="caster_link">
    <visual>
      <geometry><sphere radius="${caster_radius}"/></geometry>
      <material name="grey"><color rgba="0.6 0.6 0.6 1"/></material>
    </visual>
    <collision>
      <geometry><sphere radius="${caster_radius}"/></geometry>
    </collision>
    <xacro:inertial_sphere mass="0.05" radius="${caster_radius}">
      <origin xyz="0 0 0"/>
    </xacro:inertial_sphere>
  </link>
  <joint name="caster_joint" type="fixed">
    <parent link="base_link"/>
    <child link="caster_link"/>
    <origin xyz="0.16 0 ${caster_radius - wheel_radius}"/>
  </joint>

  <!-- lidar: a fixed (static) transform on the front of the chassis.
       Its link name is the frame_id the lidar driver must use. -->
  <link name="lidar_link">
    <visual>
      <geometry><cylinder radius="0.035" length="0.04"/></geometry>
      <material name="black"/>
    </visual>
    <collision>
      <geometry><cylinder radius="0.035" length="0.04"/></geometry>
    </collision>
    <xacro:inertial_cylinder mass="0.1" radius="0.035" length="0.04">
      <origin xyz="0 0 0"/>
    </xacro:inertial_cylinder>
  </link>
  <joint name="lidar_joint" type="fixed">
    <parent link="base_link"/>
    <child link="lidar_link"/>
    <origin xyz="0.12 0 ${chassis_top + 0.02}"/>
  </joint>

  <!-- mast at the back of the chassis -->
  <link name="mast_link">
    <visual>
      <origin xyz="0 0 ${mast_height/2}"/>
      <geometry><cylinder radius="0.015" length="${mast_height}"/></geometry>
      <material name="grey"/>
    </visual>
    <collision>
      <origin xyz="0 0 ${mast_height/2}"/>
      <geometry><cylinder radius="0.015" length="${mast_height}"/></geometry>
    </collision>
    <xacro:inertial_cylinder mass="0.1" radius="0.015" length="${mast_height}">
      <origin xyz="0 0 ${mast_height/2}"/>
    </xacro:inertial_cylinder>
  </link>
  <joint name="mast_joint" type="fixed">
    <parent link="base_link"/>
    <child link="mast_link"/>
    <origin xyz="-0.06 0 ${chassis_top}"/>
  </joint>

  <link name="pan_link">
    <visual>
      <origin xyz="0 0 0.01"/>
      <geometry><box size="0.04 0.04 0.02"/></geometry>
      <material name="grey"/>
    </visual>
    <collision>
      <origin xyz="0 0 0.01"/>
      <geometry><box size="0.04 0.04 0.02"/></geometry>

  </link>

    <child link="pan_link"/>
    <origin xyz="0 0 ${mast_height}"/>
    <axis xyz="0 0 1"/>

  </joint>

  <!-- the camera body: a detailed shape for looks, a plain box for collisions -->
  <link name="tilt_link">
    <visual>
      <geometry><box size="0.03 0.08 0.03"/></geometry>
      <material name="black"/>
    </visual>
    <visual>
      <origin xyz="0.017 0 0" rpy="0 ${pi/2} 0"/>
      <geometry><cylinder radius="0.008" length="0.006"/></geometry>   <!-- lens -->
      <material name="grey"/>
    </visual>
    <collision>
      <geometry><box size="0.04 0.08 0.03"/></geometry>
    </collision>
    <xacro:inertial_box mass="0.05" x="0.03" y="0.08" z="0.03">
      <origin xyz="0 0 0"/>
    </xacro:inertial_box>

    <child link="tilt_link"/>
    <origin xyz="0 0 0.035"/>
    <axis xyz="0 1 0"/>

</robot>
```

Now walk through it, because every one of these choices will matter later.

1. **The properties block.** Every dimension has a name and a value, and the values are written once. Change `chassis_x` from 0.30 to 0.35 and the visual box, the collision box and the inertial all follow, because they all read `${chassis_x}`. `${chassis_z/2 - 0.01}` even does arithmetic for you.
2. **`base_footprint` to `base_link`.** A fixed joint lifts `base_link` by `wheel_radius`, so `base_link` sits on the axle and `base_footprint` stays on the floor. The chassis box is then pushed forwards by `chassis_offset_x`, because the axle is not in the middle of the body.
3. **One wheel macro, called twice.** The `wheel` macro takes `side` and `reflect`; the left wheel is called with `reflect="1"` and the right with `reflect="-1"`, so one piece of text draws both wheels at opposite sides. The wheel cylinder is turned by `rpy="${-pi/2} 0 0"` because a URDF cylinder stands along its own z axis and a wheel must lie on its side; the joint axis is `0 1 0`, so a positive rotation drives the robot forward on both sides.
4. **The caster touches the floor.** Its origin is at z = `caster_radius − wheel_radius` = 0.025 − 0.05 = −0.025, so the little ball hangs exactly to ground level under the axle-centre frame.
5. **The lidar is fixed, and its name is a promise.** `lidar_link` sits on a static transform in front of the deck. The name of the link is the `frame_id` that the lidar driver must stamp into its scans; nothing enforces this, so it is on you to get it right (Section 6 shows what happens when you do not).
6. **Pan about z, tilt about y, both revolute.** The pan joint rotates about z with limits of ±π/2, the tilt joint about y with limits of ±0.8 rad. Both are `revolute`, and the limits written here are the ones the sliders, and later the simulator and the controllers, keep each joint inside.
7. **Separate visual and collision.** `tilt_link` has two visuals — the camera body and a small lens cylinder — for looks, but a single plain box for collisions. A physics engine checks collisions thousands of times a second, so simple shapes are cheap and pretty shapes are not; keep the detail in the visual and the simplicity in the collision.
8. **`camera_link` versus `camera_optical_link`.** `camera_link` follows the ROS body convention, x forward. The optical frame is rotated by `rpy="${-pi/2} 0 ${-pi/2}"`, which turns z forward, x right and y down — the frame that image drivers and OpenCV expect. URDF applies roll, then pitch, then yaw about the fixed axes, so R = Rz(−90°) · Ry(0) · Rx(−90°). Applied to the optical axes: R · (0, 0, 1) = (1, 0, 0), so optical z is body x (forward); R · (1, 0, 0) = (0, −1, 0), so optical x is body −y (right); R · (0, 1, 0) = (0, 0, −1), so optical y is body −z (down). Image tooling will look for the optical frame, so it is worth adding even though it is only a fixed rotation.

## Inertia without guessing

RViz does not care about mass. Gazebo cares enormously. Every link that has mass needs an `<inertial>` element carrying a mass and a 3×3 inertia matrix; without one, or with nonsense values in it, the robot explodes, jitters, or sinks through the floor the moment physics starts — the same symptoms Lesson 4 lists under Gazebo failure modes once the robot is spawned. The frame-only links — `base_footprint`, `camera_link` and `camera_optical_link` — have no inertial on purpose, because they represent points and orientations, not matter.

You do not need CAD to get this right. Approximate each part as a uniform solid and use the standard formulas, which we wrap in three macros so they can never be mistyped twice.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-4/urdf-tf/m4_description/urdf/inertial_macros.xacro" />

```xml
<?xml version="1.0"?>
<!-- Inertia of simple solids (uniform density), about the centre of mass.
     Each macro takes the same pose as the geometry it belongs to. -->
<robot xmlns:xacro="http://www.ros.org/wiki/xacro">

  <xacro:macro name="inertial_box" params="mass x y z *origin">
    <inertial>
      <xacro:insert_block name="origin"/>
      <mass value="${mass}"/>
      <inertia ixx="${mass / 12 * (y*y + z*z)}" ixy="0" ixz="0"
               iyy="${mass / 12 * (x*x + z*z)}" iyz="0"
               izz="${mass / 12 * (x*x + y*y)}"/>
    </inertial>
  </xacro:macro>

  <!-- cylinder along its local z axis -->
  <xacro:macro name="inertial_cylinder" params="mass radius length *origin">
    <inertial>
      <xacro:insert_block name="origin"/>
      <mass value="${mass}"/>
      <inertia ixx="${mass / 12 * (3*radius*radius + length*length)}" ixy="0" ixz="0"
               iyy="${mass / 12 * (3*radius*radius + length*length)}" iyz="0"
               izz="${mass / 2 * radius*radius}"/>
    </inertial>
  </xacro:macro>

  <xacro:macro name="inertial_sphere" params="mass radius *origin">
    <inertial>
      <xacro:insert_block name="origin"/>
      <mass value="${mass}"/>
      <inertia ixx="${2 / 5 * mass * radius*radius}" ixy="0" ixz="0"
               iyy="${2 / 5 * mass * radius*radius}" iyz="0"
               izz="${2 / 5 * mass * radius*radius}"/>
    </inertial>
  </xacro:macro>

</robot>
```

The formulas in plain text, all taken about the centre of mass:

```text
box:        Ixx = m/12·(y²+z²),  Iyy = m/12·(x²+z²),  Izz = m/12·(x²+y²)
cylinder:   Ixx = Iyy = m/12·(3r²+h²),  Izz = m/2·r²      (about its own z axis)
sphere:     I = 2/5·m·r²
```

**Worked example 1: the chassis.** The box is m = 2.0 kg and measures 0.30 × 0.24 × 0.08 m. Every step:

```text
Ixx = 2.0/12 × (0.24² + 0.08²) = 0.16667 × (0.0576 + 0.0064)
    = 0.16667 × 0.0640 = 0.010667 kg·m²
Iyy = 2.0/12 × (0.30² + 0.08²) = 0.16667 × (0.0900 + 0.0064)
    = 0.16667 × 0.0964 = 0.016067 kg·m²
Izz = 2.0/12 × (0.30² + 0.24²) = 0.16667 × (0.0900 + 0.0576)
    = 0.16667 × 0.1476 = 0.024600 kg·m²
```

Notice which dimension is missing from each moment: Ixx measures rotation about the x axis, so it uses the two sizes perpendicular to x (y and z), never x itself.

**Worked example 2: a wheel.** m = 0.2 kg, r = 0.05 m, h = 0.03 m. A wheel is a cylinder about its own axis:

```text
Izz = 0.2/2 × 0.05² = 0.1 × 0.0025 = 0.00025 kg·m²
Ixx = Iyy = 0.2/12 × (3 × 0.05² + 0.03²)
    = 0.016667 × (3 × 0.0025 + 0.0009)
    = 0.016667 × (0.0075 + 0.0009) = 0.016667 × 0.0084 = 0.00014 kg·m²
```

The macro takes the same origin as the geometry it belongs to: the chassis's inertial is shifted forwards with its box, and the wheel's inertial is turned with its cylinder. If you forget that, the inertia is computed about the wrong point and the simulation feels subtly wrong even though no error appears.

There is one plausibility rule that any real object obeys, and it catches most typos: the **triangle rule**. Each moment of inertia is at most the sum of the other two — Ixx + Iyy ≥ Izz, and so on. No solid can be harder to spin about one axis than about the other two combined, so a single mistyped digit usually breaks the inequality immediately.

Rather than trust your arithmetic, let a script check it. This one reads a URDF and flags every link a physics engine could not simulate:

<CodeSource path="examples/month-4/urdf-tf/inertia_check.py" />

```python
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Check the <inertial> of every link in a URDF before Gazebo does it the hard way.

usage: xacro robot.urdf.xacro > robot.urdf && python3 inertia_check.py robot.urdf

Flags links that a physics engine cannot simulate: no inertial at all (fine only for
massless frames such as base_footprint or camera_link), a mass of zero or less, a
non-positive moment of inertia, or moments that break the triangle rule
(Ixx + Iyy >= Izz and so on), which no real object can have."""
import sys
import xml.etree.ElementTree as ET


def main(path):
    robot = ET.parse(path).getroot()
    total, problems = 0.0, 0
    print(f'{"link":20} {"mass kg":>8} {"Ixx":>10} {"Iyy":>10} {"Izz":>10}  check')
    for link in robot.findall('link'):
        name = link.get('name')
        inertial = link.find('inertial')
        if inertial is None:
            print(f'{name:20} {"-":>8} {"":>10} {"":>10} {"":>10}  no inertial (frame only)')
            continue
        m = float(inertial.find('mass').get('value'))
        i = inertial.find('inertia')
        ixx, iyy, izz = (float(i.get(k)) for k in ('ixx', 'iyy', 'izz'))
        ok = m > 0 and min(ixx, iyy, izz) > 0 and \
            ixx + iyy >= izz and iyy + izz >= ixx and ixx + izz >= iyy
        problems += not ok
        total += m
        print(f'{name:20} {m:8.3f} {ixx:10.3e} {iyy:10.3e} {izz:10.3e}  {"ok" if ok else "BAD"}')
    print(f'total mass {total:.3f} kg, {problems} problem(s)')
    return 1 if problems else 0


if __name__ == '__main__':
    sys.exit(main(sys.argv[1]))
```

## Two publishers, one tree

This is the first of the course's common issues, and it is worth being blunt about it: most beginners cannot tell `joint_state_publisher` from `robot_state_publisher`, and the confusion quietly wrecks everything downstream.

`robot_state_publisher` is the one that matters. It reads the URDF (which arrives in the `robot_description` parameter) and, for every joint position it hears on `/joint_states`, computes and publishes the matching transforms on `/tf`. The joints that never move — the fixed ones — it publishes exactly once on `/tf_static`. It invents nothing; it only turns joint positions plus the URDF into transforms.

`joint_state_publisher`, and its slider version `joint_state_publisher_gui`, does the opposite: it **generates** joint positions without any sensor, and publishes them as `/joint_states`. On a real robot, or in Gazebo, the hardware driver or the simulator publishes `/joint_states` instead, and you simply stop running `joint_state_publisher`. The two programs sit on opposite ends of the same pipe, which is why they have such similar names and why people mix them up.

## Reading a transform

A transform is a translation plus a rotation that together turn coordinates in one frame into coordinates in another. Chain several transforms along the tree and you get the transform between any two frames — and the course names frames and transforms as the concept that blocks most people's understanding of URDF, so it is worth doing this chaining once by hand.

To pose the head at an exact angle instead of dragging a slider, here is a tiny publisher:

<CodeSource path="examples/month-4/urdf-tf/pose_head.py" />

```python
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Publish /joint_states for m4bot with the head at a chosen pan and tilt (in degrees).

usage: python3 pose_head.py PAN_DEG TILT_DEG
Stands in for joint_state_publisher_gui when you want exact angles from the terminal.
On the real robot, the motor driver publishes /joint_states instead."""
import math
import sys

import rclpy
from rclpy.executors import ExternalShutdownException
from rclpy.node import Node
from sensor_msgs.msg import JointState


def main():
    pan, tilt = (math.radians(float(a)) for a in sys.argv[1:3])
    rclpy.init()
    node = Node('pose_head')
    pub = node.create_publisher(JointState, 'joint_states', 10)

    def tick():
        msg = JointState()
        msg.header.stamp = node.get_clock().now().to_msg()   # robot_state_publisher needs a stamp
        msg.name = ['left_wheel_joint', 'right_wheel_joint', 'pan_joint', 'tilt_joint']
        msg.position = [0.0, 0.0, pan, tilt]
        pub.publish(msg)

    node.create_timer(0.1, tick)
    try:
        rclpy.spin(node)
    except (KeyboardInterrupt, ExternalShutdownException):
        pass
    finally:
        node.destroy_node()
        rclpy.try_shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

It publishes `/joint_states` ten times a second with a fresh timestamp, which `robot_state_publisher` needs in order to work. How to read it. The translation is in metres. The rotation appears twice: as a quaternion (x, y, z, w), which is what TF stores because it has no singularities, and as roll-pitch-yaw, which humans read — here the yaw is 30°, the pan angle we asked for. The last block is the 4×4 homogeneous matrix: the top-left 3×3 is the rotation (cos 30° = 0.866 and sin 30° = 0.500 appear where you would expect), and the right-hand column is the translation. Every one of those numbers is a transform you could have computed yourself, and that is the point of doing it.

**Worked example.** Walk the chain `base_link` to `camera_link` by hand, all steps shown:

```text
base_link -> mast_link:  translate (-0.06, 0, 0.07)          [chassis_top = 0.08 - 0.01 = 0.07]
mast_link -> pan_link:   translate (0, 0, 0.20), then rotate 30° about z
pan_link  -> tilt_link:  translate (0, 0, 0.035)
tilt_link -> camera_link: translate (0.02, 0, 0)

The last two offsets live in the turned pan frame, so rotate (0.02, 0, 0.035) by 30° about z:
    x = 0.02 × cos 30° = 0.02 × 0.866 = 0.0173
    y = 0.02 × sin 30° = 0.02 × 0.500 = 0.0100
    z = 0.035
Add the part that the pan rotation leaves untouched:
    x = -0.06 + 0.0173 = -0.0427 ≈ -0.043
    y =  0.00 + 0.0100 = 0.010
    z =  0.07 + 0.20 + 0.035 = 0.305
```

Rounded to millimetres, that is exactly `tf2_echo`'s `[-0.043, 0.010, 0.305]`. This chaining is what TF does for you thousands of times a second, and it is also why a wrong origin anywhere up the chain moves everything below it: get `mast_joint` wrong and the whole head, camera included, moves with it.

## Diagnosing a broken tree

Break a TF tree and, in the course's words, you have the single most common failure in the whole stack — the month's milestone even asks you to diagnose one. Rather than wait for it to happen, we will cause three realistic breaks on purpose, using `joints:=none` for one of them and a driver with the wrong frame name for another.

```bash
ros2 launch m4_description display.launch.py joints:=none rviz:=false
ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_echo base_link camera_link
ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_echo base_link laser
ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_echo base_link lidar_link
```

```text
$ ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_echo base_link laser
[INFO] [1792444932.407209955] [tf2_echo]: Waiting for transform base_link ->  laser: Invalid frame ID "laser" passed to canTransform argument source_frame - frame does not exist
$ ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_echo base_link lidar_link
At time 0.0
- Translation: [0.120, 0.000, 0.090]
- Rotation: in Quaternion (xyzw) [0.000, 0.000, 0.000, 1.000]
- Rotation: in RPY (radian) [0.000, -0.000, 0.000]
- Rotation: in RPY (degree) [0.000, -0.000, 0.000]
```

Read the three messages; each names a different disease.

1. **"not part of the same tree … two or more unconnected trees"** means a link in the chain is cut. Here the pan and tilt joints have no positions, so `mast_link` and everything above it form a separate tree from `base_link`. The fix is whatever should be publishing `/joint_states`: the GUI, the driver, or Gazebo.
2. **`Invalid frame ID "laser" … frame does not exist`** means nobody publishes a frame with that name at all. The classic cause is a lidar driver that stamps its scans with `laser` while your URDF calls the link `lidar_link`.
3. **`lidar_link` works even now**, because it hangs on fixed joints that `robot_state_publisher` publishes from the URDF alone. The `At time 0.0` in that output marks a static transform — it is valid at every instant.

For break (2) you have two fixes. The better one is to rename the frame in the driver's parameters so it matches your URDF. The quick one is to add a static transform that bridges the two names:

```bash
ros2 run tf2_ros static_transform_publisher --frame-id lidar_link --child-frame-id laser
```

With that running, `tf2_echo base_link laser` gives:

```text
At time 0.0
- Translation: [0.120, 0.000, 0.090]
- Rotation: in Quaternion (xyzw) [0.000, 0.000, 0.000, 1.000]
```

That missing lidar transform is, in the course's words, behind more beginner SLAM failures than any algorithm problem: SLAM cannot place a scan it cannot locate. A perfect mapping algorithm fed a scan whose frame does not exist will do nothing at all.

When your own tree breaks, work in this order:

1. `ros2 run tf2_tools view_frames` writes a PDF of the tree as it really is. Compare it with what you expect; this is exactly how the figure in Section 1 was made.
2. `tf2_echo <parent> <child>` for the exact pair that fails, and read which of the two errors it gives you.
3. `ros2 topic echo /joint_states` to see whether anyone is reporting the moving joints at all.
4. Check the `frame_id` in the failing message (`ros2 topic echo <topic> --field header.frame_id`) against the link names in your URDF.
5. For timing errors ("extrapolation into the future" or "into the past"), check that every node uses the same clock. This matters in Lesson 4, where Gazebo's simulated clock comes into play.

## Asking for a transform at the right time

The diagnosis list above ends with timing errors, "extrapolation into the future" or "into the past". This section explains where they come from and what each way of avoiding them costs. A transform has a time as well as two frame names, and "the newest transform" is a different question from "the transform when this image was taken". It is **not** the SROS2 or composition material from Lessons 1–2, and **not** the Gazebo step-size section from Lesson 4. Every output above this section is a real ROS 2 run. This one is a **synthetic** Python model of a buffer, so its output is labelled as a model.

<Resource id="tf2-time-tutorial" />

**Sources actually opened (body read).** docs.ros.org served a bot-check page to `curl` from the editor's host, so the Jazzy tutorials were read from their reStructuredText source in the `ros2_documentation` repository (jazzy branch), and the buffer behaviour from the `geometry2` source (jazzy branch).

- **ROS 2 tutorial — Using time (C++):** tf2 keeps a time snapshot of every transform, for up to 10 seconds by default. `lookupTransform()` with time 0 (`tf2::TimePointZero`) means "the latest available" transform. Asking for "now" fails with "Lookup would require extrapolation into the future", because a broadcast transform takes a few milliseconds to reach the listener's buffer. An optional fourth argument, a timeout, makes `lookupTransform()` block until the transform arrives, and raise only if it has not arrived when the timeout runs out. 以原文为准.
- **ROS 2 tutorial — Traveling in time (C++):** a lookup 5 seconds in the past fails until 5 seconds of history exist. The advanced overload takes a target time, a source time and a fixed frame, so you can ask where a frame was then relative to where another frame is now. 以原文为准.
- **ROS 2 tutorial — Using stamped datatypes with `tf2_ros::MessageFilter`:** for camera and laser data, the filter caches a stamped message until it can be transformed into the target frame. 以原文为准.
- **geometry2 (jazzy) — `tf2` BufferCore / TimeCache source:** the default cache time is 10 s (`BUFFER_CORE_DEFAULT_CACHE_TIME`); time 0 returns the newest stored sample. A time newer than the newest sample raises "extrapolation into the future", and one older than the oldest raises "extrapolation into the past". A time between two samples is interpolated: translation linearly, rotation by slerp. Nothing in this code limits how far apart the two samples may be. tf2 itself never extrapolates. 以原文为准.

<Resource id="tf2-buffercore-source" />

### Three ways to ask

An image is stamped `t_img` when the shutter closes but reaches your node later, at `t_q`. Meanwhile the head kept panning. There are three ways to ask the buffer:

| Arm | Call | What it returns |
|---|---|---|
| A_latest | `lookupTransform(base_link, camera_link, time 0)` | the newest sample in the buffer at `t_q` |
| B_stamped | `lookupTransform(base_link, camera_link, t_img, 50 ms)` | interpolated at `t_img`; if `t_img` is newer than the newest sample, it waits up to 50 ms and then fails |
| C_extrapolate | a hand-written workaround | the same interpolation when `t_img` is inside the buffer; otherwise a straight line through the two newest samples, with no wait |

**Worked example: a pan joint at 2 rad/s and a point 1 m in front of the camera.**

```text
latest (time 0), image 80 ms old, joint at 2 rad/s:  Δθ = 2 × 0.080 = 0.16 rad -> 2 × 1 m × sin(0.08) = 159.8 mm
stamped, samples 20 ms apart, |θ''| ≤ 40 rad/s²:     interpolation error ≤ 40 × 0.020² / 8 = 0.002 rad -> 2.0 mm
extrapolated 40 ms past the newest sample:            error ≈ ½ × 40 × 0.040² = 0.032 rad -> 32.0 mm
```

The latest transform is wrong by the joint's motion over the image's age. For one rotation axis tf2's slerp reduces to linear interpolation of the angle, so the stamped lookup is wrong only by the curvature between two samples. Extrapolation is wrong by the curvature over the whole time it reaches past the data, and that time grows with the transport latency.

**No integration step here.** The joint angle is a closed-form function of time and the buffer is a lookup table. Nothing is stepped in time, so no step-size stability criterion applies (the independent tests check that the script has no time step). The Smith-predictor toy in Month 5 is the one in this pair that integrates, and it states its criterion.

### Fair setup

`tf_buffer_time_lookup_toy.py` is **synthetic** and stdlib only. It is a Buffer stand-in, not tf2: there is no BufferCore, no message filter, no clock and no executor. **Model assumptions:** one pan joint (a planar rotation about z) is broadcast at 50 Hz with up to 2 ms of stamp jitter, and each sample reaches the buffer after a transport latency. The joint angle is a sum of two sines, 0.8–1.2 rad at 0.4–0.6 Hz plus 0.2–0.4 rad at 1.5–2.0 Hz. A 30 Hz camera stamps images that reach the node after a delivery lag. Stamps are integer microseconds, as tf2 uses integer time. The cache is 10 s and B's timeout is 50 ms. A query that is older than the cache fails for B and C alike. The error is the distance between the estimated and true position of a point 1 m in front of the camera, in millimetres. It is averaged over the images that **every** arm answered, so the arms are scored on the same images. `fail_%` is the share of images with no answer, and `wait_ms` is the mean time B blocked.

**Common random numbers:** for each (regime, seed) the trajectory, the broadcast stamps and their jitter, every transport latency, the image stamps and every delivery lag are drawn once and shared by all three arms. The arms differ **only** in the lookup rule. Five seeds; reported as mean±sd over seeds.

| Regime | Joint | Transport latency | Image delivery lag | TF stream |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| static_tree | still | 2–8 ms | 60–100 ms | continuous |
| lagging_stamp | moving | 2–8 ms | 60–100 ms | continuous |
| fresh_stamp | moving | 15–30 ms | 1–3 ms | continuous |
| tf_dropout | moving | 2–8 ms | 60–100 ms | stops for 300 ms every 1000 ms |

```python
"""tf2 Buffer lookup time: latest (time 0) vs lookup at the image stamp vs extrapolating past the buffer.

Synthetic, stdlib only.  A Buffer stand-in, not tf2: no C++ BufferCore, no
message filters, no clocks, no executor.  Real `ros2 run tf2_ros ...` and a
real camera: 本页未运行.  tf2 statements are 以原文为准.

One pan joint (planar rotation about z) is broadcast at 50 Hz; every sample
reaches the listener's buffer after a transport latency.  A camera stamps an
image at t_img and the node asks for base_link <- camera at t_q (delivery).
    A_latest      lookupTransform(target, source, time 0): newest sample in the buffer
    B_stamped     lookupTransform(..., t_img, timeout 50 ms): interpolate between the two
                  samples around t_img; if t_img is newer than the newest sample, wait up to
                  the timeout for it to arrive, else fail (ExtrapolationException)
    C_extrapolate t_img inside the buffer: same interpolation as B; newer than the newest
                  sample: extrapolate linearly from the two newest samples, no wait
tf2 itself never extrapolates (it raises); C is what a hand-rolled workaround does.
For one rotation axis, tf2's slerp reduces to linear interpolation of the angle.

Common random numbers: per (regime, seed) the joint trajectory, broadcast
stamp jitter, transport latencies, image stamps and image delivery lags are
drawn once and shared by all three arms.  Arms differ only in the lookup rule.

No integration step: the joint angle is a closed-form function of time and
the buffer is a lookup table, so no step-size stability criterion applies.
"""
import math
import random

SEEDS = (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)
US = 1_000_000            # microseconds per second; stamps are integers, as tf2 uses integer time
TF_PERIOD = 20_000        # us: 50 Hz joint_states -> robot_state_publisher -> /tf
IMG_PERIOD = 33_000       # us: about 30 Hz camera
TIMEOUT = 50_000          # us: B's lookupTransform timeout
LEVER = 1.0               # m: error is measured on a point 1 m in front of the camera
T_END = 10 * US
CACHE = 10 * US           # tf2 default cache time (BUFFER_CORE_DEFAULT_CACHE_TIME = 10 s)
NUM_ZERO = 1e-9           # |m| and sd both below this: floating-point residue, not a gap

# name: (moving, transport latency range us, image delivery lag range us, dropout (period us, length us) or None)
REGIMES = {
    "static_tree": (False, (2_000, 8_000), (60_000, 100_000), None),
    "lagging_stamp": (True, (2_000, 8_000), (60_000, 100_000), None),
    "fresh_stamp": (True, (15_000, 30_000), (1_000, 3_000), None),
    "tf_dropout": (True, (2_000, 8_000), (60_000, 100_000), (1_000_000, 300_000)),
}
ARMS = ("A_latest", "B_stamped", "C_extrapolate")


def shared_draws(regime, seed):
    """Common random numbers: one trajectory, one broadcast timeline, one camera timeline for all arms."""
    moving, lat, lag, drop = REGIMES[regime]
    rng = random.Random(f"{regime}/{seed}")
    amp, f1, ph1 = rng.uniform(0.8, 1.2), rng.uniform(0.4, 0.6), rng.uniform(0.0, 2 * math.pi)
    amp2, f2, ph2 = rng.uniform(0.2, 0.4), rng.uniform(1.5, 2.0), rng.uniform(0.0, 2 * math.pi)
    rest = rng.uniform(-0.5, 0.5)
    traj = (moving, rest, amp, f1, ph1, amp2, f2, ph2)
    tf = []                                   # (stamp, arrival) for every broadcast sample
    t = 0
    while t <= T_END:
        stamp = t + rng.randrange(0, 2_001)   # broadcast jitter up to 2 ms
        if not (drop and drop[0] // 2 <= stamp % drop[0] < drop[0] // 2 + drop[1]):
            tf.append((stamp, stamp + rng.randrange(lat[0], lat[1] + 1)))
        t += TF_PERIOD
    imgs = []                                 # (t_img, t_query)
    t = US
    while t <= T_END - US:
        imgs.append((t, t + rng.randrange(lag[0], lag[1] + 1)))
        t += IMG_PERIOD
    return traj, tf, imgs


def angle(traj, t_us):
    moving, rest, amp, f1, ph1, amp2, f2, ph2 = traj
    if not moving:
        return rest
    t = t_us / US
    return rest + amp * math.sin(2 * math.pi * f1 * t + ph1) + amp2 * math.sin(2 * math.pi * f2 * t + ph2)


def lerp(s0, a0, s1, a1, t):
    if s1 == s0:
        return a1
    return a0 + (a1 - a0) * ((t - s0) / (s1 - s0))


def lookup(arm, buf, t_img, t_q, arrivals):
    """buf: samples (stamp, angle) already in the buffer at t_q, oldest first.  Returns (angle or None, wait_us)."""
    if arm == "A_latest":
        return buf[-1][1], 0
    if buf[0][0] <= t_img <= buf[-1][0]:
        for (s0, a0), (s1, a1) in zip(buf, buf[1:]):
            if s0 <= t_img <= s1:
                return lerp(s0, a0, s1, a1, t_img), 0
    if t_img < buf[0][0]:
        return None, 0                        # into the past: older than the cache
    if arm == "C_extrapolate":
        (s0, a0), (s1, a1) = buf[-2], buf[-1]
        return lerp(s0, a0, s1, a1, t_img), 0
    for stamp, ang, arrival in arrivals:      # B: wait for a sample newer than t_img
        if stamp >= t_img:
            if arrival - t_q > TIMEOUT:
                return None, 0                # ExtrapolationException after the timeout
            return lerp(buf[-1][0], buf[-1][1], stamp, ang, t_img), arrival - t_q
    return None, 0


def run(regime, seed):
    traj, tf, imgs = shared_draws(regime, seed)
    samples = sorted((arr, st, angle(traj, st)) for st, arr in tf)
    out = {a: {"err": [], "fail": 0, "wait": []} for a in ARMS}
    answers = []
    for t_img, t_q in imgs:
        buf = sorted((st, ang) for arr, st, ang in samples if arr <= t_q and st >= t_q - CACHE)
        later = sorted((st, ang, arr) for arr, st, ang in samples if arr > t_q)
        truth = angle(traj, t_img)
        row = {}
        for arm in ARMS:
            ang, wait = lookup(arm, buf, t_img, t_q, later)
            row[arm] = None if ang is None else 2.0 * LEVER * abs(math.sin((ang - truth) / 2.0)) * 1000.0
            if ang is None:
                out[arm]["fail"] += 1
            else:
                out[arm]["wait"].append(wait / 1000.0)
        answers.append(row)
    common = [r for r in answers if all(r[a] is not None for a in ARMS)]
    res = {}
    for arm in ARMS:
        res[arm] = {
            "err": sum(r[arm] for r in common) / len(common),
            "fail": 100.0 * out[arm]["fail"] / len(imgs),
            "wait": sum(out[arm]["wait"]) / len(out[arm]["wait"]),
            "n": len(common),
        }
    return res


def mean_sd(xs):
    mu = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    return mu, math.sqrt(sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1))


def num(x):
    if x != 0.0 and abs(x) < 5e-4:
        return f"{x:.3e}"
    return f"{x:.4f}"


def sign_note(diffs, m):
    n, opp, zero = len(diffs), sum(1 for d in diffs if d * m < 0), sum(1 for d in diffs if d == 0)
    if opp == 0 and zero == 0:
        return f"same sign on all {n} seeds"
    if opp == 0:
        return f"no seed has the opposite sign, {zero} of {n} equal"
    note = f"{opp} of {n} seeds {'has' if opp == 1 else 'have'} the opposite sign"
    return note + (f", {zero} of {n} equal" if zero else "")


def fmt_pair(m, s):
    return f"{num(m)}+-{num(s)}"


def verdict(xa, xb):
    diffs = [a - b for a, b in zip(xa, xb)]
    if all(d == 0.0 for d in diffs):
        return "0.0000+-0.0000: tie: identical on every seed"
    m, s = mean_sd(diffs)
    if abs(m) < NUM_ZERO and s < NUM_ZERO:
        return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: numerically zero (floating-point residue), effectively a tie"
    if abs(m) <= s:
        label = "near-tie / small gap, within seed noise (|m| <= sd)"
    elif abs(m) < 0.2 * min(abs(mean_sd(xa)[0]), abs(mean_sd(xb)[0])):
        label = "small but consistent gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    else:
        label = "clear gap, not a tie (|m| > sd)"
    return f"{fmt_pair(m, s)}: {label}; {sign_note(diffs, m)}"


def main():
    print("Synthetic tf2 Buffer stand-in: one pan joint at 50 Hz, a 30 Hz camera; arms differ only in the lookup time rule; not tf2 itself")
    print(f"TF period {TF_PERIOD / 1000:.0f} ms, B timeout {TIMEOUT / 1000:.0f} ms, cache {CACHE // US} s, error on a point {LEVER:.0f} m in front of the camera; seeds={len(SEEDS)}")
    print("No integration step: the joint angle is closed-form in time and the buffer is a lookup table")
    res = {r: [run(r, s) for s in SEEDS] for r in REGIMES}
    print("regime arm err_mm fail_% wait_ms  (mean+-sd over seeds; err on images every arm answered)")
    for r, (moving, lat, lag, drop) in REGIMES.items():
        dtxt = f", TF dropout {drop[1] // 1000} ms every {drop[0] // 1000} ms" if drop else ""
        print(f"{r} ({'moving' if moving else 'static'} joint, transport {lat[0] // 1000}-{lat[1] // 1000} ms, image delivery lag {lag[0] // 1000}-{lag[1] // 1000} ms{dtxt}; {min(x['A_latest']['n'] for x in res[r])}-{max(x['A_latest']['n'] for x in res[r])} common images per seed)")
        for a in ARMS:
            cols = " ".join(f"{num(mean_sd([x[a][m] for x in res[r]])[0])}+-{num(mean_sd([x[a][m] for x in res[r]])[1])}" for m in ("err", "fail", "wait"))
```

### Read the regimes

How the labels are used on this page, in this order: **tie: identical on every seed** only when the paired per-seed difference is exactly zero on every seed; **numerically zero (floating-point residue), effectively a tie** when `|paired mean|` and its sd are both below 1e-9 (no sign count is printed on such a line); **near-tie / small gap, within seed noise** when `|paired mean| ≤ sd`; otherwise a **consistent** gap (`|paired mean| > sd`), called **small** if `|m|` is under 20 % of the smaller arm mean and **clear** otherwise; the 20 % rule only chooses between those two words. Every consistent line carries a same-sign count over the 5 seeds, and any seed with the opposite sign is reported. Gaps are computed from unrounded values.

- **static_tree:** the joint does not move, so every sample holds the same angle and every lookup returns it: **tie: identical on every seed** on every metric, for A−B and C−B alike. When nothing moves, time 0 is good enough.
- **lagging_stamp:** each image is 60–100 ms old when it arrives. **A loses with a clear gap:** A is off by 171.3114±37.8550 mm against 0.7814±0.2768 mm for B (paired A−B 170.5300±37.6692, same sign on all 5 seeds). Every stamp is already inside the buffer, so B never waits, and C never extrapolates. C−B is therefore a **tie: identical on every seed**, not a win for either.
- **fresh_stamp:** the image arrives 1–3 ms after its stamp, but transforms need 15–30 ms. Every stamp is newer than the newest sample. **A loses with a clear gap** (A−B 76.1896±6.5537), and **C loses with a clear gap** (C−B 15.5360±3.4333, 19.0878±4.2234 mm against 3.5518±0.7919 mm). Both are the same sign on all 5 seeds. Here B pays for it: **B loses on latency with a clear gap**, blocking 30.4452±0.4321 ms per image while A and C answer at once (A−B wait −30.4452±0.4321, same sign on all 5 seeds).
- **tf_dropout:** the TF stream stops for 300 ms every second. **B loses with a clear gap on failures:** it gives no answer for 19.7531±0.5040 % of the images, while A and C always answer (A−B fail −19.7531±0.5040, same sign on all 5 seeds). On the images every arm answered, A is still off by 199.9840±21.7527 mm and C by 47.1618±17.2687 mm, against 21.1206±9.1382 mm for B (A−B 178.8633±15.0951, C−B 26.0411±11.8584, both clear and same sign on all 5 seeds). B is not near zero here: once data resumes, an image stamped inside the gap is interpolated across 300 ms of motion, and tf2 does the same, because nothing in the cache limits the gap.

The page **does not declare a universal winner**. A stamped lookup removes the lag error, but it waits for fresh data and fails while TF is silent. Time 0 never waits and is wrong whenever the joint moves. Extrapolation never fails, and is wrong when the joint turns. Real `ros2 run tf2_ros …`, a real camera and a message filter: **本页未运行**.

## Parallel-axis inertia and the inertial origin

<Resource id="wiki-parallel-axis" />

The inertia section above computes every moment **about the centre of mass** and moves it with the geometry: the chassis's `<inertial>` takes the same origin as its box. This section asks what happens when that origin and that matrix do not belong together. In URDF the `<inertial><origin>` **is** the centre of mass, and `ixx`…`izz` are taken about **that** origin. A physics engine that rotates the link about a joint somewhere else has to move the matrix there itself. Wikipedia's *Parallel axis theorem* (body read, rev 1337841361; 以原文为准) gives the rule: for an axis parallel to one through the centre of mass and a **perpendicular** distance d away, I = I_cm + m d². Its tensor form moves the whole matrix to a new point at displacement R: J = I + m [(R · R) E₃ − R ⊗ R]. Two consequences matter here. A shift **along** an axis does not change the moment about that axis, and a shift in two directions creates a **product of inertia**, J_xy = −m R_x R_y. URDF's `ixy` is this tensor entry J_xy, sign included: an archived Wayback copy of the ROS wiki page for the URDF `<link>` element (body read; snapshot listed in the batch-21 PR3 sources note; the live page is the bot wall mentioned below) says URDF assumes a negative product-of-inertia convention, so ixy = −∫ x y dm, the same sign as J_xy = −m R_x R_y here.

This section is **not** a redo of the box / cylinder formulas or of `inertia_check.py`, and it is **not** the Gazebo step-size or contact section from Lesson 4. The ROS wiki page for the URDF `<link>` element served a bot wall to the editor's `curl`, so it is **not** cited here; the convention above is the one this lesson's macros already use.

<Formula label="Parallel-axis theorem">I = I_cm + m × d²   (d perpendicular to the axis);   J = I + m [(R · R) E₃ − R ⊗ R]</Formula>

**Worked example — a labelled hand calculation, also printed by the toy.** Take the chassis from worked example 1, m = 2.0 kg and 0.30 × 0.24 × 0.08 m, so Izz_cm = 0.0246 kg·m² and its radius of gyration √(Izz_cm / m) is 0.1109 m. Put a yaw joint at the rear edge, d = 0.15 m from the centre of mass: m d² = 2.0 × 0.15² = 0.0450 kg·m², so the moment about the joint is 0.0246 + 0.0450 = 0.0696 kg·m². A file that writes the centre-of-mass matrix at the joint claims only 0.3534 of that. At d = 0.01 m the extra term is 2.000e-4 kg·m², 0.8130 % of Izz_cm. For an offset r = (0.10, 0.08, 0) the product term is J_xy = −2.0 × 0.10 × 0.08 = -0.0160 kg·m².

**No integration step here.** The toy moves matrices with a closed-form rule. Nothing is stepped in time, so no step-size criterion applies.

### Fair setup

`urdf_parallel_axis_inertia_toy.py` is **synthetic** and stdlib only. It is not Gazebo and does not parse a URDF. **Model assumptions:** one rigid uniform box, the lesson's chassis, with a mass tolerance of ±3 % and a size tolerance of ±2 % per side (**declared toy parameters**, not a measured robot); its centre of mass sits at an offset r from a joint; a shared yaw torque TAU = 0.05 N·m. The "engine" does what any simulator has to do with a written `<inertial>`: it moves the written matrix from the written origin to the joint with the tensor form. The gravity torque about the joint's y axis is m g x, where x is the written origin's x, because the origin is where the file says the mass is.

| Arm | How the `<inertial>` is written |
|---|---|
| A_com | origin = r (the centre of mass), matrix = I_cm — the URDF convention |
| B_typo | origin = the joint, matrix = I_cm — the common typo |
| C_shifted | origin = the joint, matrix = I_cm moved to the joint with the tensor form |

**Common random numbers:** for each (regime, seed) the mass and the three sizes are drawn once and shared by all three arms. The arms differ **only** in how the `<inertial>` is written. Five seeds. Metrics: J_zz, J_xx and J_xy about the joint, the yaw acceleration alpha_z = TAU / J_zz, and the gravity torque tau_g, as mean±sd over seeds.

| Regime | Offset r (m) | What was pre-declared |
|---|---|---|
| zero | (0, 0, 0) | A, B and C **tie** on every metric |

| diagonal | (0.10, 0.08, 0) | B misses the product J_xy; C ties A |

```python
    """Tensor parallel-axis form: J = I + m[(R.R)E3 - R(x)R]."""
    rr = r[0] * r[0] + r[1] * r[1] + r[2] * r[2]
```
