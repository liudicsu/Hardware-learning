## From five lessons to one robot

Month 2 took the five pieces of a robot apart so you could meet each one on its own: Arduino input and PWM, timing with millis() and interrupts, the ESP32, motors with drivers and an encoder loop, and sensors on I2C with the complementary filter. This final lesson bolts them back together. Every robot you build from here — a line follower, a self-balancer, and later an arm or a rover — runs the same loop underneath:

**sense → estimate → decide → act**, repeated at a fixed rate.

| Step | Line follower | Self-balancer | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sense | 8 reflectance sensors | accelerometer + gyro over I2C | Lesson 5 (sensors, I2C, complementary filter) |
| Estimate | line position 0..7000 | pitch angle from a complementary filter | Lesson 5 (sensors, I2C, complementary filter) |
| Decide | PD on position error | PD on angle and angular rate | this lesson |
| Act | two motors, PWM through a TB6612FNG | same | Lesson 4 (motors, drivers, encoder loop) |
| Keep time | 100 Hz loop with millis() | 200 Hz loop with micros() | Lesson 2 (timing and interrupts) |

Read that table once more, because it is the whole month in five rows. The sensors differ and the maths differs, but the shape never changes: read something, turn it into one meaningful number, decide, drive the motors, and be back at the top of the loop before the next tick. Only the "decide" row is genuinely new; every other row you have already built, just in smaller pieces.

There is one difference in temperament, and it decides the order you build in. The line follower is **forgiving**. Get the gain wrong and it wobbles, runs wide on a corner, saws back and forth — but it keeps driving, and you can watch what it is doing and fix it. The self-balancer is **not forgiving**. If the filter, the sign of a loop, or the loop timing is even slightly off, it does not wobble; it simply falls over. "Falls over" tells you far less than "wobbles" does — it throws away almost all the information you need to fix it. So build the line follower first. It is the same loop with the consequences turned down, and everything you learn while tuning it transfers straight to the balancer.

## PID in one page

Every controller in this lesson is a PID controller, so let us compress the whole idea into one page. Define the error first, because everything else is built on it:

error = setpoint − measurement

For the line follower the setpoint is the centre of the array, `3500`, and the measurement is whatever the sensors report, so `error = 3500 − position`. For the balancer the setpoint is "level", `0` degrees, and the measurement is the pitch the filter estimates. Once you have one meaningful number like this, the controller is almost trivial.

The balancer's code writes `KP × (pitch − SETPOINT) + KD × rate`; with `error = SETPOINT − pitch` this equals `−KP × error + KD × rate`. The minus sign comes from the actuator direction: tilting forward makes the error negative, and the wheels must then drive forward, i.e. positive PWM. The D term uses the gyro rate in °/s, the derivative of the measurement, which already contains the division by time.

The three terms:

- **P (proportional)** — the output is proportional to the error, so the further off you are, the harder it pushes back. On its own, with any delay anywhere in the system, P overshoots and oscillates: it is still pushing hard as you arrive back at the setpoint, so you sail straight past it. Every real system has delay — a motor cannot change speed instantly — which is why P alone almost always rings.
- **D (derivative)** — proportional to how fast the error is changing. It behaves like a damper. As you approach the target the error is shrinking, so D produces an output that opposes the motion and brakes the correction. This is the term that kills the overshoot.
- **I (integral)** — proportional to the accumulated error over time. It removes a steady offset, for example one motor being weaker than the other, that P alone would leave behind. It can also **wind up**: while the robot is stuck against a wall, or its output is saturated, the accumulated error grows enormous, and the moment it frees itself that stored-up number flings it across the line. Clamp the integral, and leave it out entirely until P and D work.

The standard discrete form includes `dt`:

```text
output = KP × e + KI × Σ(e × dt) + KD × (e − e_prev) / dt
```

With a fixed loop period, `dt` can be folded into the gains: `KI_loop = KI × dt` and `KD_loop = KD / dt`. This is why `line_follower.ino` multiplies the per-period change `error − lastError` by `KD_loop = 0.003 / 0.01 = 0.3`, and why the firmware's `KD` (0.3) and the model's `kd` (which multiplies `(e − e_prev)/DT`, in s) are different numbers.

**Worked example.** A line follower with `KP = 0.06`, `KD = 0.003 s` and `dt = 0.01 s`. The sensors report position 4700, so the error is `3500 − 4700 = −1200`. The previous error was −1500, so the change is `−1200 − (−1500) = +300`.

```text
P term = 0.06 × (−1200) = −72
D term = 0.003 × (+300 / 0.01) = 0.003 × 30000 = +90
correction = −72 + 90 = +18
```

The P term alone wanted −72, but the error shrank by 300 in one period, so the D term adds +90 and leaves +18. The D term opposes the rate at which the error is shrinking, which limits overshoot. That correction is subtracted from the left motor and added to the right motor (as in the code), so the left motor gets `120 − 18 = 102` and the right motor gets `120 + 18 = 138`. The robot turns gently toward the line instead of swinging wide across it.

The sketch in the next part targets an **Arduino Uno**, because every earlier example in the course did and its PWM pins are well known. Moving it to the ESP32 is a pin-choice exercise, not a rewrite: on the ESP32 avoid the strapping pins and remember that ADC2 is shared with Wi-Fi (Lesson 3 covers strapping pins, ADC2 and Wi-Fi). `analogWrite` does work on recent ESP32 Arduino cores, so the motor calls stay almost identical.

Now the sensor that makes the whole robot possible. The **QTR-8RC** is eight IR emitter/phototransistor pairs in a row at 9.5 mm pitch. Each pair shines infrared at the floor and measures how much bounces back: a white surface reflects a lot, a black line reflects almost nothing. The "RC" in the name is the clever part. Each sensor output is a digital pin that the library first drives high (charging the small capacitor on the sensor's output), then releases. The library times how long the pin takes to decay back to low: fast when there is a lot of reflected light (white), up to a few milliseconds when there is little (black). Because it only measures a time, a plain digital pin is enough — **no ADC is needed**.

<Resource id="qtr-8rc" />

The Pololu `QTRSensors` library does all that timing for you. After a calibration pass it returns values from 0 (white) to 1000 (black) for each sensor, and `readLineBlack()` combines the eight into a single weighted-average position from 0 to 7000, where 3500 means the line is under the middle of the array. That one number — the estimated position — is the whole "sense" and "estimate" column of the table for this robot.

Calibration is not optional. During `calibrate()` every sensor must see both the darkest and the lightest surface it will meet, and the library records those two extremes per sensor. The usual method is to hold the robot and slide it across the line, left to right, for the full 5 seconds the routine runs. Calibrate under the same lighting you will drive under, because IR reflectance shifts with ambient light.

Mounting matters. Put the array **ahead of the wheels** and about **3 mm above the floor**; Pololu gives 3 mm as the optimal height and 9.5 mm as the maximum, after which the reflected signal is too weak. The board draws about **100 mA** with its IR LEDs on, so give it a clean supply and common ground. The wiring for the sketch in the next part is:

- Sensors 1–8 → A0, A1, A2, A3, A4, A5, D10, D11 (analog pins are used as digital inputs here).
- TB6612FNG left motor: PWMA → D5, AIN1 → D7, AIN2 → D8.
- TB6612FNG right motor: PWMB → D6, BIN1 → D4, BIN2 → D12.
- STBY → D9.
- VM → motor battery positive.
- All grounds common.

## P vs PD in a simulation

Before you touch a single wire, run this toy model and watch P and D fight it out. It is a Python program written by the site editor, not robot code — a simplified picture of a line follower that starts 20 mm off the line and is tuned with P only, then with two different values of D. The output below is exactly what this file prints.

<CodeSource path="tools/sim/models/line_sim.py" />

```python
"""line_sim.py: a line follower that starts 20 mm off the line, tuned with P and then PD.

Toy model: 8 reflectance sensors 9.5 mm apart (like a QTR-8RC), a 100 Hz control loop,
and wheels that need about 80 ms to reach a new turning speed.
"""
import math

SPEED = 0.30           # forward speed, m/s
DT = 0.01              # control loop period, s (100 Hz)
MOTOR_TAU = 0.08       # how fast the turn rate follows the command, s
SPACING = 0.0095       # sensor spacing, m
LINE_HALF_WIDTH = 0.009


def read_position(offset_m):
    """Weighted average of 8 sensor readings: 0..7000, 3500 = line under the centre."""
    total = weighted = 0.0
    for i in range(8):
        x = (i - 3.5) * SPACING + offset_m           # sensor position relative to the line
        r = 1000 * math.exp(-(x / LINE_HALF_WIDTH) ** 2)  # 0 = white, 1000 = black
        total += r
        weighted += r * i * 1000
    if total < 50:                                    # line lost: report the nearest edge
        return 0 if offset_m > 0 else 7000
    return weighted / total


def run(kp, kd, seconds=3.0):
    y, heading, turn_rate = 0.020, 0.0, 0.0          # start 20 mm to one side
    last_error = None
    log = []
    for step in range(int(seconds / DT)):
        error = 3500 - read_position(y)               # setpoint − measurement
        d_error = 0.0 if last_error is None else (error - last_error) / DT
        last_error = error
        command = kp * error + kd * d_error            # rad/s of turning, positive turns towards -y
        command = max(-6.0, min(6.0, command))
        for _ in range(10):                            # robot physics at 1 ms
            turn_rate += (command - turn_rate) * 0.001 / MOTOR_TAU
            heading -= turn_rate * 0.001
            y += SPEED * math.sin(heading) * 0.001
        log.append(((step + 1) * DT, y))
    return log


def describe(log):
    ys = [y for _, y in log]
    overshoot = max(0.0, -min(ys)) * 1000              # mm past the line on the other side
    crossings = sum(1 for a, b in zip(ys, ys[1:]) if a * b < 0)
    settled = next((t for t, _ in log if all(abs(y) < 0.002 for tt, y in log if tt >= t)), None)
    return overshoot, crossings, settled


if __name__ == "__main__":
    cases = (("P only", 0.0003, 0.0), ("P + small D", 0.0003, 0.0001), ("P + D", 0.0003, 0.0002))
    logs = {name: run(kp, kd, seconds=4.0) for name, kp, kd in cases}
    print("offset from the line (mm), every 0.5 s")
    print("  t_s  " + "  ".join(f"{name:>11}" for name, _, _ in cases))
    for k in range(0, 9):
        t = k * 0.5
        row = []
        for name, _, _ in cases:
            y = 20.0 if k == 0 else logs[name][int(round(t / DT)) - 1][1] * 1000
            row.append(f"{y:11.1f}")
        print(f"  {t:3.1f}  " + "  ".join(row))
    print()
    for name, kp, kd in cases:
        overshoot, crossings, settled = describe(logs[name])
        settled_txt = f"{settled:.2f} s" if settled is not None else "never"
        print(f"{name:11} kp={kp} kd={kd}: overshoot {overshoot:4.1f} mm, "
              f"{crossings} line crossings, within 2 mm from {settled_txt}")
```

All three runs start 20 mm to one side and all three turn toward the line. **P only** reaches the line quickly — by 0.5 s the offset is down to 4.1 mm — but it does not stop turning there. The wheels need about 80 ms to reach a new turn rate, so by the time the robot knows it has arrived its heading is still pointing across the line, and it overshoots. The sign of the offset then flips, then flips back, and the swings grow wider each time: at 3.5 s it is 61.9 mm off, and the summary says it is **never** within 2 mm. That is a system dominated by delay, exactly what P-alone does.

**A small D (0.0001)** changes almost everything. It cuts the overshoot from 62.3 mm to **4.8 mm** and settles within 2 mm by **1.47 s**. The D term is braking the correction as the error shrinks, which is precisely the damping promised in the PID discussion above. **D = 0.0002** does better still: it approaches the line without crossing it even once and is within 2 mm from **1.30 s**.

Notice the units. This model's output is a turn rate in rad/s, so the gains look tiny, and they are nothing like the `KP = 0.06` the sketch will use. Gains never transfer between models or between robots; only the procedure transfers. You will always have to tune on your own hardware. The model uses the dt form (`(e − e_prev)/DT`), while the firmware folds dt into `KD`.

## The line-follower sketch and how to tune it

Here is the whole robot in one sketch. It was compiled for the Uno with the `QTRSensors` 4.0.0 library, but it was **not run on a robot** — there is no simulator for the robot, so there is no output to show here.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-2/first-robots/line_follower/line_follower.ino" />

```cpp
// PD line follower: QTR-8RC array + TB6612FNG + two gearmotors (Arduino Uno).
// Library: "QTRSensors" by Pololu (Library Manager).
#include <QTRSensors.h>

// TB6612FNG
const int PWMA = 5, AIN1 = 7, AIN2 = 8;     // left motor
const int PWMB = 6, BIN1 = 4, BIN2 = 12;    // right motor
const int STBY = 9;

const uint8_t SENSOR_PINS[8] = {A0, A1, A2, A3, A4, A5, 10, 11};
QTRSensors qtr;
uint16_t values[8];

const float KP = 0.06;          // PWM per unit of position error  (start here)
const float KD = 0.0;           // add D after P is working: try 0.3 (= 0.003 s / 0.01 s), then tune
const int BASE_PWM = 120;       // straight-line speed
const unsigned long PERIOD_MS = 10;   // 100 Hz control loop

int lastError = 0;
unsigned long last = 0;

void setMotor(int pwmPin, int in1, int in2, int speed) {   // speed -255..255
  speed = constrain(speed, -255, 255);
  digitalWrite(in1, speed > 0);
  digitalWrite(in2, speed < 0);
  analogWrite(pwmPin, abs(speed));
}

void setup() {
  int outs[] = {PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, PWMB, BIN1, BIN2, STBY};
  for (int p : outs) pinMode(p, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(STBY, HIGH);

  qtr.setTypeRC();
  qtr.setSensorPins(SENSOR_PINS, 8);

  // Calibrate for ~5 s: slide the robot by hand so every sensor sees black and white.
  pinMode(LED_BUILTIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(LED_BUILTIN, HIGH);
  for (int i = 0; i < 200; i++) qtr.calibrate();
  digitalWrite(LED_BUILTIN, LOW);
  last = millis();
}

void loop() {
  unsigned long now = millis();
  if (now - last < PERIOD_MS) return;
  last += PERIOD_MS;

  uint16_t position = qtr.readLineBlack(values);   // 0..7000, 3500 = centred
  int error = 3500 - (int)position;                // setpoint − measurement
  int derivative = error - lastError;              // per loop period (fixed 10 ms): KD here = KD_continuous / dt
  lastError = error;

  int correction = (int)(KP * error + KD * derivative);
  setMotor(PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, BASE_PWM - correction);
  setMotor(PWMB, BIN1, BIN2, BASE_PWM + correction);
}
```

Walk through it from the top. The loop runs at a fixed 100 Hz using `last += PERIOD_MS` rather than `last = now` (Lesson 2: advancing by a fixed step keeps the rate steady, so "derivative per loop" means the same thing every single time). In `setup()` the robot calibrates 200 times with the built-in LED lit as a visual cue — that LED is also how you know when to start sliding the robot across the line. In the loop, `readLineBlack()` returns the position, `error` is the centre minus the position (setpoint − measurement), and `derivative` is the change in error since the last pass. The correction is subtracted from motor A (left) and added to motor B (right). Finally `setMotor()` clamps the speed to −255..255 and sets the direction pins, exactly like the `drive()` helper from Lesson 4.

One thing to check before you trust any of it: if the robot steers **away** from the line instead of toward it, the sign is wrong for your wiring. Flip the sign of `KP` and `KD` (or swap which motor gets the plus and which gets the minus). There is no universal right answer here; it depends on which side of the robot you call left.

Tuning is a procedure, not a guess. Follow it in order:

1. **Wheels off the ground first.** Move the line under the sensors by hand and watch the motors. Check they react the right way: line to the left should slow the left wheel, not the right.
2. Set `KD = 0` and a low `BASE_PWM` (about 80). Raise `KP` until the robot follows the line but wiggles.
3. **Film it.** This is your "P only" clip.
4. Add `KD` in small steps until the wiggle dies. If the motors buzz or twitch, `KD` is too high — it amplifies sensor noise.
5. **Film it again.** This is your "PD" clip.
6. Raise `BASE_PWM` and repeat. Faster needs more D; the faster you go, the sooner delay bites.
7. Write every attempt in a tuning log: `KP`, `KD`, `BASE_PWM`, and what happened.

The oscillation you see with P only is not a bug — it is the exact thing you are learning to remove. Keep that video. A before-and-after pair teaches a reader more than a single clip of a robot that happens to work.
</Callout>

## Self-balancer: why it is hard

A self-balancer is an **inverted pendulum** — the same problem as balancing a broom upright on your hand. Left alone, it does not drift slowly; it falls in well under a second. That has a hard consequence: the controller must react within milliseconds, and it must do so **every few milliseconds**, not once in a while. On top of that it needs an angle estimate that is both fast and non-drifting, which is exactly the job of the complementary filter from Lesson 5 — the gyro gives a fast but drifting angle, the accelerometer a slow but absolute one, and the filter fuses them so you get a number good enough to act on. The wheels then move under the centre of mass to catch the fall: the robot is not balancing statically, it is continuously driving itself back under its own centre of gravity.

Now the model. This second Python toy model was also written by the site editor, and its output is exactly what it prints. It simulates the robot's centre of mass 0.10 m above the axle, with the controller setting the wheels' acceleration. The angle comes from a complementary filter with a 0.5 s time constant at every loop rate.

<CodeSource path="tools/sim/models/balance_sim.py" />

```python
"""balance_sim.py: why a self-balancer needs both a good angle estimate and a fast, steady loop.

Toy inverted pendulum: the robot's centre of mass sits 0.10 m above the axle and the
controller sets the wheels' acceleration. The angle comes from a complementary filter
(gyro bias already calibrated out, noisy gyro and accelerometer) whose time constant
is 0.5 s at every loop rate.
Real robots also need an outer speed loop from the wheel encoders; this model leaves it out.
"""
import math
import random

G, L = 9.81, 0.10            # gravity, height of the centre of mass (m)
MAX_ACCEL = 6.0              # m/s^2 the motors can deliver
MOTOR_TAU = 0.02             # wheels reach a new acceleration in ~20 ms


def run(loop_hz, kp, kd, use_filter=True, seconds=5.0, seed=3):
    """Start tipped 3 degrees; at t = 2.5 s someone gives the robot a push."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    dt_ctrl = 1.0 / loop_hz
    theta, omega, accel = math.radians(3.0), 0.0, 0.0    # starts tipped 3 degrees
    est, next_ctrl, command, t = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    worst, sq, n, pushed = 0.0, 0.0, 0, False
    while t < seconds:
        if t >= next_ctrl:                               # --- controller runs here ---
            next_ctrl = t + dt_ctrl
            gyro = math.degrees(omega) + rng.gauss(0, 0.3)
            acc = math.degrees(theta) + rng.gauss(0, 2.5)     # accelerometer tilt, shaken by the motors
            if use_filter:
                a = 0.5 / (0.5 + dt_ctrl)                  # same 0.5 s time constant at any rate
                est = a * (est + gyro * dt_ctrl) + (1 - a) * acc
            else:
                est = acc                                # raw accelerometer angle only
            command = kp * est + kd * gyro               # PD on angle (deg) and rate (deg/s)
            command = max(-MAX_ACCEL, min(MAX_ACCEL, command))
        # --- physics at 1 ms ---
        accel += (command - accel) * 0.001 / MOTOR_TAU
        alpha = (G * math.sin(theta) - accel * math.cos(theta)) / L
        omega += alpha * 0.001
        theta += omega * 0.001
        if not pushed and t >= 2.5:
            omega += math.radians(40)                      # a push: +40 deg/s
            pushed = True
        if 1.0 < t < 2.5:
            sq += math.degrees(theta) ** 2
            n += 1
        t += 0.001
        worst = max(worst, abs(math.degrees(theta)))
        if worst > 45:
            return f"fell over after {t:.2f} s"
    return f"upright, wobble {math.sqrt(sq / n):.2f} deg rms, worst tilt {worst:.1f} deg"


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("200 Hz, PD, filtered angle:  ", run(200, kp=0.8, kd=0.04))
    print("200 Hz, PD, raw accel angle: ", run(200, kp=0.8, kd=0.04, use_filter=False))
    print("200 Hz, P only:              ", run(200, kp=0.8, kd=0.0))
    print(" 50 Hz, PD, filtered angle:  ", run(50, kp=0.8, kd=0.04))
    print(" 10 Hz, PD, filtered angle:  ", run(10, kp=0.8, kd=0.04))
```

```text
200 Hz, PD, raw accel angle:  upright, wobble 1.09 deg rms, worst tilt 3.6 deg
200 Hz, P only:               fell over after 1.27 s
 50 Hz, PD, filtered angle:   upright, wobble 0.71 deg rms, worst tilt 3.5 deg
 10 Hz, PD, filtered angle:   fell over after 1.23 s
```

</SimOutput>

Read the five lines one at a time.

- **200 Hz, PD, filtered angle: upright, wobble 0.36 deg rms, worst tilt 3.6 deg.** The robot starts tipped 3° and takes a 40 °/s push at 2.5 s, and stays up. It wobbles only 0.36° rms — a very steady balance. This is the whole design working.
- **200 Hz, PD, raw accel angle: upright, wobble 1.09 deg rms, worst tilt 3.6 deg.** Here the filter is bypassed and the controller uses the raw accelerometer angle, which the model shakes with 2.5° of noise (motor vibration). It still stands, but it wobbles **three times as much**. On a real robot that noise goes straight into the motors and shows up as heat and buzzing.
- **200 Hz, P only: fell over after 1.27 s.** With no D term nothing damps the swing. It never recovers from the 3° start: each swing is bigger than the last, and it falls at 1.27 s, before the push at 2.5 s even arrives.
- **50 Hz, PD, filtered angle: upright, wobble 0.71 deg rms, worst tilt 3.5 deg.** Half the loop rate still stands, but the wobble roughly doubles. Slower correction means less damping.
- **10 Hz, PD, filtered angle: fell over after 1.23 s.** At a twentieth of the rate, by the time the next correction arrives the robot has already moved too far. It falls at 1.23 s, again before the push. Raising the gains only makes it fall sooner. Softer gains (we tried kp = 0.3, kd = 0.02 in the same model) keep it up at 10 Hz, but it wobbles about 3° rms, nearly nine times worse than at 200 Hz, with almost no margin. The loop itself is too slow.

Here is the arithmetic behind that last point. Loop period is just one over the rate:

```text
200 Hz: period = 1 / 200 = 0.005 s = 5 ms
 10 Hz: period = 1 / 10  = 0.1 s   = 100 ms
```

Five milliseconds is a short time for something that falls in about a second; a hundred milliseconds is twenty times longer than the fast loop and simply too late.

Be honest about what this model is. Its own docstring says it: there is **no outer speed loop**, so it ignores the fact that a real balancer slowly rolls away and needs the wheel encoders (Lesson 4) to fix that. And the gains `kp = 0.8`, `kd = 0.04` are in the model's own units, not PWM per degree — they are not values you can paste into firmware. The model shows you *why* each requirement exists, not the numbers to ship.

When the complementary filter is no longer enough — when you want a principled way to blend the sensors and to handle the gyro bias properly — the standard next step is a Kalman filter. The book below is the usual recommendation, and it runs as notebooks you can actually execute.

## The balancer sketch

This is the firmware. It runs the complementary filter and a PD controller at a fixed 200 Hz, and it protects itself when it falls over.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-2/first-robots/balancer/balancer.ino" />

```cpp
// Self-balancer skeleton: MPU-6050 + complementary filter + PD at a fixed 200 Hz.
// Uno + TB6612FNG (both channels) + two gearmotors. Mount the MPU-6050 so that
// tilting forward makes the pitch positive. Tune KP first, then KD (see the lesson).
#include <Wire.h>

const int PWMA = 5, AIN1 = 7, AIN2 = 8;     // left motor
const int PWMB = 6, BIN1 = 4, BIN2 = 12;    // right motor
const int STBY = 9;

const uint8_t MPU = 0x68;
const float ACCEL_LSB_PER_G = 16384.0;      // +-2 g (default)
const float GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS = 131.0;       // +-250 deg/s (default)

const unsigned long PERIOD_US = 5000;       // 200 Hz control loop
const float DT = PERIOD_US / 1e6;
const float TAU = 0.5;                      // filter time constant, s
const float ALPHA = TAU / (TAU + DT);       // = 0.990 at 200 Hz

float KP = 25.0;                            // PWM per degree
float KD = 0.8;                             // PWM per deg/s
const float SETPOINT = 0.0;                 // pitch when balanced, deg (trim this)
const float CUTOFF_DEG = 40.0;              // beyond this it has fallen: motors off

float pitch = 0, gyroBias = 0;
bool running = false;
unsigned long nextTick = 0, worstLoopUs = 0, lastReport = 0;

void setMotor(int pwmPin, int in1, int in2, int speed) {   // -255..255
  speed = constrain(speed, -255, 255);
  digitalWrite(in1, speed > 0);
  digitalWrite(in2, speed < 0);
  analogWrite(pwmPin, abs(speed));
}

void stopMotors() {
  setMotor(PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, 0);
  setMotor(PWMB, BIN1, BIN2, 0);
}

void readImu(float &accelPitch, float &rate) {
  Wire.beginTransmission(MPU);
  Wire.write(0x3B);                         // ACCEL_XOUT_H
  Wire.endTransmission(false);
  Wire.requestFrom(MPU, (uint8_t)14);
  int16_t v[7];
  for (int i = 0; i < 7; i++) v[i] = (Wire.read() << 8) | Wire.read();
  accelPitch = atan2(v[0] / ACCEL_LSB_PER_G, v[2] / ACCEL_LSB_PER_G) * 180.0 / PI;
  rate = v[5] / GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS - gyroBias; // gyro Y
}

void setup() {
  int outs[] = {PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, PWMB, BIN1, BIN2, STBY};
  for (int p : outs) pinMode(p, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(STBY, HIGH);
  Serial.begin(115200);
  Wire.begin();
  Wire.setClock(400000);                    // fast-mode I2C: shorter reads
  Wire.beginTransmission(MPU);
  Wire.write(0x6B);                         // PWR_MGMT_1: wake up
  Wire.write(0);
  Wire.endTransmission();

  // Gyro bias: hold the robot still for one second at power-up.
  float sum = 0, a, r;
  for (int i = 0; i < 200; i++) { readImu(a, r); sum += r; delay(5); }
  gyroBias = sum / 200;
  readImu(a, r);
  pitch = a;                                // start the filter from the accelerometer
  Serial.print(F("gyro bias (deg/s): "));
  Serial.println(gyroBias, 2);
  nextTick = micros();
}

void loop() {
  if ((long)(micros() - nextTick) < 0) return;   // wait for the next 5 ms tick
  unsigned long start = micros();
  nextTick += PERIOD_US;

  float accelPitch, rate;
  readImu(accelPitch, rate);
  pitch = ALPHA * (pitch + rate * DT) + (1 - ALPHA) * accelPitch;

  if (fabs(pitch) > CUTOFF_DEG) running = false;   // fallen: stop
  if (!running && fabs(pitch) < 5.0) running = true; // stood back up by hand: go

  if (running) {
    int out = (int)(KP * (pitch - SETPOINT) + KD * rate);
    setMotor(PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, out);
    setMotor(PWMB, BIN1, BIN2, out);
  } else {
    stopMotors();
  }

  unsigned long used = micros() - start;
  if (used > worstLoopUs) worstLoopUs = used;
  if (millis() - lastReport >= 1000) {      // once a second; printing takes time too
    lastReport = millis();
    Serial.print(F("pitch "));
    Serial.print(pitch, 1);
    Serial.print(F("  worst loop us "));
    Serial.println(worstLoopUs);
    worstLoopUs = 0;
  }
}
```

Walk through the important parts.

- **Fixed 200 Hz tick with micros().** `nextTick += PERIOD_US` advances the target by exactly 5 ms every loop. The comparison `(long)(micros() - nextTick) < 0` survives the micros() overflow, as Lesson 2 explained — signed subtraction makes the wrap-around behave.
- **ALPHA from the time constant.** `ALPHA = TAU / (TAU + DT)` with `TAU = 0.5` and `DT = 0.005` gives 0.990. Because ALPHA is derived from the period, changing the loop rate keeps the same 0.5 s time constant — you are not silently changing the filter when you change the clock.
- **Gyro bias measured at startup.** The first second, while the robot is held still, the code averages 200 gyro readings into `gyroBias`. That bias is the thing that makes a gyro-only angle drift, which is why the complementary filter of Lesson 5 exists; here it is subtracted on every read.
- **Start from the accelerometer.** `pitch = a` seeds the filter with an absolute angle instead of 0, so it does not have to spend seconds converging.
- **PD on pitch and the gyro rate directly.** The gyro reading *is* a clean derivative of the angle, so the code uses it straight, rather than differencing the noisy angle.
- **The 40° cutoff.** Past 40° the robot has fallen, so `running` goes false and the motors stop; it only restarts when you stand it back up within 5°. This is a safety feature — never skip it.
- **Worst loop time, once a second.** The code tracks the longest loop and reports it every second, so you can see whether you are close to overrunning the 5 ms budget.

This is a 10 s run.

The measured bias of **1.54 °/s** matches the simulated sensor's +1.5 °/s. The pitch follows the scripted tilt up to 30° and back to 0° with no drift, exactly as the complementary filter is supposed to do. And the loop used about **0.73 ms** of its 5 ms budget, so there is room to spare.

The one caveat: this simulator does not model the time the I2C bus takes, so that 0.73 ms is optimistic. Estimate the real cost:

```text
18 bytes × 9 clock cycles each = 162 cycles
400 kHz: 162 / 400 000 = 0.000 405 s ≈ 0.41 ms
100 kHz: 162 / 100 000 = 0.001 62 s  ≈ 1.62 ms
```

Reading 14 data bytes, plus addressing and the register byte, is about 18 bytes, and each byte on I2C takes 9 clock cycles (8 bits plus an acknowledge). At 400 kHz that is about 0.41 ms; at the default 100 kHz it is 1.62 ms — nearly a third of the whole 5 ms budget, just for the sensor. That is why the sketch calls `Wire.setClock(400000)`.

Bring-up is the same discipline as the line follower, but even less forgiving:

1. **Check the sign with the wheels off the ground.** Tilt the robot forward; the wheels must drive **forward**, i.e. in the direction the robot is falling, to get back under it. If they drive backward, flip `KP` and `KD`.
2. Set `KD = 0` and raise `KP` until it tries to stand but oscillates quickly.
3. Add `KD` until the oscillation is damped.
4. Trim `SETPOINT` so it does not lean — battery position changes where "balanced" is.
5. Expect it to roll slowly away. The fix is an outer loop on wheel speed from the encoders (Lesson 4) that nudges `SETPOINT`; that is the natural next step and a great README section.

<Callout type="warn" title="A balancer can lunge">
A self-balancer can lunge suddenly at full power, with no warning. Hold it by the frame, keep fingers well away from the wheels, keep the 40° cut-off, and for the first tests have the battery where you can unplug it instantly.
</Callout>

### Optional challenge: the outer speed loop

This part is optional. The balancer above stays upright, but it slowly drifts away, because nothing in it cares where the wheels are going. A balancer that stays in one place needs a second controller wrapped around the first one. Control engineers call this a **cascade**.

- The **inner loop** is the one you already have. It runs at 200 Hz, reads the pitch, and drives the motors so the robot holds a requested angle, `SETPOINT`.
- The **outer loop** is new. It runs about 5–10 times slower (every 4th to 8th tick, so 25–50 Hz). It reads the wheel speed from the encoders (Lesson 4) and changes `SETPOINT` a little, to slow the robot down.

<Diagram id="first-robots-cascade" />

The surprising part is the direction. Say the robot is drifting forward. To stop, it has to lean slightly **backward**, which makes gravity pull it back. To get into that backward lean, the inner loop first drives the wheels forward a little harder, and only then does the robot slow down. So the setpoint the outer loop asks for leans the opposite way to the direction the robot is moving. Expect it to look wrong for a moment every time.

Outline of the outer loop (a description of the structure, not tested code):

```text
every 8th 200 Hz tick (25 Hz):
  speed     = (left counts + right counts) / 2 since the last outer tick
  error     = 0 − speed
  integral  = clamp(integral + error, −I_MAX, I_MAX)          # anti-windup
  SETPOINT  = clamp(KP_SPEED × error + KI_SPEED × integral, −3°, +3°)
```

**You have done it right when:**
- the inner loop alone already balances. Do not start the outer loop before that works.
- the outer loop is clearly slower than the inner one, and its output is clamped to a few degrees. A wild setpoint knocks the robot over.
- on a flat floor it stays within about one wheel turn of where it started for 60 s, instead of rolling away.
- after a gentle push it rolls a little, comes back, and stops without falling or oscillating more and more.
- your tuning log records the outer gains separately from the inner ones, and the README explains the cascade in your own words.

Do not worry if this does not work yet. [Month 3, Lesson 4 (PID & control)](/en/month/month-3/pid-control) comes back to this exact robot and finishes the outer loop: a cascade model, firmware with anti-windup, and why the robot first moves the "wrong" way.
