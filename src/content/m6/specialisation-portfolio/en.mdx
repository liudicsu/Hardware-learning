Five and a half months ago this course started with Ohm's law. Since then you have built circuits, written firmware, designed and printed parts, assembled an arm, run ROS 2 robots in simulation, worked through the maths of control and kinematics, and trained policies from demonstrations. That is a broad base, and broad is not what gets you hired. The last milestones of the roadmap are to **pick one direction**, build portfolio work in it, and start applying. This lesson is about doing those three things deliberately.

## Choosing a direction

Robotics jobs cluster into a handful of specialisations. Each one maps onto parts of this course you have already done, which is the best evidence of whether you like it:

| direction | the daily work | where you did it in this course |
|---|---|---|
| Embedded and controls | firmware, motor control, sensor drivers, real-time loops, tuning | Month 2 (Arduino, motors, sensors), Month 3 PID, Month 5 control and LQR |
| Mechanical and hardware design | CAD, mechanisms, tolerances, prototyping, design for manufacture | Month 3 (CAD, FDM printing, transmissions, the gripper) |
| Robot software and navigation | ROS 2 systems, mapping, localisation, planning, integration | Month 4 (ROS 2, URDF, Gazebo, ros2_control, SLAM and Nav2) |
| Manipulation and motion planning | kinematics, grasping, MoveIt, calibration, perception for picking | Month 5 (kinematics, camera calibration, MoveIt) |
| Robot learning | datasets, imitation learning, VLAs, evaluation, sim-to-real | Month 6 (lessons 1 to 4) |

Three questions decide it better than any ranking of which field is hottest. **Which lessons did you go back to without being asked?** Interest that survives the boring parts is what keeps you going through a hard project. **What can you show already?** A direction where you have one working project is months closer than one where you have none. **Which jobs exist where you want to work?** Read 20 real job adverts in your region or remote, and count which skills they ask for; that tally is better evidence than any article. Write your answer as one sentence, for example "embedded and controls, because I spent the most extra time on the balancer and local robotics companies hire firmware engineers", and keep it where you can see it.

## Three portfolio projects

The milestone asks for three projects, each with **video, metrics and failure analysis**. Pick them so that together they show depth in your direction plus one adjacent skill. From this course, good starting points are:

- the self-balancer with its cascade loop ([Month 3, PID](/en/month/month-3/pid-control)): metrics such as how long it stays within 5 cm of its start point, and how it recovers from a push;
- the mapped and navigated room ([Month 4, SLAM and Nav2](/en/month/month-4/slam-nav)): metrics such as goals reached out of goals sent, and map accuracy against the known room;
- a learned pick and place on your SO-101 (lessons 1 to 4 of this month): success out of a fixed list of start positions, with a failure table.

Move each one a step beyond the lesson. A reviewer has seen the tutorial version many times; what they look for is the part you decided yourself: a different object, a harder condition, a measurement nobody asked you for, a fix for a failure you found.

## The README a reviewer reads

A reviewer gives a project about two minutes. In that time they want to see it working, see how well it works, see that you know how it fails, and see what you built yourself. `portfolio_check.py` checks a project README for the eight parts that answer those questions. It works by simple text rules, so it can only tell you that a part is missing, not whether it is good:

<CodeSource path="examples/month-6/specialisation-portfolio/portfolio_check.py" />

```python
"""portfolio_check.py: check a project README for what a reviewer looks for in the first two minutes.

usage: python3 portfolio_check.py PROJECT_DIR [PROJECT_DIR ...]      (exit code 1 if anything is missing)

It reads PROJECT_DIR/README.md and looks for eight things, by simple text rules (it cannot judge quality,
only whether the part is there):
  video         a link to a video or animation (YouTube, Bilibili, Vimeo, .mp4, .gif, .webm)
  result        a success count written as k/n, or a measured number with a unit, in a table row
  trials        the number of trials or test conditions stated in words ("20 trials", "10 positions")
  failures      a section on failures (heading containing "failure" or "失败")
  reproduce     a section on reproducing it (heading with "reproduce", "how to run" or "复现") with a code block
  hardware      a section listing the hardware (heading with "hardware", "bill of materials", "BOM" or "硬件")
  contribution  what you did yourself versus reused (heading with "my part", "what I built", "credits" or "贡献")
  next          limitations or next steps (heading with "limitation", "next step" or "局限", "下一步")
Standard library only.
"""
import os
import re
import sys

UNIT = r'(?:mm|cm|m|s|ms|Hz|deg|°|%|N|g|kg|V|A|mA)'
CHECKS = [
    ('video', 'a video or GIF of it working',
     lambda t, h: re.search(r'(youtube\.com|youtu\.be|bilibili\.com|vimeo\.com|\.mp4|\.gif|\.webm)', t, re.I)),
    ('result', 'a results table with k/n or measured numbers',
     lambda t, h: re.search(r'^\|.*(\b\d+\s*/\s*\d+\b|\d(?:\.\d+)?\s*' + UNIT + r'\b).*\|\s*$', t, re.M)),
    ('trials', 'how many trials or conditions were tested',
     lambda t, h: re.search(r'\b\d+\s+(trials|runs|attempts|positions|episodes)\b|\d+\s*(次试验|次运行|个位置)', t, re.I)),
    ('failures', 'a failure analysis section',
     lambda t, h: any(re.search(r'failure|失败', x, re.I) for x in h)),
    ('reproduce', 'how to reproduce it, with commands',
     lambda t, h: any(re.search(r'reproduce|how to run|复现', x, re.I) for x in h) and '```' in t),
    ('hardware', 'the hardware or bill of materials',
     lambda t, h: any(re.search(r'hardware|bill of materials|\bBOM\b|硬件', x, re.I) for x in h)),
    ('contribution', 'what you built yourself versus reused',
     lambda t, h: any(re.search(r'my part|what i built|credits|贡献', x, re.I) for x in h)),
    ('next', 'limitations or next steps',
     lambda t, h: any(re.search(r'limitation|next step|局限|下一步', x, re.I) for x in h)),
]


def check(project):
    path = os.path.join(project, 'README.md')
    with open(path, encoding='utf-8') as f:
        text = f.read()
    headings = [line.lstrip('#').strip() for line in text.splitlines() if line.startswith('#')]
    words = len(re.findall(r'\w+', text))
    print(f'{os.path.basename(os.path.normpath(project))}: README.md, {words} words, {len(headings)} headings')
    missing = 0
    for name, what, rule in CHECKS:
        ok = bool(rule(text, headings))
        missing += not ok
        print(f'  {"ok     " if ok else "MISSING"} {name:<13} {what}')
    print(f'  {len(CHECKS) - missing} of {len(CHECKS)} present')
    return missing


def main():
    if len(sys.argv) < 2:
        sys.exit(__doc__.strip().splitlines()[2])
    missing = 0
    for i, project in enumerate(sys.argv[1:]):
        if i:
            print()
        missing += check(project)
    sys.exit(1 if missing else 0)


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

## Three levels of follow-up questions

The last milestone is to answer three levels of follow-up questions about any line of your code. Interviewers do this on purpose: they pick a line and keep asking "why" until they find the edge of your understanding. Practise it on your own code. Take this line from [lesson 1](/en/month/month-6/behaviour-cloning)'s `bc_reach.py`:

```python
best = min(obs, key=lambda o: (o[0][0] - p[0]) ** 2 + (o[0][1] - p[1]) ** 2)
```

- **What does it do?** It finds the recorded frame whose observation is closest to the current position, by squared Euclidean distance, so the policy can replay that frame's action.
- **Why this way?** Squared distance gives the same nearest frame as distance without the square root. Nearest-neighbour is the simplest policy that fits the data exactly, which makes covariate shift easy to see; a neural network would hide it behind training choices.
- **What breaks, and what would you change?** It scans every frame each step, so time grows with the dataset (3061 frames is fine, a million is not; a k-d tree fixes that). It treats x and y as having the same scale, which would be wrong if the observation mixed metres and radians, so you would normalise each dimension first. And it can only replay actions it has seen, which is why it fails near the goal.

If you cannot answer the third level about a line, that line is what to study next. Doing this for the key lines of each portfolio project is the best interview preparation there is.

## Applying

- **One page CV, projects first.** For each project: one line on what it does, one number from its results table, and a link. Skills lists without evidence are skipped.
- **A short message per application.** Two or three sentences: which of your projects matches what they build, and the number that shows it works. Point at the video.
- **Apply while you build.** Do not wait for the third project to be perfect. Start with roles that match your direction, including internships and technician or test roles that put you next to the robots.
- **Show your work in public.** Push the repositories, keep the READMEs current, and write a short post when a project hits a milestone. A reviewer who finds a steady history of real work is already halfway convinced.
- **Keep a log.** Note every application, the reply and every interview question you could not answer, and study those.

## Common mistakes

- **Staying broad.** "I can do a bit of everything" is the default for a course graduate. One direction with three deep projects is more convincing than six shallow ones.
- **Projects without numbers.** "Works well" invites the question "how well?". Give success out of trials, a time, an error in millimetres.
- **Hiding failures.** A failure table shows you understand the system; a perfect video with no failures looks like the one good take out of many.
- **Tutorial projects as-is.** Reviewers recognise the standard tutorials. Show what you changed and why.
- **Code you cannot explain.** If you copied it or generated it and cannot answer the third-level question, either learn it or say plainly that it is reused, in the README's credits section.
