The [previous lesson](/en/month/month-6/behaviour-cloning) cloned an expert that always did the same thing in the same place. Human operators do not. Asked to move a cup past an obstacle, one operator goes left, another goes right, and the same operator does both on different days. Both are correct. A policy trained to predict "the" action has to cope with data that contains two right answers, and the simplest training loss fails in a systematic way. This lesson shows the failure on a toy task and then looks at the two ideas that today's most used imitation policies are built on: action chunking (ACT) and diffusion (Diffusion Policy).

## Two right answers

Behaviour cloning with a squared-error loss picks, for each observation, the action that minimises the average squared distance to the recorded actions. That is their **mean**. If 7 demos at a spot swerve left by 4.2 cm and 3 swerve right by 4.2 cm, the mean is (−7 + 3) / 10 × 4.2 = −1.7 cm: a gentle drift left that none of the demonstrators made, and one that may be exactly the wrong thing, straight into the obstacle the demonstrators were avoiding.

<Formula label="What a squared-error loss learns">π(o) = mean of the recorded actions at o, which is not one of them when the actions form two groups</Formula>

The fix is to learn a policy that represents the whole distribution of actions and can **sample** one of them, so it picks left or right rather than a blend. ACT does this with a conditional variational autoencoder over chunks of actions; Diffusion Policy does it with a denoising diffusion model over chunks of actions. Both also predict a **chunk** of future actions at once instead of one.

## The block task

The model is a point gripper starting near (0, 0) that must reach (0, 1). A 0.3 m square block sits in between, centred at (0, 0.5). The expert drives straight up to a fork at (0, 0.2), then heads for a waypoint at (−0.3, 0.4) or (+0.3, 0.4), chosen 50/50 per demonstration, and then for the goal. Each step moves at most 5 cm, plus 0.5 cm of actuator noise per axis.

All the policies look at the same thing: the 10 recorded frames nearest the current position. They differ only in what they do with them, which is exactly the difference between the training losses of real policies. `average` takes the mean of their actions (a squared-error network). `sample` replays one of them chosen at random. `chunk N` picks one at random and plays the next N actions of that demonstration before looking again. `diffusion` runs a real DDPM sampler; where a trained network would predict the clean action, it uses the exact answer for these 10 frames.

<CodeSource path="examples/month-6/act-diffusion/multimodal.py" />

```python
"""multimodal.py: why averaging fails on multimodal demonstrations, and what action chunks and diffusion change.

A point gripper starts near (0, 0) and must reach GOAL (0, 1). A square block of side 0.3 m sits in the way,
centred at (0, 0.5). The expert drives straight up to a fork at (0, 0.2) and then passes it on the left or on the right, 50/50, chosen at the start of each
demonstration: both are equally good, so the demonstrations are multimodal. Each step moves at most 0.05 m
plus 0.5 cm of actuator noise per axis. Success: within 0.05 m of GOAL inside 60 steps without touching the block.

Policies (all look at the K = 10 recorded frames nearest the current position):
  average    the mean of their actions; this is what a network trained with a squared-error loss predicts
  sample     the action of one of them, chosen at random, every step
  chunk N    pick one of them at random and play the next N actions of its episode, then look again
             (action chunking, the core idea of ACT; ACT also blends overlapping chunks)
  diffusion  start from Gaussian noise and denoise a chunk of 8 actions in 20 DDPM steps, using the exact
             denoiser for the demonstration set (the function a diffusion policy's network is trained to
             approximate), then play the chunk and look again
Standard library only, fixed seeds.
"""
import math
import random

GOAL, BLOCK_Y, HALF, FORK_Y = (0.0, 1.0), 0.5, 0.15, 0.2
STEP, NOISE, MAX_STEPS, K = 0.05, 0.005, 60, 10


def hits_block(p):
    return abs(p[0]) <= HALF and abs(p[1] - BLOCK_Y) <= HALF


def toward(p, target):
    dx, dy = target[0] - p[0], target[1] - p[1]
    d = math.hypot(dx, dy)
    k = min(1.0, STEP / d) if d > 0 else 0.0
    return dx * k, dy * k


def expert(p, side):
    """Straight up to the fork at (0, 0.2), then to a waypoint beside the block, then to the goal.
    side is -1 (left) or +1 (right); before the fork both kinds of demo look the same."""
    if p[1] < FORK_Y and abs(p[0]) < 0.05:
        return toward(p, (0.0, FORK_Y + 0.05))
    if p[1] < 0.4 and abs(p[0]) < 0.28:
        return toward(p, (0.3 * side, 0.4))
    return toward(p, GOAL)


def run(choose, rng, record=None):
    """choose(p, t) returns a list of actions to play before asking again."""
    p = (rng.uniform(-0.03, 0.03), rng.uniform(-0.03, 0.03))
    plan, t = [], 0
    while t < MAX_STEPS:
        if not plan:
            plan = list(choose(p, t))
        a = plan.pop(0)
        if record is not None:
            record.append((p, a))
        p = (p[0] + a[0] + rng.gauss(0, NOISE), p[1] + a[1] + rng.gauss(0, NOISE))
        t += 1
        if hits_block(p):
            return 'block', t
        if math.hypot(p[0] - GOAL[0], p[1] - GOAL[1]) < 0.05:
            return 'success', t
    return 'timeout', t


def record_demos(n, rng):
    episodes = []
    for _ in range(n):
        side = rng.choice((-1, 1))
        rows = []
        run(lambda p, t: [expert(p, side)], rng, record=rows)
        episodes.append(rows)
    return episodes


class Demos:
    def __init__(self, episodes):
        self.frames = [(e, i, rows[i][0]) for e, rows in enumerate(episodes) for i in range(len(rows))]
        self.episodes = episodes

    def nearest(self, p, k=K):
        return sorted(self.frames, key=lambda f: (f[2][0] - p[0]) ** 2 + (f[2][1] - p[1]) ** 2)[:k]

    def chunk(self, frame, n):
        """The next n actions of that frame's episode, padded with zero moves at the end."""
        e, i, _ = frame
        acts = [a for _, a in self.episodes[e][i:i + n]]
        return acts + [(0.0, 0.0)] * (n - len(acts))


def average_policy(demos):
    def choose(p, t):
        near = demos.nearest(p)
        return [(sum(demos.chunk(f, 1)[0][0] for f in near) / K, sum(demos.chunk(f, 1)[0][1] for f in near) / K)]
    return choose


def chunk_policy(demos, n, rng):
    def choose(p, t):
        return demos.chunk(rng.choice(demos.nearest(p)), n)
    return choose


def diffusion_policy(demos, n, rng, steps=20):
    """DDPM sampling of an n-step action chunk with the exact denoiser of the K nearest demo chunks."""
    betas = [1e-4 + (0.2 - 1e-4) * s / (steps - 1) for s in range(steps)]
    alphas = [1 - b for b in betas]
    abar, prod = [], 1.0
    for a in alphas:
        prod *= a
        abar.append(prod)
    scale = 1 / STEP                     # work in units of one full step so the noise schedule fits

    def choose(p, t):
        data = [[v * scale for a in demos.chunk(f, n) for v in a] for f in demos.nearest(p)]
        dim = len(data[0])
        x = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(dim)]
        for s in reversed(range(steps)):
            # exact denoiser: E[x0 | xs] for x0 drawn uniformly from data, xs = sqrt(abar) x0 + sqrt(1 - abar) noise
            logw = [-sum((xi - math.sqrt(abar[s]) * di) ** 2 for xi, di in zip(x, d)) / (2 * (1 - abar[s]))
                    for d in data]
            m = max(logw)
            w = [math.exp(v - m) for v in logw]
            x0 = [sum(wi * d[j] for wi, d in zip(w, data)) / sum(w) for j in range(dim)]
            if s == 0:
                x = x0
                break
            # DDPM posterior mean of x_{s-1} given xs and the predicted x0, plus its noise
            c0 = math.sqrt(abar[s - 1]) * betas[s] / (1 - abar[s])
            cs = math.sqrt(alphas[s]) * (1 - abar[s - 1]) / (1 - abar[s])
            sd = math.sqrt(betas[s] * (1 - abar[s - 1]) / (1 - abar[s]))
            x = [c0 * a0 + cs * xs + sd * rng.gauss(0, 1) for a0, xs in zip(x0, x)]
        return [(x[2 * j] / scale, x[2 * j + 1] / scale) for j in range(n)]
    return choose


def evaluate(label, make, n=100):
    rng = random.Random(2026)
    choose = make(random.Random(11))
    counts = {'success': 0, 'block': 0, 'timeout': 0}
    steps = []
    for _ in range(n):
        result, t = run(choose, rng)
        counts[result] += 1
        if result == 'success':
            steps.append(t)
    mean = f'{sum(steps) / len(steps):.1f}' if steps else '-'
    print(f'{label:<16} success {counts["success"]:>3}/100   hit block {counts["block"]:>3}   '
          f'timeout {counts["timeout"]:>3}   mean steps to goal {mean}')


def main():
    episodes = record_demos(40, random.Random(5))
    lefts = sum(1 for rows in episodes if min(p[0] for p, _ in rows) < -0.1)
    demos = Demos(episodes)
    print(f'40 expert demos: {lefts} pass on the left, {40 - lefts} on the right, {len(demos.frames)} frames')

    print('\nSideways part dx of the first action just past the fork, at (0, 0.23), 200 draws per policy')
    edges = [-0.05, -0.03, -0.01, 0.01, 0.03, 0.05]
    print('  dx bin (cm)  ' + ''.join(f'{100 * lo:+.0f}..{100 * hi:+.0f}'.rjust(9) for lo, hi in zip(edges, edges[1:])))
    rng = random.Random(3)
    near = [demos.chunk(f, 1)[0][0] for f in demos.nearest((0.0, 0.23))]
    rows = [('10 demo frames', near)]
    for label, choose in [('average', average_policy(demos)), ('sample', chunk_policy(demos, 1, rng)),
                          ('diffusion', diffusion_policy(demos, 1, rng))]:
        rows.append((label, [choose((0.0, 0.23), 0)[0][0] for _ in range(200)]))
    for label, dx in rows:
        bins = [sum(1 for v in dx if lo <= v < hi or (hi == edges[-1] and v == hi)) for lo, hi in zip(edges, edges[1:])]
        print(f'  {label:<14}' + ''.join(f'{c:>9}' for c in bins))

    print('\nRollouts: 100 each with 0.5 cm actuator noise, same seed for every policy')
    evaluate('average', lambda r: average_policy(demos))
    evaluate('sample', lambda r: chunk_policy(demos, 1, r))
    for n in (4, 8, 16):
        evaluate(f'chunk {n}', lambda r, n=n: chunk_policy(demos, n, r))
    evaluate('diffusion 1', lambda r: diffusion_policy(demos, 1, r))
    evaluate('diffusion 8', lambda r: diffusion_policy(demos, 8, r))


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

## Averaging versus sampling

Now the whole task, 100 runs per policy, each evaluation starting from the same seed:

<SimOutput source="model">

```text
Rollouts: 100 each with 0.5 cm actuator noise, same seed for every policy
average          success  67/100   hit block  33   timeout   0   mean steps to goal 25.6
sample           success  99/100   hit block   1   timeout   0   mean steps to goal 24.6
```

</SimOutput>

A third of the averaged runs drive into the block. The remaining runs succeed because noise or a lopsided set of neighbours pushes them far enough to one side that all 10 nearest frames come from the same side; from then on the average and the demonstrations agree. Sampling commits to a side at the fork and succeeds 99 times out of 100. Nothing about the data changed; only the way the policy turns 10 recorded actions into one.

This is the same failure you will see on a real arm as a gripper that stops at an intermediate pose halfway between two grasps, or a mobile robot that drives at the middle of a pillar it was taught to go around. If your demonstrations contain more than one way to do the task, the policy class has to be able to represent more than one answer.

## Action chunks: what ACT adds

ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers, from the ALOHA bimanual robot work) predicts the next k actions at once, k = 100 at 50 Hz in the paper, which is 2 seconds. Chunking helps in three ways. The policy makes k times fewer decisions, so errors have fewer chances to compound. A chunk is internally consistent: it cannot switch from "go left" to "go right" halfway through. And it copes with demonstrations in which the observation does not say what comes next, such as a person pausing before they act, because the chunk carries the timing. To keep motion smooth, ACT also queries the policy every step and blends all chunks that cover the current step with exponential weights (temporal ensembling).

Chunks have a cost, and the toy model shows it clearly. Between decisions the chunk plays **open loop**:

<SimOutput source="model">

```text
chunk 4          success  98/100   hit block   0   timeout   2   mean steps to goal 24.8
chunk 8          success  88/100   hit block  10   timeout   2   mean steps to goal 25.0
chunk 16         success  80/100   hit block  19   timeout   1   mean steps to goal 26.1
```

</SimOutput>

Longer chunks hit the block more. The return leg from the waypoint (±0.3, 0.4) to the goal passes the block's top corner with only 2.5 cm to spare, and a chunk copied from a demonstration that started a few centimetres away, plus noise that nothing corrects for 16 steps (0.5 cm × √16 = 2 cm), is enough to clip it. In this toy the position already tells the policy which side it is on, so the benefits of chunking do not show and only its cost does. On a real robot both are real, which is why the chunk length (ACT's `chunk_size`, how many steps to execute before asking again) is something you tune and measure, not a constant.

## Diffusion policy

A diffusion model generates a sample by starting from pure Gaussian noise and removing noise in many small steps. Training adds noise to a recorded action chunk, x_s = √ᾱ_s x_0 + √(1 − ᾱ_s) ε, and teaches a network to predict the noise ε (equivalently the clean chunk x_0) from x_s, the step s and the observation. Sampling runs that the other way: from noise, predict x_0, step to a slightly less noisy x, repeat. Because each sample starts from different noise, the result lands in one mode or another; it does not average them. Diffusion Policy (Chi et al.) predicts chunks of 16 actions this way and executes the first 8 before planning again.

<Formula label="Forward noising, step s">x_s = √ᾱ_s · x_0 + √(1 − ᾱ_s) · ε,   ε ~ N(0, I)</Formula>

The model here skips the network. For a fixed set of data points, the best possible prediction of x_0 from x_s has a closed form: a weighted average of the data points, each weighted by how likely it is to have produced x_s. That is the function the network is trained towards, so `diffusion_policy` runs a real 20-step DDPM sampler with the perfect denoiser for the 10 nearest frames:

<SimOutput source="model">

```text
diffusion 1      success  96/100   hit block   4   timeout   0   mean steps to goal 24.7
diffusion 8      success  93/100   hit block   7   timeout   0   mean steps to goal 24.9
```

</SimOutput>

It behaves like sampling (96 and 93 against 99 for `sample` and 88 for `chunk 8`) because, with the perfect denoiser and only 10 points, diffusion can only reproduce those points: it has memorised them. A trained network is not perfect, and that is useful: it smooths between demonstrations and produces chunks no single demonstration contained but which still lie in one mode. Its costs are inference time (several denoising passes through a network for each chunk) and a lot of training compute, which is why diffusion and ACT training runs take hours on a GPU while the policies in this lesson take two seconds on a laptop.

## Common mistakes

- **Averaging over modes without noticing.** If a policy moves gradually towards the middle of two options, or stalls between them, check whether your demonstrations contain two ways of doing the task. Either demonstrate one way consistently or use a policy that can sample.
- **Thinking chunking is free.** A chunk runs open loop until the next decision. Tight clearances, a moving object or a slipping grasp need the policy to look again sooner; tune how many actions to execute per decision and measure it.
- **Comparing policies on different data or seeds.** Every line above uses the same 40 demonstrations, the same seed and the same number of runs. Lesson 4 goes one step further and gives every run its own seed, so that each policy meets exactly the same starting conditions; on hardware, that means the same list of start positions for every policy.
- **Calling a memorising policy a good one.** The diffusion policy here can only replay demonstrations. Test on start positions that are not in the dataset before claiming a policy generalises.
- **Mixing up training and inference cost.** A diffusion policy needs several network passes per chunk, so check that inference keeps up with your control rate on the computer that will run it, not only on the training GPU.
