The policies in the last two lessons were trained from scratch for one task on one robot. A vision-language-action model (VLA) starts instead from a vision-language model, a network already trained on huge amounts of images and text, and is trained further on robot demonstrations from many robots, so that it maps a camera image plus an instruction such as "put the red cube in the bin" to robot actions. You rarely train one yourself; you download one and **fine-tune** it on a few dozen demonstrations of your task. This lesson covers how actions get in and out of such a model, which models you can actually download, and under what licence.

## What a VLA is

Three parts. A **vision encoder** turns each camera image into a sequence of feature vectors. A **language model** reads those together with the tokenised instruction (and, in most models, the robot's joint state, fed into the language model or the action head). An **action output** turns the language model's result into numbers for the motors. The first two come from a pretrained vision-language model (in every model below except Octo). OpenVLA reports that, after that initialization, most of the adaptation to robot actions happens in the action tokeniser / output path and in fine-tuning on robot trajectories rather than by training the vision-language backbone from scratch (Kim et al., arXiv:2406.09246; similarly RT-2, Brohan et al., arXiv:2307.15818). Treat "mostly in the third part and in the fine-tuning" as that attributed claim, not as a result measured in this lesson.

There are two ways to build the action output, and they decide much of a model's behaviour:

- **Actions as tokens.** Extend the language model's vocabulary with action tokens and let it generate action tokens autoregressively, one token after another. RT-2 and OpenVLA work like this, one action step per call (8 and 7 tokens); π0-FAST does too, but its tokeniser, FAST, compresses a whole chunk first (see below).
- **A separate action head.** Feed the language model's features into a smaller network that produces a whole chunk of continuous actions, by diffusion or flow matching (a close relative of the diffusion you met in the [previous lesson](/en/month/month-6/act-diffusion)). π0, SmolVLA and GR00T work like this. π0.5 is pretrained on FAST tokens only, gets a flow-matching action expert in post-training and uses that expert at run time. Octo also has a diffusion head but no vision-language model: it is a small transformer trained from scratch, with a frozen T5 text encoder.

Where the figure's numbers and arrows come from. RT-2 writes each action step as 8 integers ([arXiv:2307.15818](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15818), §3.2) and OpenVLA as 7 ([arXiv:2406.09246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09246), §3.3). The FAST paper counts 20 to 29 tokens per 1-second chunk for single 7-D arms (BridgeV2, DROID, table bussing) and 53 for a two-arm, 14-D T-shirt-folding task, so about 30 or fewer per arm ([arXiv:2501.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09747), Table I). The joint state goes to different places. SmolVLA projects it into one token that joins the image and text tokens in the language model ([arXiv:2506.01844](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01844), §3.1). π0 gives it to the action expert's weights together with the noisy actions ([arXiv:2410.24164](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24164), §IV). GR00T N1 encodes it and passes it with the language model's features to its diffusion transformer ([arXiv:2503.14734](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14734), §2.1). Octo's authors report that policies trained with proprioceptive observations "seemed generally worse" ([arXiv:2405.12213](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12213), Appendix E), and the released Octo checkpoints take only camera images and a task, given as a language instruction, a goal image or both ([octo-base-1.5 model card](https://huggingface.co/rail-berkeley/octo-base-1.5)).

The first approach reuses the language model unchanged but has to decide how to write actions as tokens. That is the part you can run and inspect without a GPU.

## Actions as tokens: 256 bins

RT-2 and OpenVLA give each action dimension 256 tokens: the range of each dimension is cut into 256 equal bins, and a value becomes the index of its bin. The count 256 is a vocabulary budget, not a physics constant: OpenVLA overlays the continuous-action bins onto the 256 least-used tokens in the Llama tokenizer vocabulary so the pretrained language model keeps its text tokens (§3.2 of Kim et al., arXiv:2406.09246). RT-2 uses the whole min/max range of the data. OpenVLA instead sets the range with the dataset's 1st and 99th percentiles and clips values beyond them to the end bins. Percentiles spend the 256 bins on the mass of the data; min/max spends many of them on rare extremes and makes every ordinary step coarser. This lesson follows OpenVLA.

<Formula label="Bin token for one action value">token = floor(256 × (clip(a, p1, p99) − p1) / (p99 − p1)),   from 0 to 255</Formula>

```python
"""action_tokens.py: how a VLA turns robot actions into tokens, per-step bins versus a DCT of the whole chunk.

A language model outputs tokens, so an autoregressive VLA needs a way to write actions as tokens. Two schemes:
  bins  (OpenVLA): each action dimension at each step becomes one of 256 tokens, bins spread evenly
        between that dimension's 1st and 99th percentile over the dataset (RT-2 spreads them over the full range).
  DCT   (the idea behind FAST, used by pi0-FAST): normalise each dimension to -1..1 with the same percentiles,
        take a discrete cosine transform of each dimension over the chunk, multiply by a scale and round.
        Smooth motion puts almost everything into a few low-frequency coefficients; the rest round to zero.
        (FAST then compresses the integers with byte-pair encoding; this program counts nonzero coefficients.)
The actions are synthetic but shaped like a teleoperated SO-101: 6 joint targets in degrees at 30 Hz, chunks of
1 s (30 steps). Standard library only, fixed seeds.
"""
import math
import random

HZ, CHUNK, JOINTS, BINS = 30, 30, 6, 256


def episode(rng, seconds=10):
    """Smooth joint targets: a few slow sine components per joint, like a person moving a leader arm."""
    comps = [[(rng.uniform(5, 40), rng.uniform(0.1, 0.8), rng.uniform(0, 2 * math.pi)) for _ in range(3)]
             for _ in range(JOINTS)]
    offset = [rng.uniform(-30, 30) for _ in range(JOINTS)]
    return [[offset[j] + sum(a * math.sin(2 * math.pi * f * t / HZ + ph) for a, f, ph in comps[j])
             for j in range(JOINTS)] for t in range(seconds * HZ)]


def percentile(values, q):
    v = sorted(values)
    k = (len(v) - 1) * q
    lo = int(k)
    return v[lo] + (v[min(lo + 1, len(v) - 1)] - v[lo]) * (k - lo)


def to_bins(x, lo, hi):
    u = (min(max(x, lo), hi) - lo) / (hi - lo)
    return min(int(u * BINS), BINS - 1)


def from_bins(token, lo, hi):
    return lo + (token + 0.5) * (hi - lo) / BINS


def dct(x):
    """DCT-II with orthonormal scaling."""
    n = len(x)
    return [math.sqrt((1 if k == 0 else 2) / n) * sum(x[i] * math.cos(math.pi * k * (2 * i + 1) / (2 * n))
                                                      for i in range(n)) for k in range(n)]


def idct(c):
    n = len(c)
    return [sum(math.sqrt((1 if k == 0 else 2) / n) * c[k] * math.cos(math.pi * k * (2 * i + 1) / (2 * n))
                for k in range(n)) for i in range(n)]


def errors(chunk, rebuilt):
    diffs = [abs(a - b) for row, rrow in zip(chunk, rebuilt) for a, b in zip(row, rrow)]
    return math.sqrt(sum(d * d for d in diffs) / len(diffs)), max(diffs)


def main():
    rng = random.Random(6)
    data = [episode(rng) for _ in range(50)]
    lo = [percentile([s[j] for ep in data for s in ep], 0.01) for j in range(JOINTS)]
    hi = [percentile([s[j] for ep in data for s in ep], 0.99) for j in range(JOINTS)]
    print(f'Dataset: 50 episodes of 10 s, {JOINTS} joints at {HZ} Hz; 1st..99th percentile per joint (deg):')
    print('  ' + '  '.join(f'j{j} {lo[j]:+.0f}..{hi[j]:+.0f}' for j in range(JOINTS)))
    print(f'  bin width = range / {BINS}: ' + ', '.join(f'{(hi[j] - lo[j]) / BINS:.2f}' for j in range(JOINTS)) + ' deg')

    chunk = episode(random.Random(99))[:CHUNK]          # a new episode, not in the dataset
    print(f'\nOne {CHUNK}-step chunk (1 s) from a new episode: {CHUNK} x {JOINTS} = {CHUNK * JOINTS} numbers')

    tokens = [[to_bins(chunk[t][j], lo[j], hi[j]) for j in range(JOINTS)] for t in range(CHUNK)]
    rebuilt = [[from_bins(tokens[t][j], lo[j], hi[j]) for j in range(JOINTS)] for t in range(CHUNK)]
    rms, worst = errors(chunk, rebuilt)
    clipped = sum(1 for t in range(CHUNK) for j in range(JOINTS) if not lo[j] <= chunk[t][j] <= hi[j])
    print(f'bins: {CHUNK * JOINTS} tokens, error rms {rms:.3f} deg, max {worst:.3f} deg '
          f'({clipped} values outside the 1st..99th percentile, clipped)')
    print('  joint 0 tokens, first 12 steps: ' + ' '.join(str(tokens[t][0]) for t in range(12)))
    same = sum(1 for t in range(1, CHUNK) for j in range(JOINTS) if tokens[t][j] == tokens[t - 1][j])
    near = sum(1 for t in range(1, CHUNK) for j in range(JOINTS) if abs(tokens[t][j] - tokens[t - 1][j]) <= 2)
    total = (CHUNK - 1) * JOINTS
    print(f'  token equal to the previous step: {same}/{total}; within 2 of it: {near}/{total}')

    print('\nDCT of each joint over the chunk, normalised to -1..1, then scale and round:')
    norm = [[2 * (chunk[t][j] - lo[j]) / (hi[j] - lo[j]) - 1 for j in range(JOINTS)] for t in range(CHUNK)]
    coeffs = [dct([norm[t][j] for t in range(CHUNK)]) for j in range(JOINTS)]
    for scale in (5, 10, 30, 100):
        q = [[round(c * scale) for c in coeffs[j]] for j in range(JOINTS)]
        nonzero = sum(1 for j in range(JOINTS) for v in q[j] if v != 0)
        back = [idct([v / scale for v in q[j]]) for j in range(JOINTS)]
        rebuilt = [[(back[j][t] + 1) / 2 * (hi[j] - lo[j]) + lo[j] for j in range(JOINTS)] for t in range(CHUNK)]
        rms, worst = errors(chunk, rebuilt)
        print(f'  scale {scale:>3}: {nonzero:>3} nonzero coefficients of {CHUNK * JOINTS}, '
              f'error rms {rms:.3f} deg, max {worst:.3f} deg')
    q = [round(c * 10) for c in coeffs[0]]
    print('  joint 0 at scale 10: ' + ' '.join(str(v) for v in q))


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

Each bin is about 0.6 degrees wide, so a value inside the range is off by at most half a bin, 0.3 degrees. A model trained to predict the next token can score well by copying the previous one, which teaches it very little. The faster you sample, the worse this gets.

## Compressing a chunk with the DCT

FAST, the tokeniser behind π0-FAST (Pertsch et al., arXiv:2501.09747), tokenises the whole chunk at once. It normalises each dimension to −1..1 with the same percentiles, takes a discrete cosine transform (DCT) of each dimension over the chunk, multiplies by a scale and rounds to integers. Why low frequencies dominate: a teleoperated joint trajectory varies slowly from step to step, so it is well approximated by a few slow cosines; the DCT is an orthogonal change of basis onto those cosines, and energy compaction puts most of the ℓ₂ mass in the first coefficients. Rounding to integers then zeros any coefficient whose absolute value is below 1/(2 · scale) — raising the scale keeps more small coefficients and lowers reconstruction error, at the cost of a longer nonzero sequence (see the table). FAST then compresses the integer sequence with byte-pair encoding. The program counts the nonzero coefficients as a stand-in for the final tokens:

| π0, π0-FAST, π0.5 | Physical Intelligence (openpi) | flow-matching action expert; π0-FAST uses FAST tokens; π0.5 is pretrained on FAST tokens only and adds the action expert in post-training | code Apache-2.0; base checkpoints downloadable; built on PaliGemma, LeRobot's copies carry the Gemma licence |

Licences change with new releases; check the model card on the day you download. The model that fits a beginner's SO-101 best is SmolVLA: it is small enough to fine-tune on one consumer GPU, it was pretrained on open community datasets shared with LeRobot and evaluated on SO-100 and SO-101 arms, and LeRobot fine-tunes it with the same dataset format as ACT. ## What a VLA does not do for you

A VLA does not remove the work of the previous lessons; it relocates it. You still need a good dataset of your robot, with the variation and recoveries you want it to handle. You still need to evaluate it on a fixed set of conditions. And "zero-shot" claims only hold for robots and scenes like those in the pretraining data: OpenVLA's own model card says it does not generalise to unseen robot embodiments without fine-tuning. What you gain is language conditioning (one model, many instructions) and a better starting point, so fewer demonstrations per task.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "open source" as "open weights" as "free to use commercially".** Read the licence of the weights, not only of the code. GR00T N1.6's code is Apache-2.0 while its weights are research-only.
- **Forgetting the normalisation statistics.** A VLA outputs normalised actions. Converting them back needs the 1st/99th percentiles (or mean and standard deviation) of the dataset it was fine-tuned on; the wrong statistics give the right shape at the wrong size.
- **Expecting zero-shot on your arm.** Without fine-tuning on your robot, a VLA's joint or end-effector conventions, camera position and gripper may all differ from what it learned. Plan for about 50 demonstrations.
- **Ignoring inference time.** A 7B model needs a large GPU to run fast enough. Measure actions per second on the computer that will drive the robot, and pick a smaller model or chunked execution if it cannot keep up.
- **Picking bins without looking at the range.** If your task includes motions bigger than anything in the dataset, bin tokenisation clips them, as the 6.2-degree error above shows.
